CUM DECENTISSIME VENERATIONE ET summa honorificentia la Eutrapela Polia et io affectuosamente dinanti al mysterioso fonte dilla divina genitrice congenulati, io da una imperceptibile dulcedine diffusamente invadere vexabonda me sentiva, che in quel puncto che fare non sapeva. Imperò che per lo amoenissimo et incredibilmente delitioso sito, et ultra omni credito dilectoso di virentia et vernale decoramento. Per lo immortale Iove rectamente ignorava ad quale sensitivo potere, lo intento obiecto mio da tanto diverso dilecto distracto, et excessivo et foelice solatio et voluptico placimine firma, et stabilmente applicare, et nescio me accusava. Le quale tutte belle, et dulcissime cose tanto più gratioso et desiderabile allectamento ad me allhora plenamente causando offerivano, quanto che io conosceva la Uranothia Polia participe placidamente delectarsene. In questo loco, at etiam di questo ammirando fonte la novitate et excellentia mirando.

Notes

Poliphilo describes the marvellous artifice of the Venerean fountain standing at the centre of the theatre's floor, and how the little curtain was torn away; and he saw the divine Mother in her majesty, and how she imposed silence upon the singing nymphs, of whom she assigned three apiece to Polia and to him. Afterwards Cupid wounded them both, and the goddess sprinkled them with the water of the fountain, and Poliphilo was clothed again. Last of all, Mars coming, they obtained leave and departed.

With the most fitting reverence and the highest honour, the graceful Polia and I knelt devotedly before the mysterious fountain of the divine mother, and I felt an imperceptible sweetness spreading through me and troubling me, so that in that moment I did not know what to do. For what with the delightful place, incredibly pleasing and beyond all belief lovely in its greenness and spring adornment; and the little birds coursing through the purest air and chattering as they flew among the new leaves, most joyfully, to the outward senses; and the hearing of those most beautiful nymphs singing their melodies together with their unfamiliar instruments; and the sight of their divine gestures and most modest movements, which drove me ardently toward the extremity of pleasure; and the careful, curious weighing of a fabric of such dignity of conception and such elegant arrangement; and the greedy drinking-in of a fragrance I had never known — by immortal Jove, I truly did not know to which of my senses I should firmly and steadily apply the object of my attention, distracted as it was by so many different delights, by so excessive and happy a solace and voluptuous pleasing; and I accused myself of not knowing.

And all these beautiful and most sweet things offered me an enticement so much the more gracious and desirable, in that I knew that Polia — heavenly Polia — was placidly taking her share of the delight in them.

In this place, then, and marvelling too at the novelty and excellence of this astonishing fountain.

Of that blackest stone which alone, and entire, made the whole paving — that is, the floor of the area — there rose at its centre a little wall a foot high, brought to an excellent polish and furnished with every fitting ornament, heptagonal on the outside and round within. It had its running cymatium and socle, its small plinths and its wave-mouldings, wrought and set in due order beneath the bases; and over the middle point of each angle there was built up a column swelling in the shaft — that is, bellied — seven of them in all, turned with the utmost exquisiteness. Two of these stood answering one another, equally, opposite the entrance, in full view of where we knelt face down.

One of these lathe-turned columns, on the right hand, shone blue with the finest sapphire; and on the left one glowed spring-green with an emerald of a colour so surpassing, and more brilliant, than the stones set for eyes in the lion on the tomb of Hermias the prince. Nor was such a one given by Ptolemy to Lucullus; nor was that of so great price which the King of Babylon presented to the King of Egypt; nor were those of the obelisk in the Temple of Jove of such estimation; nor was the statue in the temple of Hercules at Tyre so great a marvel, as this one showed itself to be.

Next to this followed a column of turquoise stone, most beautifully coloured a lovely cerulean, endowed graciously with its virtue; and though it was an opaque stone, it was nonetheless most brilliant, and shone back like a mirror.

Touching the sapphire column there stood another precious one, opaque also, of a most delightful colour like melilot, and with a lustre like the translucent flower of the buttercup. Adjoining this was one of jasper of a glassy colour, and the other of topaz flashing with a golden colour. The seventh, alone and singularly, was hexagonal, of the most limpid Indian beryl with an oily sheen, giving back whatever stood before it reversed.

And this one answered to the midpoint between the first two — because every figure of an odd number of angles has one angle facing the middle of the interval between two others. The three shining columns of the right-hand order had likewise, each of them, an infant boy caught in a kind of receptacle; and so too on the shaft of each of the precious columns of the left there hung, set into it, the female sex. And this, by a mystical artifice, was expressed naturally at the middle of the columns, with such a scintillation of polish as neither the biting of whetstone nor emery with licking Tripoli chalk can give.

The bases, the capitals, the Phrygian architrave and the cornice stood out in the purest gold. The arches, and all the solid between one column and the next, were of the wrought stone of one of the columns of the surrounding order — that is, of sapphire toward the emerald, and of emerald toward the turquoise — and so on in sequence, the whole arcade was marvellously constructed.

At the angles of the crown, above the true central perpendicular of each column beneath, there stood a small altar, and rising from it projected the image of a planet with its proper attribute, its size exactly a third of the column below it, symmetrically, and of the purest gold. On the front face, at the right, sat scythe-bearing Saturn, and at the left the night-shining Cynthia; and beginning from the first and going round in order they ended at Selene. Beneath these, in the frieze running round, chased with the most exquisite artifice, were to be seen the twelve signs of the zodiac, with their governing impressions and characters, expressed in outstanding carving.

The crown then of this most marvellous fountain, and its roofing, blazed with an extraordinary cupola of the finest crystal, veinless, utterly pure and transparent. Xenocrates saw none such; none like it was found in Cyprus, nor produced in Asia, nor in Germany — without rust or roughness, without a clouded speck, no core of salt, nor any hairline flaw to be seen. Nor did Nero shatter such a one. But pure, surpassing, and unalloyed, it was girdled with a raised work, in a fitting and even binding of foliage issuing from certain little monsters, with small boys wonderfully interwoven playing among them; and it was of excellent bulk and convexity.

At the tapering of its topmost peak was fixed a miraculous show: in a golden and most cunningly wrought [?setting], a carbuncle flashing lightning on every side, egg-shaped, and of the thickness of an ostrich's.

Poliphilo here ends his Hypnerotomachia, grieving that the sleep was no longer, and that the Sun was envious, and made it day.

So unlooked-for a delight snatched away, that angelic spirit taken from my eyes, and the sweet and gentle sleeping drawn out of my drowsy limbs as I woke — in that instant, alas, ah me, amorous readers, I ached all over from the hard clasping of that blessed image, that happy presence, that venerable majesty, which left me and deserted me between wondrous sweetness and piercing bitterness. When from my gaze there departed that most joyful sleep and that divine shade, the mysterious apparition broken off, scattered, and carried away — by which I had been led and lifted to thoughts so high, so sublime, so piercing.

And then, as it happened, the Sun, stirred to envy by so blessed a dream, coming to plunder the glorious night like a public enemy and an informer against the divine Mother, came suddenly with his shining splendours and painted the whitening Dawn a rosy colour, the long night dispelled. And the new-made day being lit and set between us, I was left crammed and utterly filled with a sweet and subtle deception.

Consider, then, what livid spite he would have had, if I had truly felt, in the enjoying of them, the very voluptuous delights of so shapely and divine a damsel, so matchless a nymph — he who would not endure (because, I judge, with a goddess it is not permitted) to grant me the long night of Alcmena. Ah me, why did he not exchange a little of his swiftness for a little sloth, for the sake of my restored rest, and overrun his appointed hour by just so much? And why was the Stygian sleep out of curious Psyche's box not laid upon me then?

And there Philomela, weeping before daybreak, hidden among thorny brambles and in thickets close with the dark foliage of small oaks wound about with twining honeysuckle, was pouring out the violence of that adulterous and faithless Tereus, saying with tuneful chatter: Tereus, Tereus — [?have I been wronged]. Sighing, risen and released from the sweet sleep, I woke of a sudden into the small hours, saying: Farewell then, Polia.

At Treviso, where wretched Poliphilo was held fast in the most comely little thongs of Polia's love. 1467, on the Kalends of May.
