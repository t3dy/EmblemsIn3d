Nymphs, and How to Draw Them

A working gallery of nymphs in Western art — assembled to give faces, drapery, and gesture to the figures who people the Dream Garden of Poliphilo: the five sense-nymphs at the bath, the naiads of the Fountain of Venus, Polia with her torch, and her guides Logistica and Thelemia. Each entry is sourced and set in context, so a choice of model is also a choice of meaning.

Every image below is in the public domain and served from Wikimedia Commons; each caption cites the holding institution and the Commons file. This page is a private reference for asset development, not a publication — think of it as a mood-board with footnotes.

Section I

The Source — the woodcuts of 1499

Before borrowing a nymph from any painter, look at the ones the book already owns. The anonymous Venetian who cut the illustrations for the Aldine Hypnerotomachia Poliphili (Venice, 1499) gave Poliphilo's nymphs their first faces — outlines, not flesh; a clarity of contour that the whole 3-D "woodcut" render-style tries to keep. These are the ground truth: whatever later art we borrow, it is being poured back into this line.

Section II

Springs & Fountains — the naiads

Water runs through the whole Dream Garden: the spring where Poliphilo kneels in the wood, the nymphs' bath, and the great Fountain of Venus with its jets and basins. The nearest cousins in art are the reclining nymph of the spring — a Renaissance invention that fuses classical naiad with the "do not disturb my sleep" epigram — and the carved fountain-nymph, whose wet drapery and poured urn are exactly the vocabulary the fountain court needs.

Section III

The Bathing Company — the five senses

Five nymphs find Poliphilo trembling at a fountain's lip and laugh his fear away; they bear the names of the senses in Greek — Aphea (Touch), Osfressia (Smell), Orassia (Sight), Achoe (Hearing), Geussia (Taste) — and lead him, teasing, to their Queen. What the great nymph-companies of art teach here is grouping: how five or more bodies arc, overlap, and pass a gesture from one to the next around water.

Section IV

Diana & Her Train — the vow before love

In the book's second half Polia tells her own story: a plague-vow that bound her to chaste Diana, and the cold years before she relented and took the fainting Poliphilo in her arms. The huntress and her nymphs — bathing, surprised, punishing — are the register for Polia-before-love: the nymph who is beautiful and unavailable, and for the moral heat of being seen.

Section V

The Beloved & the Idea — Polia, Logistica, Thelemia

Some nymphs in the Dream Garden are also arguments. Polia is the long-sought beloved (white worked with gold, a lit torch in her hand); Logistica is Reason and Thelemia is Desire, the two guides who quarrel at the Three Doors. For a maiden who is also an idea, the model is the Botticelli nymph — Flora, Chloris, the welcoming Hora — where a woman and an allegory are made to occupy one body without strain.
