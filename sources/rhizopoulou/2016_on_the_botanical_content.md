## Abstract

This study focuses on botanical features gleaned from the literary source Hypnerotomachia Poliphili (1499); the analysis reveals that Hypnerotomachia Poliphili includes 285 botanical entities associated with 672 textual passages, which have not hitherto been reported. Roses, myrtle, pines, cypresses, laurel, acanthus, oaks, junipers, ivy, thyme, violets, sandalwoods and orange trees are the most frequently cited plants. Shrubs dominate with 43%, while herbs and trees follow with 28% and 26%, respectively. Arbutus, dittany, cypress, fig tree, labdanum, laurel, myrtle, oaks, oleander, wild and cultivated olive trees, oregano, pine, thyme, grapevines are associated with the diversity of Mediterranean ecosystems. In addition, numerous textual passages of Hypnerotomachia Poliphili mentioning flowering and fruiting plants reveal the seasonality of the Mediterranean region. Exotic plants, such as frankincense tree, myrrh, banana, pepper, sandalwood and jasmines are also included. Thorny plants, sharp thistles and cedars are cited in the text as occurring among ancient monuments and historical ruins. The botanical content of Hypnerotomachia Poliphili constitutes an alternate source of botanical observation, which includes historical, allegoric and symbolic information.

**Keywords:** Botany; environment; Hypnerotomachia Poliphili; living archives; Mediterranean; plants; plant history

## Introduction

Hypnerotomachia Poliphili, which has been translated as "The strife of love in a dream" (in French Songe de Poliphile), is the short title of an influential book of the Renaissance (Blunt 1937; Szépe 1996; Péricard-Méa 1998). (The original long title of this book in Latin has been translated into English as "Hypnerotomachia Poliphili in which it is shown that all human things are but a dream and many other things worthy of knowledge and memory".) The etymology of the invented term Hypnerotomachia is derived from three Greek words, i.e. hypnos (ὓπνος): sleep, eros (ἔρως): love, and mache (μάχη): strife. The term Poliphili (plural form of Poliphilos) is also of Greek origin, meaning beloved. Hypnerotomachia Poliphili is a bizarre romance that narrates the love-quest of a man (Poliphilos), which takes place in the dream realm. Poliphilos falls asleep and dreams of traversing pleasant landscapes, old groves and gardens containing ancient ruins and allegorical figures, in order to be reunited with his beloved (Polia), who, as the epitaph at the end of the book explains, has died (Stewering and Maher 1998). The narration is enriched by detailed descriptions of natural and human-induced landscapes, seductive gardens, pergolas overarched with roses and vines, and sacred groves often associated with antiquity.

The woodcut-illustrated first edition of Hypnerotomachia Poliphili was published by Aldus Manutius Press in Venice (1499) (Ivins 1923; Praz 1947; Pozzi 1981; Priki 2012; Farrington 2015; Pagliarini 2015). In its original form the book was anonymous, and many people have been credited with its authorship, over the centuries (Blunt 1937; Monga 1989; Leatherbarrow 2015). Nevertheless, authorship has been most commonly attributed to the Venetian monk Francesco Colonna (1433‒1527), who lived in the convent of the Dominicans at Treviso, on the basis of an acrostic formed by the initial elaborately decorated capital letters of the first 36 chapters (Weiss 1961; Russell 2013). However, the attribution has been questioned by scholars, who proposed the humanist and polymath Leon Battista Alberti (1404–1472) as the author of Hypnerotomachia Poliphili (Lefaivre 1997; Godwin 2000; March 2015).

Hypnerotomachia Poliphili (hereafter HP) has attracted much interest from art and architecture scholars, as well as garden and print historians (Amert 2008; Priki 2009; Russell 2014). Also, HP has influenced a wide range of disciplines, including garden design (Mac Dougall 1994; Hunt 1998; Polizzi 1998; Segre 1998), art (Oettinger 2011), literature (Eco 2005; Priki 2015), architecture and landscape architecture (Weddle 1994; Stewering 2000). However, very little research has been carried out in relation to the botanical content displayed in HP (Masson 1961; Colonna 2010), which is an essential element of the text because it appears in numerous passages.

Literary sources concerning plants and natural resources extracted from plants capture customs, beliefs and traditions of the culture and the time in which they were written (Ryan 2011; Taiz and Taiz 2011; Rhizopoulou 2014). The rediscovered knowledge preserved in old textual sources, which contain neglected botanical information that has not yet been fully exploited, may contribute to a better understanding of historical relationships and interactions between plants, nature, culture and human activities. This study was conducted in an effort to explore the botanical content of HP, which according to the best of our knowledge has not hitherto been reported.

## Material and methods

Hypnerotomachia Poliphili was written in an invented language combining ancient Latin, Italian and Greek, and is replete with Greek terms and words based on a mix of Latin and Greek, which are notoriously difficult to read (Trippe 2002; Campanelli, 2014; White 2015). Hence, the text is in a language that has been very difficult for both contemporary and current readers (Kirkham 2015; Burioni 2016). The present study of the botanical content cited in HP is based on the first complete English translation by J. Godwin (Colonna 2005), published by Thames and Hudson (Chishty-Mujahid 2009). Information on plants and plant products retrieved from HP was sorted by genus, taxon and species, and nomenclature follows ThePlantList. Unravelling the contents of old books and the compilation and documentation of lists matching vernacular names with binomial scientific names is difficult in the absence of detailed information and illustrations. The endeavour to match vernacular plant names quoted in HP (i.e. English names, Latin names, laymen colloquial names of Antiquity and transliterated Greek names) with scientific binomials was based on literary sources, dictionaries and archival research (Johns 1902; Stannard 1964; Wellisch 1978, and references therein; Elliott 1992; Cristofolini and Mossetti 1998; Radcliffe-Smith 1998; Collins 2000; Egerton 2003; Beck 2005; Gledhill 2008; Sillasoo 2006). It has been considered that few vernacular names are synonyms and correspond to the same generic name; for example, both vermilion [o5, z6] and wormwood [p2′, t3, t7, u4′, u6′] refer to Artemisia species (Arber 1953; Radcliffe-Smith 1998). Also, some plants with unidentified names were detected, i.e. cedovaria [e1], cervicello [a7], cynacanth [m3′], geusia (aquatic plant) [e8], pulliphora [y1], terrambula [u5] and vitilago [s7′]. There is a quotation for corn [n4] (Colonna 2005); but as there is no evidence for the presence of corn (Zea mays L.) in Europe in the fifteenth century, the meaning to be assigned to corn according to the Concise Oxford Dictionary of English Etymology is grain, or seed of a cereal crop (Hoad 1991). Generic names of plants followed by ‛spp.' refer to several species within a genus and by ‛sp.' to a taxon in a genus of uncertain binomial name and specific identity. Textual passages of HP, relevant to plants and plant products, are given below in square brackets.

## Results

A total of 672 textual passages related to 285 botanical entities are present in HP (Table 1). The most frequently quoted plants (Table 1) are laurel, myrtle, junipers, oaks, acanthus, violets, thyme, ivy, pines, cypress, sandalwoods, orange trees and flowering roses; in some cases, roses were coupled with geographical mentions, such as Cairo [g3], Persia [m6′] and Tyre [x2′]. Arboreal and bush plants and stands of evergreens with dense foliage are quoted in HP [a3, d6′]; shrubs dominate with 43%, whereas herbs and trees with 28% and 26%, respectively. A large proportion of plants (approximately 50%) are cited in HP with the lowest frequency (Table 1); this category also includes some typical current Mediterranean plants, e.g. carob tree, oleander, rosemary, dittany and fig tree. Navelwort and centaury as well as thorny vegetation comprised of prunus, sharp thistles and other spiny plants sprouted over and among historical ruins. Pellitory and hammerwort were growing in dry cracks of tombs, while rushes, reeds and sedges were found in wet places and on marshy ground [t8′]. The salt-loving, littoral cock's crest, oxeye, saltwort and wormwood were reportedly growing in fissures of sea-dashed breakwaters. Purslane and spurge were growing on sandbanks [p2′]. Numerous water plants were arranged among the aquatic angiosperms water lotus and white water lily [h7].

### Flowering plants

Plants with ornamental flowers, frequently presented in HP, were distributed by colour [e1′, e2]: i.e. for yellow, sweet clover (Melilotus sp.) and crowfoot (Ranunculus sp.), for blue, centaury and eyebright (Euphrasia sp.), for azure, chicory and periwinkle, for white, myrtle and lily-of-the-valley, and for purple, cyclamen, dittany and loosestrife. Furthermore, varicoloured flowering plant taxa quoted in the text, such as violets with sky-blue, yellow and white flowers, gladioli with blue and purple flowers, jasmines with red, yellow and white flowers, and roses with scarlet and white petals, were associated with certain, symbolic virtues.

### Fruit-bearing plants

The description of trees and shrubs laden with edible fruits and nuts was highlighted in HP (Table 1). This category includes characteristic elements of Mediterranean ecosystems, such as olive trees, strawberry trees, grapevines and pistachios. Indigenous and naturalized plants with edible fruits and nuts include almond, apple, apricot, chestnut, date palm, pear, pine, Damascene plum, pomegranate, quince and walnut. Introduced species include banana and citrus trees (i.e. citron, lemon, lime and orange).

### Medicinal plants

Three botanical items with medicinal use were found in HP: a) the sow-bread that "hinters child birth" [d6], b) garlic that "heals human eyes" [n7] and c) the umbelliferous panacea [a7], i.e. a remedy held in high repute among the ancients for its medicinal virtues, most probably representing a species of Apiaceae (Umbelliferae).

### Symbolic plants

Hypnerotomachia Poliphili contains symbolic associations linked to plants. Cane and reed were symbols of emptiness [A6′]. Oleander, aloe and cypress were symbols of bitterness [p3, C8, E4]. Oaks were considered to be symbols of hardness and strength [s8], while osiers and firs stood for vulnerability and flexibility [m3′]. Junipers symbolized eternity [m3′], laurel immortality [f7′, l6′, z6], and date palm victory [m3′]. Flowering jasmine is referred to as the symbol of divine love and happiness [g2′, i3, s7′, y1], and roses are identified as the symbol of affection, beauty and love [g7, t2].

### Botanical elements of woodcuts

It has been estimated that out of the 172 greatly influential, wood-engraved illustrations of HP with aesthetic, historic and symbolic associations, 53 illustrate botanical elements, as features of various landscape and garden sceneries. In this set of woodcuts, plant sketches portray ferns, trees (i.e., palms, oaks, cypresses, box trees) and leaves of laurel, acanthus, grapevine and English ivy.

## Discussion

The narration in HP constitutes a textual source of information featuring plants, at a time when comparable sources were very scarce (Sillasoo 2009). In the first years of printing (1450–1600), texts enriched with herbal content were popular; however, they were not intended as general handbooks for the public, but rather as reference books for those with knowledge, while they became essential for physicians and apothecaries (Buenz et al. 2004).

The frequency of references to plants in HP (Table 1) might reflect the importance of these plants to everyday life in those days (Reeds 1976; Bandini Mazzanti et al. 2005). Plants such as fig tree, labdanum, laurel, myrtle, oleander, wild and cultivated olive trees, oregano, oaks, pines, strawberry tree, thistles, thyme and viticulture are all linked to the aesthetic of Mediterranean ecosystems (Herrera 1992; Blondel 2006; Rhizopoulou 2007). The above-mentioned plants, grown in the natural environment, probably reflected a perception of the author's surroundings. Frankincense tree, banana, pepper, sandalwoods and jasmines might reveal appreciation for exotic plants and plant products (Freedman 2005). Furthermore, the HP's narration indicates that wild and cultivated plants were grown side by side; this might be connected to plant niches and patterns of human livelihood (Frayn 1975). Cultivated plants together with grapevines, olive groves and fruit trees might also reveal an arboricultural economy (Montanari 2006). Mentions of wild cedars, centauries and sharp thistles sprouting among historical ruins, as well as pellitory and hammerwort growing in dry cracks of tombs throw some light on environmental botany.

Descriptions of trees and shrubs laden with edible fruits and nuts are highlighted in HP. Cosmological views extolled plant tissues grown close to the sky and deprecated plant tissues grown into and/or close to the soil (Grieco 1991; Bosi et al. 2011; Janick 2012); the taller the plant, the longer the digestion process, and the inferior earthbound sources were transformed into some kind of superior, more ethereal plant material (Grieco 1991). The fruit was a symbol of social standing, which probably explains the intensive cultivation of fruit-bearing trees in central and northern Italy during the thirteenth century (Montanari 2006; Mercuri et al. 2015, and references therein).

In HP, there are quotations related to mysterious plants, such as the Homeric moly (μώλυ) and the ancient silphium (σίλφιον), which might be related to a fascination with antiquity. The black rotted moly [a6] has preoccupied many scholars, being a subject of debate; actually, it has been diversely identified as Allium species [e.g. Allium siculum subsp. dioscoridis (Sm.) K.Richt, Allium nigrum L. (syn. Allium magicum L.), Allium moly L., Allium multibulbosum Jacq. and Allium roseum L.], Leucojum aestivum L., Galanthus nivalis L., Peganum harmala L., Atriplex halimus L. and Withania somnifera (L.) Dunal (Stannard 1962; Carver 1975; Plaitakis and Duvoisin 1983; Amigues 1995). Silphium, which was described by Theophrastus (Helbig 2012) and had a social and economic value for Romans, could have been a Ferula species, although some authors argued that it was a variety of Thapsia garganica L. (Koerper and Kolls 1999; Asciutti 2004; Kiehn 2007; Haas 2008; Kandeler and Ullrich 2009; Day 2013) or Margottia gummifera (Desf.) Lange (Amigues 2004).

Plant citation in literature is a valuable indicator, recording human–nature relationships over time (Thompson and Harris 2010; Day 2013; Rhizopoulou 2014); references to plants of antiquity might be used as a kind of linguistic code that contributed to the "cultural" dimension of the novel. Ancient plant names such as moly (μώλυ) and silphium (σίλφιον) may also reveal historic, botanical knowledge. Furthermore, one might argue that the author had no direct knowledge of the above-mentioned plants and included them in the HP text after encountering them in other books and manuscripts, so extracting botanical information from earlier texts, transmitted and inherited from antiquity (Warren 2004; Sillasoo 2006; Curtius 2013; Russell 2014). It has been stated that early readers did observe that the author of HP drew clusters of similar references from other sources (Russell 2013). For example, Poliphilo after wandering lost and thirsty in a dark forest, gratefully encounters a stream and lists the riparian plants that were growing along its shores [a3‒a4]; an early reader noticed in the marginalia of a HP copy (Russell 2013) that all the plants listed therein can be found in the 25th chapter of Naturalis Historia by Pliny the Elder (first century ad). Pliny the Elder included in his Naturalis Historia ancient knowledge from numerous Roman and Greek authors (Stannard 1965; Nauert 1979; Ogilvie 2006; Rhizopoulou 2008; Totelin 2012; Fögen 2013). In this concept, numerous textual passages of HP represent literal evidence of botanical lore. Nevertheless, plants mentioned in HP might be connected to the author's thorough botanical knowledge and quest.

Phenological traits of plants, mentioned in textual passages of HP, reveal the seasonality of Mediterranean ecosystems, e.g. late summer and autumn, when branches of myrtle and carob trees hung with berries and long ripe fruits [e2, f4′], when vines are fruitful [a3] and arbutus are red-fruited [z6]. The emphasis given to a natural palette of plant colours consistent with a ‛festival of flora' [m6′] during the yellow harvest [l6, m5] and autumn vintage [m5] might indicate aesthetic appreciation of landscapes. Moreover, colourful garlands [df′, o5′] of amethystine violets, crimson and scarlet roses, myrtle, laurel and lysimachia coincide with spring [m4′], i.e. the main flowering season in the Mediterranean region (Rhizopoulou and Pantazi 2015). The flowering hyacinth, quoted in HP [g3′, l2′, o6′], might refer to gladioli (Gladiolus) species (Mabberley 1998), because the first bulbs of the heavily scented Hyacinthus orientalis L. had been brought from the eastern Mediterranean and were cultivated in the botanic garden at Padua, only shortly after its foundation in 1545 (Darlington, Hair, Hurcombe 1951; Rhodes 1984).

Odoriferous substances quoted in HP, [r6′] from frankincense trees (Boswellia sp.), myrrh (Commiphora sp.) [u6′], benzoin, amygdaloid-benzoin or almond-benzoin [o5, r6′] derived from the resin of Styrax spp. and mixed with almonds (Hovaneissian et al. 2008), and Indian unguent [o5] (attributed to Cannabis sativa L., Tucker 1986; Villiers 2001; Frazzetto 2003) provide an insight into exotic plant resources and ‛cosmopolitism' (Freedman 2005). Scents, which enhance sensory perception, might also be a cue for a certain kind of behaviour, in those days. In addition, natural products from indigenous and endemic Mediterranean plants widely used throughout the Mediterranean region, in ancient and medieval times, are included in HP (Table 1); for example, labdanum (a gum extracted from Cistus spp.), mastic [i.e. the resin of Pistacia lentiscus L. var. chia (Desf. ex Poir.) DC., uniquely cultivated in the southern part of the Aegean island of Chios in Greece, has been used for more than 2500 years] and two types of styrax/storax [o5], i.e. scented resin exudates from Styrax officinalis L. (Tayoub et al. 2006; Amigues 2007a) and Liquidambar orientalis Mill. (Fritsch 1999; Yaltrik and Efe 2000; Deforce 2006; Amigues 2007b; Freedman 2011). Information related to the above-mentioned natural plant products could also be derived from other references and literary sources preserved in medieval, monastic scriptoria (Collins 2000; Rhizopoulou 2008).

In HP the symbolic connection between virtues (the spiritual) and plants (the mundane), which compose powerful repertories of symbolism in manuscripts (Warren 2004; Rhizopoulou 2014), was associated with immortality, love, well-being, strength and vulnerability (De Cleene and Lejeune 2003; Rhizopoulou 2004).

The medicinal use of plants was rarely mentioned in HP, although of extreme interest in that era (Palmer 1985; Collins 2000; Rhizopoulou and Katsarou 2008; De Vos 2010); this might be interpreted as resulting from the main topic of the novel, i.e. the deathless love.

It is likely that the 53 woodcuts of HP illustrate plant elements, as features of diverse scene settings, natural sceneries and gardens, and present interaction between textual and visual narration. Plant images are an unequivocal tool for assessing the historical presence of taxa in a particular region (Janick 2010). The woodcuts mostly portray palms, oaks, olives, cypresses, acanthus, vines, ivy and ferns; the above-mentioned plants are frequently found in archaeological sites of the Mediterranean region (Retallack 2008; Mercuri, Sadori, and Blasi 2010; Yılmaz, Akkemik, and Karagöz 2013; Caneva, Savo, and Kumbaric 2014; Rhizopoulou, Papafotiou, and Kanellou 2015). Finally, plant representation in woodcuts of HP might stimulate a relationship with readers, even if that was not the primary purpose of the narrative.

## Conclusions

Hypnerotomachia Poliphili is a literary source that witnesses numerous botanical elements, presented as part of imaginary natural and human-induced landscapes, which have not been studied till now. The treatise provides evidence for the presence of 285 vernacular names of plants and natural plant products, retrieved from 672 different textual passages, which may be a source of contemporary knowledge of plants. Mediterranean shrubs and herbs, ornamentals, fruit-bearing trees and frankincense trees are the most frequently cited. It might be argued that the different frequencies of plants and plant products reflect their importance in daily life, at the time HP was written. Many plants quoted in the text have been presented as vehicles for expressing emotions, allegory, historical knowledge and environmental attributes, thus indicating links between botany and cultural history of plants. However, further work is required to reveal the heritage of the botanical section of Hypnerotomachia Poliphili.
