# Gardens — research pass

> Read 2026-09-01 from `E:\pdf\hypnerotomachia polyphili`, chiefly John Dixon
> Hunt, "Experiencing gardens in the *Hypnerotomachia Polifili*" (*Word & Image*
> 14.1–2, 1998, 109–18) and A. Segre, "Untangling the knot: garden design in
> Francesco Colonna's *Hypnerotomachia Poliphili*" (same volume, 82–108), against
> the 1499 text in `translation/source/` and the woodcut catalogue in `hp.db`.

## 1. The finding that reframes the whole world

**Colonna's book is not a sequence of objects to look at. It is a sequence of
*experiences of looking*, and the misjudgement is part of the design.**

Hunt's argument is that criticism has over-attended to the architecture and
under-attended to the landscape, and that "the experience of landscape
architectural objects by the 'subject', or Polifilo, is very much at the centre of
Colonna's book." He then says the thing that matters most to us:

> Especially on a complex site, the relation of parts to whole is not an
> explanation or comprehension available to first-time visitors… the extreme
> precision of each scene, the extraordinarily haptic description of each sector,
> isolates that particular space and moment; preternaturally clear and explicit,
> its larger meaning… can be baffling. Polifilo, like garden visitors generally,
> is not therefore able to pace or place himself appropriately, either in his
> movement or his thinking. He misjudges his position or even his route, thinking
> an end is in sight when it is not.

Each scene is *preternaturally clear in itself and baffling in its relation to the
whole.* That is a specification for a walkable world, and it happens to vindicate
the call already made in [INTERFACECHOICES.md](INTERFACECHOICES.md) to refuse a
minimap — but for a better reason than the one given there. Not "the processional
axis is the map," but: **not being able to place yourself is the first garden
experience the book stages.**

With one exception, below, which changes everything at the end.

## 2. Three natures, and where our world sits

Hunt places the book inside the humanist doctrine of the **three natures**:

| | | Where the book puts it |
|---|---|---|
| **first nature** | wilderness, wild wood, mountain | the *selva oscura*; the "mountain unnaturall, overgrowne and shaddowed" |
| **second nature** | agriculture, the worked landscape | the "fayre and plentiful countrie, fruitfull fieldes, and fertile groundes" he enters on the way to Eleuterylida |
| **third nature** | garden art, art and nature in collaboration | everything from the bath-house onward, culminating at Cythera |

Poliphilo *compares them explicitly and repeatedly* — Hunt counts the comparisons
running right through the passage between zones. **Our world has first nature (the
dark wood) and third nature (the courts), and no second nature at all.** There is
no worked countryside between the wood and the garden. The meadow we built in the
v2 pass is doing the job of both and therefore reads as neither.

## 3. The paragone, which is the actual subject

The garden sections are an extended contest between art and nature, and Colonna
keeps nudging the reader to judge it. Three set-pieces we do not have:

- **The glass garden.** Ground strewn with "great round balles of glasses lyke
  gunne stones… like pearles shining"; and the flowers' scent is *faked* — "from
  the flowers did breath a sweet fragrancie by some cleare washing with oyle for
  that purpose."
- **The silk garden.** Trunks, branches, leaves and flowers all in "fine silk,
  wanting no store of Pearles to beautify the same," with a gold-wire arbour
  overspread with gold roses "more beautiful to the eye, then if they had been
  growing roses."
- **The water labyrinth.** Water channels replace the paths between hedges, and
  are navigated by boat — the labyrinth *"where the boats go always forward and
  never back,"* which the Dream mode narration already quotes.

**A genuine design trap, and Hunt names it.** The 1499 Italian and the 1592
English both decline to illustrate these three gardens. Only the French 1546
edition illustrated them, and Hunt argues this *damaged* them: drawing on familiar
imagery "inevitably mak[es] them seem more plausible," and "the sense of
exceptional and extraordinary artfulness is diminished." The omission "forces
readers to adjudicate these designs for themselves."

So: **if we model the glass and silk gardens in 3-D, we do exactly what the French
illustrator did, and we lose the thing that makes them worth having.** This is the
first case in the project where the right move may be to *not* build something,
and to find another way to stage it. A proposal is in §7.

## 4. The pergola where he meets Polia — which we already half have

Poliphilo meets Polia for the first time under a pergola tunnel that Hunt notes is
**illustrated not once but twice in the Venice text** — one of very few things the
book bothers to show twice. It is painted carpenter's work "lifting uppe and
bending over," flourishing with "the pleasant and odiferous flowers of three sorts
comixt" — jasmine.

We have a pergola at Polia's garden. It is four columns and a flat lattice. It
should be a **tunnel**, vaulted, and carrying three kinds of jasmine.

Segre adds that at Cythera *all* the routes are covered by vaulted pergolas
carrying ornamental climbers, and that surviving fifteenth-century examples stand
at Villa Medici in Fiesole and Trebbio Castle in the Mugello. The pergola is not
decoration in this book; it is the connective tissue of the whole garden system.

## 5. Cythera, reconstructed

Segre's reconstruction is detailed enough to build from. Cythera is a **perfect
circle, three *miliaria* in circumference, cut by 20 radial roads converging on the
garden of Venus at the centre**, and divided into three concentric *claustri*, each
a *semitertio* (a third of the radius) wide:

| Ring | Contents |
|---|---|
| **Outer — the *bosco*** | Enclosed by cypresses underplanted with myrtle without, a tall espalier of bitter orange within. **20 compartments, each a different tree plantation**, running clockwise from familiar mountain and lowland forest to Greek and North African "mythical" species. Tame wild animals live in it in peace. |
| **Middle — the *prati*** | Three orders of flowery meadow. Each lawn has **a fountain or a topiary sculpture at its centre and four fruit trees at its corners on raised beds — 240 fruit trees in all.** First order apples, second pears, third plums with pistachios. Bounded outside by a road under a vaulted pergola, inside by a peristyle and the river. |
| **Inner — an island within the island** | Enclosed by the river, which is itself **roofed by a vaulted pergola of citrus trees trained into architecture.** Six terraces climb from the bank, each reached by **a flight of seven steps**, rising ~12.5 m in all; the steps break at crossroads marked by ornate gates **for the passage of the triumphal chariots**. Four concentric garden types: conifers in geometric array, then **two rings of knot gardens**, then a wood of fragrant spice trees. |
| **The centre** | The **circular theatre**, its auditorium turned into **three tiers of flower beds, each separated from the one above by a pergola**, with the Fountain of Venus at the heart. |

Two structural notes worth keeping:

- **Planting inverts the usual logic**: the tallest trees are on the *outside*, the
  lowest planting at the centre — "a progression from a wild type of nature to a
  tame one, from a high-canopy and shade-casting plantation to one fully exposed
  to the sun."
- Colonna knows a circular plan distorts its compartments and says so: they are
  ***quadrati illegitimi***, illegitimate squares, trapezes rather than squares. He
  accepts the flaw to keep the magic circle.

**Mountains — first nature — are explicitly absent from Cythera** ("loco non di
monti devii et desueti"). The island is the one place where wilderness has no
share.

## 6. What Cythera does that nowhere else does

Hunt isolates four properties, and the first is the one that should change our
interface:

1. **It is surveyed whole, in advance.** Poliphilo describes the entire topography
   *before he lands*, and only then explores. "Unlike any route Polifilo has
   hitherto taken, these converging paths lead him down axes along which everything
   falls into place." Everywhere else in the book he is lost; here he is oriented.
2. **Plenitude.** A complete collection of herbs, plants, trees, animals, marbles
   and semi-precious stones — even microclimates, "where each tree is suited to
   the nature of the place." Hunt suggests the round Paduan botanical garden of
   1543 may echo it.
3. **Word and image finally agree.** What was fragmentary elsewhere is coherent here.
4. **The paragone resolves.** Not by abolishing artifice: "Cythera's fruit trees
   bear fruit whatever the season; yet the topiary is clipped every day." What is
   excluded is *counterfeiting* — the silk flowers, the faked scent. Hunt notes
   the English word carries the full weight of coining false money, a capital
   offence.

## 7. The Venus fountain has an enclosure we never built

Hunt, on the climax of Book I:

> A natural source or spring is fashioned into a fountain — a tactful
> re-presentation; so, too the pergola, the structure of which we might expect to
> be wood but is the finest gold; this is covered with roses that now, unlike those
> encountered earlier, are 'natural'. The enclosure, like so many medieval
> representations of a flowery mead, is at once meadow and garden… The partitions
> of the enclosure are woven or are depicted as having the zig-zag patterns of
> sliced marble.

And the enclosure's balustrade is, like the pergola, **illustrated twice in the
Venice edition.** So the fountain we rebuilt from chapter XXIII is only the jewel;
its setting is missing — a gold pergola carrying real roses, a flowery mead that is
"at once meadow and garden," and a balustrade patterned in book-matched marble.

That last detail also answers the paragone: the *gold* pergola carries *real*
roses. Artifice in the structure, nature in the growth. It is the book's thesis
built as an object.

## What to build, ranked

1. **The Venus fountain's enclosure** — gold pergola, real roses, flowery mead,
   zig-zag marble balustrade. Completes a station we have already rebuilt and is
   the resolution of the book's central argument. *One session.*
2. **Turn the Polia pergola into a tunnel** with three jasmines. Small, and it is
   one of only two things the book illustrates twice.
3. **Second nature** — a worked countryside between the wood and the courts:
   fields, orchard rows, a vineyard on elms. Currently the meadow is asked to be
   both wilderness-edge and garden and reads as neither.
4. **Cythera, properly** — the concentric island. This is the largest single build
   remaining in the project and the book's culmination; Segre's plan above is
   detailed enough to work from directly. See [RESEARCHPASSFORVR.md](RESEARCHPASSFORVR.md)
   for why it should probably come before anything else.
5. **The three artificial gardens — but not modelled.** Per §3, building them
   plausibly destroys them. Proposal: stage them as *described* rather than shown —
   a place where the walls carry the 1592 text and the geometry is suggested by
   glass and silk *materials* on otherwise abstract forms, so the visitor is put in
   the position of adjudicating rather than admiring. This is the one place in the
   world where withholding is the faithful move.
