# Page 239 — Chapter XVIII

In this place, then, upon the fresh and flower-bearing little herbs, we gladly exposed ourselves to sit. Thus
staying, insatiable, with the face-beholding eyes I subtly contemplated, in one sole perfect and untouched
little body, so much congruity, and accumulation of beauty — an object, without doubt, refusing (that I) should
not see a more gracious thing beyond, (with) my eyes, nor of so much content — where, of new and re-sprouting
conceits, my ardent heart, with tacit joy refreshing, and somewhat the vulgar and common sillinesses deposed, I
considered a more intelligible effect; and, together, the most-serene sky, the salubrious and most-mild air, the
delightful site, the delicious fatherland, the ornate verdures, the pleasant and temperate hills ornated with
opaque little groves, the clement weather and pure airs, and the comely and pleasant place — dignified by the
rivers flowing down, irriguous, through the wooded valley, near the curved hills, to the right and left part
softly running, to the near sea precipitating; a most-salubrious field, and of most-pleasant grass, filled with
manifold trees, canorous of the concert of little birds. Let yield here, then, the Thessalian river and field
(Tempe). And there, together thus sitting, among the springing and redolent flowers and roses, in this celestial
effigy, with so much delight the gaping eyes, occupied, fixedly I held; and to so beautiful and rare a make, and
divine image, with all the senses, gazing, most-devoted and applied — and, in me, more pleasant, resulting the
heated vexing impulses, in which the soul, from sweetness liquefied, insane I stood, and all anxious, projected
all and curious to consider, marvelling, by what mode and reason that purpling liquor — at the touch of the
precious flesh of the little swelling wrist of the hand, remaining most-pure milk, for some little space — to its
place returned not. No less, with what artifice, in this most-comely body, the mistress nature had particularly
dispensed, and stuffingly disseminated, all the Arabic fragrance; and how, again, industriously, in her starry
forehead, of gold threads becomingly vine-tendrilled, she had fixed the most-beautiful part of the sky, or the
shining Heraclean (star).

Then, to the comely and slender feet the sight converting, I beheld on them the vermilion shoes violently drawn
on, and, over the ivory instep, moon-wise mouthed and sinuated with a *phytontea* (plant-like) opening, with
little gold rings, and with little cords of cyan silk ensnared, and tightly re-bound — most-apt instruments to
intercalate (bind) the life, and excessively to torment the more the inflamed heart. Then, straightway, the
lascivious regard returned to the straight throat, berried in a circle with oriental pearls — not understanding,
of the one and the other whiteness, the true distinction — of a sudden descended to the glittering breast, and
delightful bosom, where sprouted two round little apples, resistant to the garment, and obstinately repugnant;
nor such, without fail, in the orchard of the Hesperides, did Hercules furtively gather; nor such does Pomona ever
see in her orchard, as these — most-white in the rosy breast, standing immovable, fixed, whiter than the flocked
and lucid snow —

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The lovers sit on the shore before the ruin, and the page turns from the Polyandrion to
**Poliphilo's rapt contemplation of Polia** — her whole beauty, the landscape around them (a Tempe-surpassing
vale), and then a head-to-foot inventory (the rose-liquid that turns to milk on her wrist, her Arabic fragrance,
her gold hair, her red laced shoes, her pearl-throat, her breast). All secure; a beauty-ekphrasis in the book's
most sensuous manner.

- **The landscape as locus amoenus.** Between the ruined tomb and the beloved, Colonna sets a perfect pastoral —
  mild air, wooded hills, twin rivers running to the sea, birdsong — "let yield the Thessalian Tempe." The
  Polyandrion's death is framed by life and beauty: the setting refuses to be only funereal. A vivid brief for
  the world's polyandrion surroundings (a green, river-cut shore around the ruin).
- **the portrait resumes.** Poliphilo's gaze runs Polia head to foot (as at the triumph, p. 337, and the boat,
  p. 287): the rose-water that becomes milk on her wrist, her fragrance, her star-set golden hair, her laced
  vermilion shoes ("instruments to torment the heart"), her pearl-throat, her breast "whiter than snow." The
  sensuous inventory the whole book returns to whenever the beloved is still.
- **"instruments to intercalate the life."** Even Polia's shoe-laces are figured as bonds that "bind the life
  and torment the heart" — the recurring conceit of beauty as sweet captivity (cf. the rose-withes, p. 341; the
  hair-snares, p. 333). Desire is always, for Colonna, a willing bondage.
- **the milk-and-rose wrist** — a delicate marvel: rose-water touched to her wrist turns to milk and does not
  run off, so pure is her flesh; Poliphilo puzzles at the "mode and reason" (his optics-mind again, cf. pp. 208,
  213). Beauty posed as a natural wonder to be investigated.
