# Page 439 — Chapter XXXII

"— (was) enticement, and incitement of love, and so-much singular beauty, as the most-workmanlike Jove could ever imagine
to put in them; and, in the residual formal and specious figment, put on her every perfect diligence — (such) that
Phianor, in the effigy of Neptune (nature's imitator), knew not, nor could, to paint. Which breathes a similitude of
purpling roses, commingled among milky and whitening lilies. And among the most-purple lips breathed a *Myropolium*
(perfumery), and Emporium of wondrous fragrance, in a little apothecary of whitest Ivory (in most-tiny teeth orderly
disposed) hedged. The head most-blond — (such that) not so is the Baetican straw matured — more beautiful to the eye than
if she had of the little river Gracis tasted. Which all things, chiefly, in her manifestly (beyond the notable occultation)
seeing — not only content, but certainly, above whatever lover, most-happy I would appreciate myself, if she should give me
her most-precious affect — with the soul, to her volitant, secretly saying: 'O highest Gods — thus, could I aptly reduce and
violate her to my inflamed desires, as Acontius reduced Cydippe (deceived by the deceitful apple), or with commodious
fortune, as the fierce Achilles conquered the most-gentle Deidamia — or by another way!' And how much the more most-intent I
stood, in immense delightment and most-jocund delight — and no other really than a celestial demonstration I seemed
presentially to enjoy — and, clearly seeing her, and, now laughing, and now mannerly speaking, some time toward me directing
her starry and most-pleasing eyes (accompanied with two vermilion roses, suffused of honesty and of elegance); and now,
expert and aptly to the sacred instituted and imposed offices ministering, with Nymphal gestures, with entire and devout
intent, and with matronal gravity; and some time, to the ears arriving that voice which, arousing, invited my soul to the
exit, and to the repudiation of its dear conjunct (its body) — all my spirits commoved themselves to me, feeling myself all
to be covered and surrounded of an inexpert sweetness — insomuch that the soul, neglecting its natural domicile, ever thus,
with Lady Polia, at pleasant feasts, it would, moribund, have persevered. Thence, then, I recognised the impetuous insult
to the amorous fire, and, of this, its quickening, by her, to contemplate. Nor, of this, knew I to redeem, with valid
forces of wit, the insanable eyes from the sweet allurement — of my heart, by the most-shapely face pedicated (led captive).
But, sighing, tacit, with most-firm purpose, I said: 'Of this notable Nymph, for certain, am I wholly. In her most-white
breast consists all my flattering hope, and in it I have reposited and —'"

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Poliphilo's beauty-praise completes (Polia's rose-and-lily face, her ivory teeth in a fragrant
mouth, her blond hair), and he recounts falling wholly in love — wishing to win her (as Acontius won Cydippe by the
inscribed apple, or Achilles Deidamia), watching her minister at the rites, his soul so captivated it "would have died
persevering at the feast," and resolving "of this notable Nymph I am wholly (hers)." All secure; his coup de foudre
complete.

- **His surrender — "I am wholly hers."** Poliphilo's vow ("of this Nymph I am wholly… in her breast all my hope") is the
  exact counterpart to Polia's "I am wholly his" (chs. XXXI). Both lovers, in their own narrations, surrender totally to
  the other. The mutual self-gift the union requires is spoken twice, from both sides — Book II's deepest structural
  point.
- **Acontius and Cydippe.** Poliphilo wishes to win Polia "as Acontius reduced Cydippe" — the lover who tricked his beloved
  into an unbreakable vow by an inscribed apple (Ovid, *Heroides* 20–21). This is the mythic seed of his *letter-writing*
  (the argument, p. 436: "the invention of sending her an epistle"): like Acontius, he will woo by writing. The exemplum
  cues the courtship-by-letters that follows.
- **the soul that would die at the feast.** So captivated is Poliphilo that his soul "neglects its natural domicile" and
  would "die persevering" gazing at Polia — the ecstatic self-loss of love (cf. Polia's soul "transmigrated among his
  lips," p. 431). Both lovers describe the same dissolution of self into the beloved.
- **the beauty-ekphrasis motivated.** The inventory of Polia's beauty (roses-and-lilies, ivory teeth, blond hair) is here
  the *cause* of love, not mere ornament — the book's habitual beauty-catalogue given narrative function. Poliphilo's
  suffering and letter follow (pp. 440+).
