"Come to the fourth tower, they find other young women, wrestlers and fighters, and these, having
examined the first tokens, draw those friendly to their exercise. And those who have not their like
they let pass on with their own. And in these circuits they find the water still more against them,
where there is need of greater study and toilsome labour of rowing.

"Landing at the fifth tower, they find it mirroring, in which they contemplate how fair is their like;
and with this most joyful and most longed-for delight, keeping it in mind, they pass on with a more
laborious course. In this place is plainly discussed that saying, and golden: *the blessed have held to
the middle*. Not of line, not of place, but of time is this passage and bound, where with a sincere
examination the middle is discerned, and with whom happiness is coupled, or the blessedness of wit, or
of plenty. Which, not having with them, in what follows they are the less able to gain it.

"Taking leave from here, the waters through the circling windings begin to draw somewhat their swift
course towards the middle end, and with little rowing, or none, they are carried to the sixth. Here
they find elegant matrons, with unwedded and chaste looks, intent upon the divine worship; by the
divine aspect of whom the guests, taken with love of them, condemning the former and turning to a
loathing of it, hold with these a quiet commerce and a peaceful passage through the seven windings.

"These being passed, through those that follow, with a darkening of the air, with many discomforts and
a toilsome voyage, they weigh the crossing by much running. Because the more the winding of the
passages draws near in the figure to the centre, the shorter they are. And so much the more one runs,
fallen, with an ineffectual swiftness, and with slippery wanderings, into the whirlpool of the central
tower; and with the utmost affliction of soul for the remembrance of the fair places and the fellowship
left behind, and the more that they know they cannot return by turning the prow of their keel, because
continually at their stern are the prows of the others sailing. There comes to a greater pain the
frightful title above the entrance of the middle tower, in this Attic expression: THEON LYKOS
DYSALGETOS.
