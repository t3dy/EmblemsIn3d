# Page 8 — Front matter

*(Two Latin epigrams by Andrea Marone of Brescia. The first is a dialogue with the Muse; the
second is addressed to Polia.)*

ANDREA MARONE OF BRESCIA.

> *Whose work is it, Muse, say?* — It is mine, and the eight sisters'.
>    *Yours? Then why was the title given to Poliphilo?*
> — He earned still more from us, our common foster-child.
>    *But I ask: who is Poliphilo, by his true name?*
> — We do not wish him to be known. *Why?* — It is settled to see first
>    whether rabid envy devours even things divine.
> If it spares him, what then? He will be known. And if not? We do not
>    think you worthy of Poliphilo's true name.

> O how happy beyond all mortals you alone are,
>    Polia, who live though dead — and better so.
> While Poliphilo lies buried in deep sleep,
>    he makes you keep vigil, through the learned mouths of men.

---

## Notes

**Confidence: high.** Read against the facsimile by a second reader, 2026-09-20 (Siena
O.III.38, f. 4v). The flagged reading in the first epigram is not a conjecture at all — the
1499 punctuates it — and the query is cleared. The second epigram was mistranslated, agent and
patient reversed in the last line, and is corrected below.

- **This page is the reason nobody knows who wrote the book, and it is a taunt.** Marone stages
  a little interrogation: *who is Poliphilo really?* And the Muses answer: **we do not wish him to
  be known** — and we will not tell *you*, because you are not worthy of the name. The anonymity
  is not an accident of the archive. It is announced, on the ninth page, as a policy, and it is
  made a test of the reader.
- **"Whether rabid envy devours even things divine."** The stated reason for withholding the name
  is to see how the book is received first. If envy spares it, the author will be known; if not,
  he stays hidden. Nobody ever published the name — which, taken at face value, is a verdict on
  how the *Hypnerotomachia* was received in 1499. Crasso is said never to have recovered his
  costs.
- **And yet the name is in the book.** The great initials of the thirty-eight chapters spell
  POLIAM FRATER FRANCISCVS COLVMNA PERAMAVIT — "Brother Francesco Colonna loved Polia
  exceedingly". The acrostic was noticed early. So the front matter refuses the name in Latin
  verse while the body of the book spells it out in capitals, which is either the joke or the
  point.
- **"You live though dead — and better so."** Polia is dead. She lives in the poem, in learned
  speech, on the page — and the epigram says this is the *better* life. That is the book's whole
  argument in a couplet, and it is the argument Poliphilo loses at the end, when what he embraces
  turns out to be an image and it leaves him.
- **The last line, corrected 2026-09-20 — it had been turned around.** The first pass gave "you
  make the man keep watch, through learned lips", making Polia the one who acts and Poliphilo
  the one kept awake. The Latin will not have it: *Te* is accusative and cannot be the subject
  of *facit*, and *virum* is the syncopated genitive plural of *vir*, not an accusative
  singular — *docta per ora virum*, "through the learned mouths of men". That phrase is a tag,
  and a famous one: Ennius's own epitaph, *volito vivus per ora virum*, "I fly living through
  the mouths of men", which Virgil borrows at *Georgics* 3.9. It is the standard Latin figure
  for outliving death by being spoken of. So the sentence runs the other way, and means more:
  **while Poliphilo lies buried in sleep, he is the one who keeps *her* awake**, in the mouths
  of learned readers. Which is exactly what the couplet above it promised — *you live though
  dead, and better so* — and exactly what this whole book is: a dead woman kept talking. The
  antithesis is the book's own pun, *somno* against *pervigilare*, sleeping against keeping
  vigil, and the first reading lost it.
- **"Quid eritnoscetur" — cleared 2026-09-20, by the facsimile.** The Wikisource transcription
  runs two words together, and the first pass repaired them by conjecture. No conjecture is
  needed: the 1499 prints a mark between them. Aldus's question mark in this fount is a small
  curled figure over a point, and it is all over these eight lines — after *musa*, after
  *Vestrum*, after *titulus*, after *Poliphilus*, after *cur* — so it is unmistakable when it
  turns up again between *erit* and *noscetur*, and once more after *sin minus*. The line reads
  *Si parcet, quid erit? noscetur. sin minus? haud uos …*. Two consequences. **First**, the
  division is the printer's, not ours. **Second**, *quid erit* is the idiom "what then? what
  will come of it?", not "what will he be?", and the English is changed accordingly; *noscetur*
  answers it, "he will be known". The same reading of the exchange is in Lefaivre 1997 (in the
  corpus, `md/Liane_Lefaivre_…`), where the identification is hers and the wording is not ours
  — though her last line, "we are not worthy", drops the *vos*: *haud vos dignamur* is **we do
  not think you worthy**, and the insult is aimed at the reader, which is the whole point of
  the poem.
- **And a word restored in line 2.** The 1499 has *Vestrum? cur datus est Poliphilo titulus?* —
  two questions, not one. The questioner catches the Muse out: *Yours?* — then why does the
  title say Poliphilo? The first pass dropped *Vestrum*; it is back.
- **Andrea Marone** (c. 1474–1527) of Brescia was a celebrated Latin improviser, later at the
  court of Leo X. He was in his twenties when this was printed.
