# Page 8 — Front matter

*(Two Latin epigrams by Andrea Marone of Brescia. The first is a dialogue with the Muse; the
second is addressed to Polia.)*

ANDREA MARONE OF BRESCIA.

> *Whose work is it, Muse, say?* — It is mine, and the eight sisters'.
>    *Why then was the title given to Poliphilo?*
> — He earned still more from us, our common foster-child.
>    *But I ask: who is Poliphilo, by his true name?*
> — We do not wish him to be known. *Why?* — It is settled to see first
>    whether rabid envy devours even things divine.
> If it spares him, [?what will he be? He will be known]. But if not, we do not
>    think you worthy of Poliphilo's true name.

> O how happy beyond all mortals you alone are,
>    Polia, who live though dead — and better so.
> While Poliphilo lies buried in deep sleep,
>    you make the man keep watch, through learned lips.

---

## Notes

**Confidence: high** for the sense. The first epigram's syntax is compressed even by epigram
standards, and the sixth and seventh lines are printed with a missing space in the source
(*quid eritnoscetur*); the reading given here — *quid erit? noscetur* — is the obvious one.

- **This page is the reason nobody knows who wrote the book, and it is a taunt.** Marone stages
  a little interrogation: *who is Poliphilo really?* And the Muses answer: **we do not wish him to
  be known** — and we will not tell *you*, because you are not worthy of the name. The anonymity
  is not an accident of the archive. It is announced, on the ninth page, as a policy, and it is
  made a test of the reader.
- **"Whether rabid envy devours even things divine."** The stated reason for withholding the name
  is to see how the book is received first. If envy spares it, the author will be known; if not,
  he stays hidden. Nobody ever published the name — which, taken at face value, is a verdict on
  how the *Hypnerotomachia* was received in 1499. Crasso is said never to have recovered his
  costs.
- **And yet the name is in the book.** The great initials of the thirty-eight chapters spell
  POLIAM FRATER FRANCISCVS COLVMNA PERAMAVIT — "Brother Francesco Colonna loved Polia
  exceedingly". The acrostic was noticed early. So the front matter refuses the name in Latin
  verse while the body of the book spells it out in capitals, which is either the joke or the
  point.
- **"You live though dead — and better so."** Polia is dead. She lives in the poem, in learned
  speech, on the page — and the epigram says this is the *better* life. That is the book's whole
  argument in a couplet, and it is the argument Poliphilo loses at the end, when what he embraces
  turns out to be an image and it leaves him.
- **"You make the man keep watch."** *Pervigilare facit* — while Poliphilo sleeps, Polia keeps
  him awake. The paradox is exact: the dreamer is unconscious, and the dead woman is the one
  doing the watching, through the mouths of the learned who read her.
- **Andrea Marone** (c. 1474–1527) of Brescia was a celebrated Latin improviser, later at the
  court of Leo X. He was in his twenties when this was printed.
