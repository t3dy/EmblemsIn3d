Finally, for the absolution of this marvellous structure of Temple, it remains to say briefly that it was
compacted of quadrates of Augustan stone in part, and part of the above-narrated marble, in perfect
jointing, without iron and timber, with more subtle investigation of sculpture than ever in our century
could be done, nor imagined — nor such did they construct for Apis, the Samnitic-Egyptian God.

Under the bases of the little Pilasters, or quadrangulars — which was the lowest and continuous binding, in
the first band, or wall; and the upper girdle likewise, with the encompassing capitals — went round, in the
most-even pavement, a strip, or band, of finest Porphyry, as much as was the projection of its square, and
contiguous, almost without dispute, another of Ophite. Under the sub-base of the columns was circled, as
much as its thickness, a strip of Porphyry, with two collateral of hardest serpentine, with the order of
the Peristyle in circumduction. The like was seen of the orifice of the cistern, girt in the pavement — a
strip of Porphyry and another of Ophite.

The remainder of the much-admired pavement, between the well-orifice and the peristyle, was of marvellous
emblemature, of little-minutiae, of little tessellation of fine stones circling themselves, elegantly
included, deduced in partition: ten roundels, a foot in diameter, of their colour and species, the one to
the line of the other. There were two of reddening Jasper, most-pleasingly suffused with various spots; two
of Litharmenon, of little sparks of gold, more minute, disseminated of rutilant atoms; two of green Jasper,
variegated with chalcedonic veins, with reddening and yellow spots; two of Agate, confusedly undulated of
milky threads; two, lastly, of most-limpid Chalcedony. And, by the narrowness of the lines toward the
cistern, likewise the circular figures decreased.

Under the vaulting were, in the floor, an *Asaroton* of wormwork emblems — foliage, animals, and flowers,
tessellated of most-minute little bodies, of stone-cuttings diligently tessellated, depicted, and most-
evenly rubbed smooth, or sculptured. Which art Zenodorus had not, in paving at Pergamon; nor such was the
*lithostroton* at Praeneste, in the shrine of Fortune.

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The temple's construction (dry-jointed **Augustan-stone and marble** ashlar, no
iron or wood) and its **pavement**: porphyry-and-ophite banding round the pilasters, columns and well; ten
foot-wide **inlaid roundels** (red jasper, gold-flecked litharmenon, green jasper, agate, chalcedony) radiating
in; and an **Asaroton** mosaic floor of foliage, animals and flowers under the ambulatory-vaults, surpassing
Zenodorus and the Praeneste Fortuna. All secure.

- **The floor, buildable.** The pavement is a radial composition: porphyry/serpentine bands framing the
  peristyle and the central well, ten colour-graded stone roundels stepping inward (shrinking toward the
  cistern), and, under the aisle-vaults, a figured *Asaroton* mosaic (the "unswept floor" type — scattered
  naturalistic motifs). A complete floor-scheme for the world's temple.
- **dry construction "without iron and timber."** The whole temple is mortarless, ironless ashlar (like the
  Cythera theatre, p. 350) — Colonna's ideal of pure stereotomy. The building holds by cutting alone.
- **the Praeneste lithostroton and Zenodorus** — the Nile-mosaic of the temple of Fortuna at Praeneste (the
  most famous Roman floor) and Zenodorus's work at Pergamon: the benchmarks the temple's floor exceeds. The
  surpassing-topos, on pavements (cf. the Cythera pergola-floors, p. 355).
- **back to the pinnacle** — the description now climbs to the cupola's gilt finial (continued p. 210),
  closing the temple's fabric from floor to summit before the rites begin.
