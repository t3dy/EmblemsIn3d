> "To the shades. Publia Cornelia Annia — that I might not, in desolate bereavement, survive, wretched, alive — of my own
> will give myself, condemned (to death), into this coffin with my dead husband, loved with incomparable love; with whom
> I lived twenty years without any controversy (quarrel). (I charge) our children and freedmen that yearly, over our

> ordered it, and by testament, to be made. Farewell, life."

## Notes

**Confidence: high** (both the Latin epitaph and the hieroglyph-interpretation are secure; some abbreviations expanded). A
widow's self-entombment: **Cornelia Annia**, refusing to outlive her husband of twenty happy years, gives herself into his
coffin and endows a **perpetual rose-and-sacrifice rite**; then a translucent stone chest holding **two preserved bodies**,
gold crowns, a burning lamp and grave-goods, its front carved with **Egyptian hieroglyphs** (decoded on p. 262). All secure.

- **Cornelia Annia — love unto death, chosen.** Unlike the tragedies of fortune, this is a *willed* union in death: a widow
  who "would not survive, wretched," and joins her husband in the grave, endowing an annual rite (Pluto, Proserpina, roses,
  a feast). Her "twenty years without a quarrel" and closing "Farewell, life" make it the Polyandrion's tenderest *conjugal*
  epitaph — faithful love that outlasts death by choice. A model for the world's polyandrion (an endowed double-tomb).
- **the preserved lovers in the ivory chest.** The translucent *chemites* stone (Pliny: a marble that preserved bodies, in
  which Darius was said to lie) lets Poliphilo see **two whole bodies** with gold crowns and a lamp still burning — an intact
  Egyptian-style double burial. A haunting, buildable centrepiece for the world's crypt: a glowing sarcophagus with the
  lovers visible within, a lamp burning over them.
- **the endowed anniversary, again** — like the gladiator-epitaph (p. 254) and the Polyandrion's own festival (p. 237),
  Cornelia founds a perpetual rite. The tombs repeatedly buy remembrance: love's refusal to be forgotten is the chapter's
  undertone.
- The hieroglyph on the chest (a meditation on death joining lovers) is read on p. 262.
