## Notes

**Confidence: high** (the epitaph is secure, read from the woodcut). The first individual **tomb-epitaph**: an
"Altar of the infernal gods" to **Laodia Publia**, who "defrauded her age" (denied her youth), refused Love against
the custom of maidens, and killed herself with the sword — one of the ice-damned (the cold, love-refusing).
All secure.

- **Laodia Publia — the frigid suicide.** Her epitaph names the second of the Polyandrion's two sins (p. 250): she
  was *cold* to love, "refused, against the custom of maidens, the commands of Love," and, despairing, killed
  herself. Not a martyr of love but a refuser of it — yet buried here among love's dead, and (by the fresco's logic)
  destined for the ice. The Polyandrion holds both kinds. A concrete epitaph the world/novel can carry.
- **the form.** Colonna gives the tombs in authentic Roman epigraphic style — *Ara Deum Infernorum*, *Viator*
  ("traveller," the standard address to the passer-by), the abbreviations (*GLAD. INTERF.*): the epitaphs read like
  real inscriptions, and are among the sources for Renaissance epigraphy. The world's polyandrion can carry these
  verbatim on its tombs.
- **"much gladdened, I departed"** — Poliphilo, absorbed in antiquarian reading, is *cheered* by the tombs: the
  therapy Polia intended (p. 242) is working — the graveyard has cooled his ardour into scholarly delight.
