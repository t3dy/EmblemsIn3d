## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The nurse's argument at its height: **Cupid overpowers even the gods** — Jupiter (made to disguise
himself for love), Mars (his armour useless), Apollo (punished for exposing Venus's adultery); his **two arrows** (golden =
love, leaden = hatred) that he wields as he pleases; therefore Polia, a mortal, must not "resist what she cannot resist"
but yield to love. All secure; the positive case (Love is inevitable and irresistible).

- **The two arrows — the key to Polia's cruelty.** The nurse names the crucial fact: Cupid's **leaden arrow** excites
  "superbity, rabid hatred, and cruelty." Polia's coldness was not her own virtue but Love's *leaden wound* (Apollo's
  punishment) — she was made cruel by the god she scorned. This reframes her guilt: she is a victim of the leaden arrow,
  curable by the golden one. The two arrows (first named at Cupid's quiver, p. 339) here become the mechanism of the whole
  Book-II drama: hatred (lead) to be replaced by love (gold).
- **resist not the irresistible.** The nurse's practical wisdom: "never oppose what you cannot resist… do not flee the
  inevitable." Since Love conquers gods, resistance is folly, not chastity. This is the argument that turns Polia — not
  that love is good, but that refusing it is *impossible and punished*. Prudence, not passion, opens the door; passion
  follows (ch. XXIX).
- **Apollo's punishment** — struck with the golden arrow (to love Daphne) and his beloveds with the leaden (to flee him),
  because he exposed Venus and Mars: even the sun-god is Love's plaything. The surpassing-of-the-gods argument (cf. p. 286)
  turned to counsel.
- The nurse's counsel concludes, and sends Polia to Venus's priestess (pp. 412+).
