## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Polia's heart stays frozen even after his death ("more cruel than Phineus… as if I had mirrored
myself in Medusa"); she recounts, once more, how his sweetest pleas could not soften "that rigid and Tigrean breast" (love
could no more fix on it than wax on wet rock); and, leaving him dead at nightfall, she flees home "a guilty butcher" —
when a **whirlwind snatches her into a pathless, thorny wood**. All secure; the transition to the vision.

- **The heart wax cannot stick to.** Polia's central self-image: her breast so hard that Love, "like wax on wet rock,"
  could not adhere — pity "incarcerated" behind reins she could not break. The stone/wax imagery (cf. porphyry, p. 389;
  the flint, p. 396) is relentless: she is the un-thawable heart the whole book is about melting.
- **the whirlwind — the machinery of punishment.** A wind snatches her (unharmed) into a wild wood: the supernatural turn
  that begins her punishment-vision (the tortured damsels, p. 400). It parallels Poliphilo's own transports in Book I —
  Polia now gets her own dream-ordeal, the female counterpart. Her conversion will come through terror, as his love came
  through wonder.
- **"conscious and guilty butcher of his amorous heart."** Again her own verdict (cf. "conscious malefactress," p. 397).
  Polia narrates herself as a self-confessed killer; the vision that follows is the punishment such a killer earns
  (Anaxarete's fate, p. 396), shown to her before it can claim her.
- **Phineus, Harpalyce, the eagle-stone** — the exempla of cruelty and hardness; Colonna arms even Polia's self-accusation
  with learning. The vision proper opens on p. 400.
