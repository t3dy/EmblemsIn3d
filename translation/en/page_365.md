and her silent sister Critoa, and be inseparable companions of this our athlete
and loving servant of Polia. And by my fated command be the cause that both of
them are of equal and mutual affection. And without pause she drew from within
the oyster-shell two rings, each with a precious violet gem set turning in it,
and gave one to Polia and the other to me, with most eloquent command that we
should endure forever adorned with such a divine gift, and should keep her edict
deep and always hidden, with a serene brow and a kindly, soothing face.

And turning in a moment, to you, Polia, she said: likewise I present four other
noble and most accomplished maidens into your unceasing company, who shall make
you worthy and elegantly honour you in this most celebrated love of yours.

Calling them too from that place, at leisure from their songs and sounds, she
charged Adiacorista with the three noble sisters, Pistinia, Sophrosyne and
Edosia, saying: now let these not leave her for the least while without you, so
that she, loving her Poliphilo by equal law, may live bound in a Herculean knot,
adorned with the most accomplished and most beautiful love that ever in her —

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Venus's speech is long, formal, and syntactically
tangled in the original, and English has to break its periods; the sense is
secure throughout. The nymphs' names are Colonna's Greek coinages for the
virtues each personifies (below), and rendering them as names rather than
translating them is a deliberate choice — as with the sense-nymphs, the reader
is meant to decode them.

- **The two sets of four attendants** are the marriage's counsellors, and their
  names are their offices. On Poliphilo's side: **Enosina** (union), **Monori**,
  **Phrontis** (φροντίς, care/forethought) and her "silent sister" **Critoa**
  (judgement). On Polia's: **Adiacorista** (the undivided), **Pistinia** (πίστις,
  faith), **Sophrosyne** (temperance — the one name a reader will recognise
  outright), and **Edosia** (also a keeper of the third door, above — decorum).
  Together they are the virtues a good marriage is dowered with.
- **Picus and Canens** — Picus, king of Latium, so faithful to his wife Canens
  that he refused Circe and was turned to a woodpecker for it. The model of the
  steadfast husband, and a transformation-myth again, which is this chapter's
  recurring key.
- **the Herculean knot** — the *nodus Herculeus*, the knot that bound a Roman
  bride's girdle, proverbially hard to loose: the emblem of indissoluble
  marriage.
- **the violet gems set "turning" (anterota)** — the stones are mounted to
  revolve in the ring; *anteros* (returned love) may also be punned. Kept as the
  literal mounting.
