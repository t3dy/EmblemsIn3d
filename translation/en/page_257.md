## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** A broken vase-inscription (illegible), then a **destroyed tribune** whose mosaic shows a
**shipwreck-romance** in fragments — a man afflicting a girl, a shipwreck, a youth swimming to shore carrying a girl,
a lion, the two rowing a boat; the story is spelled out in a **Greek epigram** on a bronze tablet, which moved
Poliphilo to tears and which he renders "as best I could" into Latin (the full text is on p. 258). All secure.

- **The mosaic and its epigram.** The fragmentary painting (man, shipwreck, rescue-swim, lion, boat) is a picture-
  narrative whose story the Greek epigram tells in full: the tragedy of **Leontia and Lollius** (p. 258). Colonna
  pairs image and text — the ruin shows the tale twice, in paint and in letters. A rich programme for a world's
  polyandrion tribune (a shipwreck-mosaic with its epitaph).
- **the Greek rendered into Latin.** Poliphilo notes he translates the Greek epigram into Latin "as best I could" —
  a nice mise-en-abyme of *this* translation-project: the reader translating the tomb, as we translate the reader.
  The Latin he gives (p. 258) is one of the book's longest inscriptions, a complete prose romance.
- **moved to tears** — the antiquarian's detachment breaks: this epitaph makes Poliphilo weep and "damn the guilty."
  The Polyandrion's tombs are not mere curiosities but move the reader — the effect the world/novel should aim for.
- The full Leontia-and-Lollius epitaph follows on p. 258.
