I in the least doubt not that the solacing Nymphs — who, for a longish delay, intently had lent benign audience — beyond the
supreme delight, took no scanty and exiguous admiration of the loves which Polia, adolescent-girl, distinctly, with such
comeliness, narrating, had prosecuted. And, the term imposed to the prolix little history, all rose from the quiet sitting.
And, just as she, enucleatedly, with supreme and wondrous fluency, narrated — neither more nor less she bound, inter-weaving
the most-odorous florets into a circular little chaplet; and, with the sweet words, it was completed — and, to my head,
affectionately genuflecting herself, cunningly she placed (it); and, with twin nectareous and cinnamon lips, dove-wise
kissing me. For which thing the Nymphs extremely lauded, approving the most-charming speaking, and the adorned fluency; and
that, with elegant procession, and outstanding gestures, and with preclear beauty, had been her most-polished eloquy — high,
and of memorable worth. Then, much more most-pleasing, the understanding of her high and noble origin, and egregious stock,
and generous lineage, and of the notable family, illustrious and ancient, and of the happy exit of her love, thus orderly
recited.

Incontinently, to their Nymphal diversions, jests, and delights, most-facetious and hilarious and all festive, they
returned — beginning the muted (many) instruments, with canorous music, to sound, and to the celestial canticles composedly
to enter, dancing in a ring around the sacred fount most-limpid, flowing sweet liquors —

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Polia's narration ends and she **finishes the floral chaplet** (woven as she spoke), crowns
Poliphilo with it and kisses him; the **nymphs**, who listened, praise her eloquence and story, and **return to their
dances** around the sacred fount. All secure; the frame closing. *(The argument foretells the book's end: Polia, embracing
Poliphilo, disappears, and the sleep — the awakening.)*

- **The chaplet woven as the tale was told.** A lovely closing image: Polia has been weaving a flower-chaplet *while*
  narrating her love, and finishes it exactly as the story ends — then crowns Poliphilo with it. The tale and the garland
  complete together; her love-story becomes a crown for her lover. A tender, buildable emblem for the world/novel (the
  beloved crowning the lover with the flowers of the finished tale).
- **the argument foretells the awakening.** The chapter-argument names the book's imminent end: Polia, "most-tightly
  embracing him — she disappeared, and the sleep." The whole dream is about to dissolve at the moment of deepest union: the
  embrace that would consummate the love is the instant Poliphilo wakes (ch. XXXVIII, the epilogue). The dream ends where
  it is fullest — the melancholy of the *Hypnerotomachia* (the "strife of love in a dream"): love attained only in sleep.
- **the story praised.** The nymphs praise Polia's "eloquence, her noble origin, and the happy exit of her love" — the
  frame-audience's verdict: the double narration (both lovers' tales) is complete and admired. Book II's whole apparatus
  (Polia telling, the nymphs hearing) closes.
- **the return to the dance at the fount.** The nymphs resume their round-dance at the sacred fountain (of Venus, ch. XXIII):
  the dream returns to its blissful setting for its last moments. The lovers alone, and the dream's end, follow (pp. 463–464).
