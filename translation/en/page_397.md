# Page 397 — Chapter XXVI

For which thing — not moved a little from my fierce purpose, presentially seeing him expired, no other sign of compassion
for him having, save to give heed, cautiously, to flee, and to leave him to whatever might befall him — but, boldly taking
him (O immanity, more than ferine!) by his cold feet, illicitly wicked, into a corner of the temple (by me nefariously
impiated and polluted), I, with all my power, drawing (him), left (him); and, relinquished without other funeral-anointing,
I sought secretly to flee. Whence, much around me looking, with the eyes I wandered; nor seeing, nor hearing anyone, out
of the Holy Basilica I escaped; by devious streets, with great fatigue of soul, distressed, I contrived thence to depart
afar — and, promptly and swift (perhaps more than Hippa), to my palace, as a conscious malefactress, to return.

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The end of **chapter XXVI**: Polia, unmoved even by his death, **drags the dead Poliphilo by his
cold feet into a corner of the temple** and, leaving him "without other funeral-rite," flees secretly through the back
streets to her palace — "a conscious malefactress." All secure; the completion of her crime.

- **The disposal of the body — her deepest guilt.** Not content with letting him die, Polia hides the corpse ("by his
  cold feet… into a corner") and abandons it unburied — a desecration (the temple "by me polluted"). She names it herself:
  "O immanity, more than ferine," "a conscious malefactress." This is the act she has been building to confess since the
  book's start (p. 382, "swinish ferocity"). It is also why she must later be punished and converted — the crime that the
  Venus-cult, and the dream, expiate.
- **the pollution of the temple.** By leaving a corpse in Diana's sanctuary, Polia violates the very chastity-cult she
  vowed herself to — her cruelty in Diana's name defiles Diana's house. The irony seals the moral: rigid chastity, pushed
  to cruelty, becomes impiety. Book II's argument against the "colds of Diana" (cf. p. 229) is dramatised.
- **"a conscious malefactress" (conscia malefica).** Polia's own verdict on herself, fleeing like a criminal. Book II is
  her trial and repentance; here is the crime, fully owned. Her punishment (a vision of the torture of cruel lovers) and
  her conversion follow in the chapters ahead — the female mirror of Poliphilo's ordeal.
- **Chapter XXVI complete.** The dark centre of Polia's narration — plague, vow, rejection, the lover's death, and the
  guilty flight — is now translated. The next chapters bring her dream-vision of Cupid's punishment of the cruel, and her
  turn to love.
