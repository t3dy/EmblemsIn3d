# Page 285 — Chapter XX

The divine Steersman I, with reverent and decent regards, willingly willing him, with every possible
effort to contemplate — the disproportionate object, my feeble sight fittingly did not suffer. But,
conniving with my cheeks (half-closing my eyes), in this manner somewhat the divine boy I manifoldly
comprehended. Sometimes he appeared to me of twin aspect; sometimes of triple; and yet, sometimes, he
showed himself with infinite effigies. Who, with Polia, made our itinerary happy, blessed, and glorious.
And by this way the amorous and prow-standing Cupid, fanning the sacred feathers of his perpetual wings —
in which Canens, lover of Picus, solaced herself — shone more than refined gold, of various and most-
pleasant colouring, rotating in a circle above the little waves. More beautiful and more pleasing than
the crystalline triangular column of Euclid demonstrates, approximated to the eyes.

Now the nautical Nymphs gave beginning — with a most-sweet note, and with a heavenly intonation, from the
human wholly deviated, and beyond belief, with reasoned song-craft — to sing, and to trill a most-sweet
concord, with consonant voice and melody. Insomuch that rightly I doubted I should shatter from excessive
sweetness, because almost from its place removed I felt the resulting and wounded heart, and of sweetness
it seemed to me, reasonably, that it should exile itself through my lips. And they, following, with
vibrating little tongues, in the sonorous palate broke, crisping, doubling, and tripling into one the
briefest little chromatics, or tailed notelets — and, first intercepted, two by two; then three and three;
then by four; last, all six — the rosy little lips, tremulous, moderately opening them, and graciously
joining them, they proffered the modulating spirits, with mosaic proportion, with mellifluous voice, in
the warm heart syncopated, and with weary utterance of love. A voice sighing to the places, most-sweetly
throating — enough to put into oblivion the natural need, and to neglect it — with stringed instruments
they sang the sweetnesses and qualities of love: the facetious thefts of supernal Jove, the solacing
warmths of most-holy Erothea (Venus), the lasciviousnesses of festive Bacchus, the fecundities of the
foster-mother and golden Ceres, the savoury fruits of Hymenaeus — with versifying manner expressing, and
rhythmically proffering, *melos emmetron* (metred song).

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Cupid the multiform steersman (seen double, triple, infinite) at the prow, his
rainbow wings; then **the nymphs' song at the oars** — a virtuoso six-voice chromatic melody, its
technique described note by note, singing the loves of Jove, Venus, Bacchus, Ceres and Hymenaeus, and
surpassing Orpheus. All secure; a page about music, and Colonna's most technical description of singing.

- **The song at the oars — the chapter's heart.** With the oars rested, the six nymphs sing a "heavenly"
  chromatic part-song (entering two by two, then three, then four, then all six), on the theme of the loves
  of the gods, to stringed accompaniment. Poliphilo nearly "shatters from sweetness." This is the music of
  the crossing to Cythera — the world's approach could carry a six-voice vocal texture. Polia answers it on
  pp. 286–289.
- **the multiform Cupid.** The god at the prow appears "twin, triple, infinite" as Poliphilo half-closes
  his eyes against the "disproportionate object" — Love cannot be seen whole. His rainbow wings "in which
  Canens solaced herself" recur (Canens, Picus's singer-wife who dissolved into song — a music-myth fit for
  this chapter).
- **surpassing Orpheus** — the nymphs' song exceeds even Orpheus's, which drew Eurydice from the dead: the
  surpassing-topos applied to music (cf. Apollo and Arion, p. 343). Music is the chapter's whole subject,
  so its superlative is the singer of the underworld.
- **"Erothea"** — a cult-name for Venus (love-goddess); the song's pantheon is the powers of generation:
  Jove, Venus, Bacchus, Ceres, Hymenaeus — desire, love, wine, harvest, marriage.
