Whereupon, thus briskly and voluptuously at leisure, with nymphish converse for a

said: O Polia, our consort and partner fellow-servant of the goddess we
cultivate, the loveliness of your elegant aspect and your conspicuous and notable
form, and your noble and illustrious virtues and excellent well-mannered mind,
and your especial and incomparable beauty, make us not undeservedly eager to
learn the cause of your happy loves, and the original beginning of your excellent
and generous lineage — which we judge to be notable and sublime and of illustrious
origin. For we have gathered you to be of probity, of wit, and not meanly learned
in letters; distinguished in shrewdness, with especial grace in your virgin
gestures, of exceptional form; of surpassing beauty, with most sweet loveliness,
and most excellent in modesty, and worthy in the highest degree of honour.
Because your most admirable countenance and heavenly likeness, extremely beautiful
and genial, is not wholly earthly, but rather appears in every way, by open
token, to have more of the divine. Therefore tell us — grateful, indeed most
grateful — of the troublesome torments and the impudent disdains, through discord
and unequal disposition, and how at times you feigned yourself deaf, not to hear
the anxious prayers of importunate and passionate lovers; and how, the two not
continually seeing one another, the sad and desiring heart feeds only on a
consoling persuasion, proceeding from composed imagination and delightful
figments, such as they would wish and most greatly desire, vainly satisfying
themselves with sweet little sighs and feigned solace and pleasure. And in such
commendable leisure, intent on the act, our quiet and solacing sitting will not
grow wearisome.

## Notes

**Confidence: high.** This is a formal set speech — the nymph Polyorimene's
request that Polia tell her story — and it is clear, courtly and free of the
botanical and mythological thickets of the surrounding pages.

- **The pivot of the whole book.** Polyorimene's speech is the doorway from Book I
  (Poliphilo's dream of the journey) into Book II (Polia's own first-person
  account). She asks for exactly what Book II delivers: Polia's lineage, and the
  story of her cruelty and disdain toward Poliphilo — "how you feigned yourself
  deaf" to a lover's prayers. The nymphs frame Polia's beauty as "not wholly
  earthly… more of the divine," setting her up as the worthy object the second
  book will justify.
- **"the sad and desiring heart feeds only on a consoling persuasion"** — a small,
  precise piece of the book's psychology of unrequited love: the lover sustained
  by imagined satisfactions, "composed imagination and delightful figments." It
  is the theme Book II will dramatise from the woman's side.
- **Polyorimene** — another Colonna coinage (πολυ‑, many; the second element
  perhaps ὄρος/ὁρίζω), left as a name.
