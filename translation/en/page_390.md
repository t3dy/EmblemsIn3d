rough ruins I saw, invading, threatening. And for so much, like a damned man awaiting the inevitable capital blow, almost
grieves not (any more), I gave and consigned my miserable life into the hand of the terrific Sisters (Fates); distempered,
and more furious some time, by rabid and stimulating love, than were Atys and Pentheus attained by the sisters and by the
wretched Agave — because I saw myself relinquished, like Achaemenides left by Ulysses between Scylla and Charybdis."

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Polia recounts **Poliphilo's declaration of love** in the temple — his physical collapse
(paling, cold, half-dead) and his plea: she is "my goddess, my heart, my life," his sole hope; better to die for her than
live without her love. All secure; a long rhetorical love-speech in Polia's retelling, thick with mythic exempla
(Eteocles/Polynices, Atys, Pentheus, Achaemenides).

- **The lover's plea — heard from the cruel side.** Book I gave Poliphilo's love as worship; here Polia reports the very
  words of his declaration, and her own **half-dead, unmoved** watching of it. The same speech is at once a moving plea
  (his) and, in her framing, a "molesting" importunity she will deride (p. 392). The double narration's power: we hear
  the love and the refusal in one voice.
- **"better to die for thee than live without thy love."** The core of his plea — death preferred to loveless life — is
  the Polyandrion's own logic (the love-suicides, ch. XIX) turned prophetic: Poliphilo is on the verge of becoming one of
  those dead. Polia, consecrated to Diana, watches him approach it. The stakes of her cruelty are mortal.
- **the exempla of unbroken hatred** — Eteocles and Polynices (whose corpses' flames split apart in mutual hatred),
  Hypsipyle, Orestes/Clytemnestra: Polia measures her *own* hardness against the cruellest myths. Her self-accusation
  (p. 382) is literal — she casts herself as more savage than Orestes. Book II is her confession, and she does not spare
  herself.
- **"sweetest lanista of my soul"** — Poliphilo calls her the *trainer* (gladiator-master) of his soul: she drills him in
  suffering. The captivity-and-combat imagery of love (cf. the triumph, p. 341) in his own mouth.
