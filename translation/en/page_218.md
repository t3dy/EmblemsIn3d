the starry eyes some few little pearls in the form of little tears (fell); insomuch that, by her blandishing
speech, and by the salivous and most-pleasing kiss, from head to feet all inflamed I was altered, melting
myself into sweetest and amorous tears, and wholly lost myself. And likewise the sacrificing Priestess, with
the others standing by — moved by singular sweetness — could not contain themselves from little tears and
sweet little sighs.

## Notes

**Confidence: high.** The **climax of Book I's later half**: Polia reveals herself fully — *"I am, without
doubt, that Polia of thine whom thou so lovest"* — declares her reciprocal love in a rush of "Behold me…"
(*Eccome*) clauses, and seals it with a kiss (the *arra*, love's pledge); Poliphilo dissolves in tears, and
even the priestess and virgins weep for sweetness. All secure; the emotional summit of the temple sequence.

- **Polia's self-revelation and reciprocation.** After a whole book of the beloved withheld and un-named,
  Polia declares herself and returns Poliphilo's love in kind — "so worthy a love (should) not be immune from…
  equivalent reciprocation." Her anaphora of *Eccome* ("Behold me — the end of thy sighs… a present remedy…
  thy consort… ready to die for thee") is the answer to every plaint since the crossing (p. 288) and the
  fulfilment of the fidelity-thread the Cythera chapters kept alive. This is the passage the world's temple-
  station and the visual-novel's romance beat exist to deliver.
- **the *arra*-kiss.** The lovers' first kiss is sealed as a legal *pledge* (arra — earnest-money of a
  contract): love formalised as a betrothal, in the temple, before witnesses. Polia's pearl-tears and
  Poliphilo's dissolution "from head to feet" are the book's most direct emotional writing, its Latinate
  ornament briefly giving way to feeling.
- **the whole company weeps** — the priestess and virgins, moved, weep too: the private union is witnessed and
  blessed. The temple of Venus Physizoa has done its work — desire (Trahit sua quemque voluptas, p. 214)
  brought to sanctioned fruition.
- **Chapter XVII nears its end.** A few pages remain (219–224) before ch. XVIII; the union achieved here is the
  hinge of Poliphilo's story, and the emotional counterpart to Cythera's later triumph.
