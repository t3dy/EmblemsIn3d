# Page 259 — Chapter XIX

From here departing, more avidly lustrating through the heaped ruins, I found another **tetragonal altar**, which on
its flat had a base without plinth — that is, a gula (cyma), then a little fascia, and then a torus. Beyond this it
was levelled. On which levelling sat a plinth, or little slab, that from angle to angle made a sinuous (curve), as
one of its square figure incurving little by little; the projection of which angles did not exceed the circumference
of the torus, or torus, subjacent. Upon which abacus-plinth lay a circular (base) of a vase; the circumstance of
which did not pass the outlet of the angles of the subjacent plinth. Which vase dilated itself, in the opening, as
much as was the extreme of the lowest of the underlying gula of the base. The rim, or lip, of which — keeping its
thickness — tended (in) an inflection, into itself inverting. On the altar I saw such an epigram:

> **INFER(IS) D(IIS) DEAB(US)Q(UE).**
> **C. VIBIUS ADULESCENS,**
> **INTEMPERATO AMORE**
> **PERCITUS PUTILLIAE**
> **SEX(TI) PUELLAE GRATISS(IMAE),**
> **QUOD ALTERI ULTRO**
> **TRADITA (EST), NON SUSTINENS,**
> **CRUENTO GLADIO**
> **SIBIMET MORTEM**
> **CONSCIVIT. VIX(IT) ANN(OS)**
> **XIX, M(ENSES) II, D(IES) IX; HORAS**
> **SCIT NEMO.**
>
> "To the infernal gods and goddesses. Gaius Vibius, a youth, stirred by intemperate love for Putillia, most-pleasing
> girl of Sextus — because she was willingly given over to another, not enduring it, with a bloody sword took death
> upon himself. He lived 19 years, 2 months, 9 days; the hours, no one knows."

Thence departing, I found a noble fragment of best porphyry, with two equine heads carved, from which came out an
intricate binding — two branches of myrtle, in-transversed and pendent, retaining. In the middle of the transverse
they were knotted with a tight shoot of wondrous make. Between the one and the other (horse-)bone, over the myrtle
fronds, I saw, of most-beautiful Ionic majuscules, such writing (the rest of the writing, with the stone, destroyed).

## Notes

**Confidence: high** (the Vibius epitaph is secure). After the Leontia-and-Lollius romance, a plainer **suicide-
epitaph**: **Gaius Vibius**, a youth of 19, killed himself with the sword when Putillia (whom he loved) "was willingly
given over to another" — dead of jealous, "intemperate" love. Then a porphyry fragment with horse-heads and myrtle,
its Ionic-lettered epitaph broken away. All secure.

- **Vibius — love's excess, a young man's suicide.** The counterpart to Nevia (p. 256): where she was refused, Vibius
  was *supplanted* (Putillia gave herself to another), and, "not enduring it," killed himself at nineteen. One of the
  fire-damned (excess of love, p. 250). His precise age — "19 years, 2 months, 9 days; the hours no one knows" — is a
  poignant, authentically Roman touch (the exact lifespan, the unknowable hour). A ready epitaph for the world's tombs.
- **the tally of the Polyandrion's dead.** The chapter is building a taxonomy of love's deaths: Laodia (frigid suicide),
  Nevia (refused, wasted away), Leontia & Lollius (faithful, killed by fortune), Vibius (jealous suicide). Each tomb a
  different way to die of love — the full casualty-list the Polyandrion memorialises. The world/novel can draw its
  polyandrion epitaphs from this range.
- **"the hours, no one knows."** The one thing not recorded is the hour of death — a quietly devastating close: the
  life measured to the day, the moment of dying left blank. Colonna's epigraphic art at its most economical.
- **horse-heads and myrtle** — the porphyry fragment pairs horses (death's swiftness, or the sea-Neptune of the
  shipwreck?) with myrtle (Venus's tree): love and death yoked, the Polyandrion's constant emblem-pairing.
