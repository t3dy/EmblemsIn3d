little box, with solemn religion, she preceding, and Polia with the others venerating following, to the lip
of the sacred cistern (opened), in order they came.

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The purification-sequence before the great prayer: the priestess **exorcises** all
impediments to love, **asperges** the company with sanctified rue dipped in Polia's rose-water, immerses the
myrtle, rue, dove-feathers and the ash-box in the cistern with sung hymns, then strikes the altar thrice and
begins the **oration to Venus in the vernacular** ("in our tongue"). All secure.

- **The rite gathers into the cistern.** Every element of the sacrifice — branches, feathers, the charactered
  ashes — is immersed in the sacred well (the same cistern where the torch was quenched, p. 216): the water is
  the rite's receiving vessel, love's element. The ash-box "buried" in the water is the sacrifice consigned to
  Venus.
- **the shift to the vernacular.** The earlier readings were in Etruscan (p. 216) and Latin/Greek (the
  mottoes, p. 214); the great prayer to Venus is spoken **"in our tongue" (Italian)** — Colonna marks it as
  the one directly-intelligible utterance, the heart's petition in the living language. It runs pp. 229–231,
  the book's fullest prayer to the goddess.
- **rue and hyssop** — the purifying herbs (rue for aspersion, hyssop for the ash-brush): the *materia* of
  ritual cleansing, precisely chosen. Colonna's liturgy is built from real cult-plants (cf. the incense,
  p. 225).
- The prayer itself begins on p. 229 with the invocation "O most-holy Erothea, pious mother."
