subtly expressed.

There, cavern-wise, was painted a vault infuscated of thick gloom, showing a huge and sad, and frightful cavern,
all carious, like a many-holed or fistulous pumice; which, on the left side toward the middle part, near a most-
rough, pathless, ferruginous and craggy rock, terminated. In which was seen a gap of concavity in front; and in

## Notes

**Confidence: medium** (high for the altar-inscription). The crypt's second altar bears the dedication **to Pluto,
Proserpina and Cerberus** (the infernal triad); the smoke of sacrifice exits an open oculus "by the Egyptian
rite"; then, returning above, Poliphilo finds in a surviving tribune a **mosaic painting of Hell** — a pumice-
carved cavern with an **iron bridge, white-hot at one end and freezing at the other**, over fire and ice. All
secure; the infernal painting begins (it fills pp. 249–250).

- **The infernal dedication.** The crypt-altar is inscribed to **Pluto, Proserpina and Cerberus** — the tomb-
  temple's true patrons (the Polyandrion is "dedicated to Pluto," p. 236). A ready inscription for the world's
  crypt, pairing with the gate's *Cadaveribus amore furentium* (p. 246).
- **the open oculus.** Colonna explains the ciborium's grated eye (p. 246) as a smoke-vent "by the Egyptian rite"
  — the crypt's furnace-smoke rises through the porphyry cupola and out the open roof. The engineering-mind again
  (cf. the temple's drainage, p. 202), here for a crematorium.
- **the mosaic of Hell.** The surviving vault-mosaic depicts a Dantesque underworld: a pumice cavern, an iron
  bridge half-fire half-ice, over a burning and a frozen lake. It is the Polyandrion's central image — the fate of
  those who died of love, painted overhead. A spectacular, buildable ceiling for the world's polyandrion (a
  fire-and-ice Hell-mosaic in the surviving tribune). It continues on pp. 249–250.
- **the altar "found by Valesius at Tarentum"** — the legendary subterranean altar of Dis and Proserpina at the
  Tarentum in the Campus Martius (origin of the Secular Games): Colonna's learned comparison places this crypt-
  altar in real Roman antiquarian lore.
