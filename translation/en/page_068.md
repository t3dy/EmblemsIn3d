# Page 68 — Chapter VII

POLIPHILO TELLS OF THE KINDNESS OF THE COUNTRY HE HAD FOUND AND ENTERED, IN WHICH, WANDERING, HE FOUND
AN EXQUISITE AND MOST NOTABLE FOUNTAIN; AND HOW HE SAW FIVE GRACEFUL DAMSELS COMING TOWARDS HIM, AND
HOW THEY, MUCH MARVELLING AT HIS COMING THERE, MERCIFULLY REASSURED HIM AND INVITED HIM TO SHARE THEIR
PLEASURES.

HAVING COME OUT OF THAT HORRENDOUS pit, and from those inner darknesses and that place almost of the
dead (for all that the holy and consecrated Aphrodisium was there), into the most desired light and
friendly air, and being come into this most welcome place, I turned back to look at where my going-out
had been. And where my life — a life I had never valued — I saw at that point troubled and in peril.

I looked upon a mountain of no great size with a moderate slope, all wooded with greenest and pliant
fronds: with acorn-bearing Oaks, Beeches, Ilexes, Turkey-oaks, Italian oaks, Cork-oaks, and the two
Holm-oaks — the one Smilax, or Holly, or Acilon. Then, towards the level, it was thick with
cornel-trees, with hazels, with sweet-smelling and flower-bearing privets, and with a fragrant
whitening flower; two-coloured Naxian, reddening in the north aspect and whitening in the south;
Hornbeams and Ashes, and the like in such aspect, with sprouting saplings.

Wrapped in greening and climbing Honeysuckle and in winding hops, they gave a cool and shadowy shade.
Beneath which was the Cyclamen, harmful to Lucina, and the jagged Polypody, and the Trientale
Hart's-tongue, or Spleenwort; and both the Hellebores named from the shepherd; and the three-leaved
tora, or triangularis; and the Groundsel, and many other shade-loving herbs, and Woodland trees, some
without and some with flowering — a place none the less broken and rough, and closely shut about with
trees.

The opening, then, by which I came out of those most hidden lairs, was somewhat high in the mountain,
all overgrown with shrubs. And so far as I could conjecture, it was built over against the other
mentioned before; and I understood that this too had been a wonderful working, a postern, and that
one ancient. But envious and rivalling antiquity, and the narrowness of the approach, and above all
