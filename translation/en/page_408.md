# Page 408 — Chapter XXVII

— I awoke, saying nothing, save, most-sadly sighing, to say: "Alas, alas me! Wah, wah me!" — finding myself all
concussed, and full of grave languors. And my shattered and contaminated breast, more, with a frequented and importunate
palpitation, was struck by the lively and terrified heart, than the solicitous Vulcan, fabricating the tremendous
lightnings of the thundering and fulminating Jove, strikes (the anvil). And, of irrigating tears, I had already
made-wet the candid linens; and the most-subtle chemise, adhering to the girlish little belly, moist, was, of my hair,
inelegant (disordered); with the soul afflicted, in dolorous torment and lamentous, by a mortiferous thought
circumvented and invaded. Nor could I, thereby, by any way, use my juvenile and scanty forces; but, abandoned and
intermitted by the mortified members, wholly prostrated, of great lassitude, more dead than alive, and almost bedridden
— then, not judging life gracious, imbecile and destitute, and deplored.

And then, somewhat after — when the benign Nurse of mine, with many blandishments adulating, and sweet entreaties, and
female suasions (seeing me in these such-made anguishes), sweetly persuaded me to relief, and to receive comfort and
consolation — (asking) what it might be (she ignorant), and, in that point, most-highly desirous, and most-eager to
understand what I felt, and, with anxious care, questing, and what so great a mania wished to say:

And, holding me in her senile embraces and arms, she likewise grieved of my ill, and un-understood accident; and, with
me bitterly weeping, after long and caressing blandishments, and my protracted anguishes and affliction — the soul,
somewhat, pusillanimously, in me revoked — with more fright tremulous than was that of supreme Jove (when that highest
father, for the Giants, personated himself in a hirsute Ram) — the horrend vision, badly, and with sighs syncopating, I
narrated to her, babbling; and the fortuitous case of the day before, and that, from the violated Temple returning, it
befell, seriously I told her — **except that, of the undue and importunate death of Poliphilo, as I suspected, not the
least I spoke to her; but well (I told her) that I had shown myself, to Love, insipid and foolish (blitea)**. No sooner,
then, had I recounted this, than she — ponderingly, and with senile expertise — the cause suspected; piously
refreshing, with many suasive blandishments, my mind, somewhat settled and a little tranquillised, she remade;
proffering herself, of all my grave and molesting languors, to be the true remedy-maker, if I, to her weighed and
salutiferous admonitions yielding, should present myself observant. And there, lifted of every other thought, and, of
extraneous cogitation, loosed — chiefly and solely, to her faithful and polished counsels a most-following imitatrix,

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The end of **chapter XXVII**: Polia wakes drenched, "more dead than alive," heart hammering; her
nurse comforts her, and Polia tells her the whole vision and the day's events — **but hides Poliphilo's death**, confessing
only that she had been "insipid and foolish to Love"; the nurse, "with senile expertise, suspects the cause," promises to
be her "true remedy" if she obeys, and Polia submits as a "most-following, most-mild, disciplinable" pupil. All secure;
the turn toward counsel and conversion.

- **Polia's half-confession.** Tellingly, she tells the nurse everything *except* that she killed Poliphilo — she admits
  only being "foolish to Love" (*blitea*). Her remorse is still incomplete: she can confess coldness, not yet the death.
  Full remorse for Poliphilo comes later; here she takes the first step, admitting her *sin against Love* (not yet against
  the man). Colonna traces the conversion in exact degrees.
- **the nurse as physician of the soul.** The nurse "suspects the cause" (love denied) and offers herself as "the true
  remedy-maker" if Polia obeys — the wise-counsellor who will, in the next chapter, argue Polia out of her cruelty and
  toward love. Polia's submission ("a most-following, most-mild, disciplinable" pupil) is the first act of her new
  humility — the proud, cruel girl now teachable. The counsel itself fills chapter XXVIII.
- **the first warmth received and returned.** The nurse weeps *with* her, holds her, and Polia yields — the bond of love
  (maternal here) that begins to melt the heart Poliphilo's eros could not. Book II's insight: the cold heart is opened
  first by being *loved* (by the nurse), then learns to love (Poliphilo). Grace before eros.
- **Chapter XXVII complete.** The whirlwind, the vision of Cupid's punishment, the nightmare of the executioners, and
  the nurse's rescue — the terror that breaks Polia — are translated. The nurse's persuasion (ch. XXVIII) turns her toward
  love.
