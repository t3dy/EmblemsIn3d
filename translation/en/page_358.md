# Page 358 — Chapter XXIII opens

*(the last line of the preceding chapter)* …by which we were brought before the
holy and sacred Cytherean fountain.

---

**Poliphilo describes the marvellous artifice of the Venerean fountain standing
at the centre of the theatre's floor, and how the little curtain was torn away;
and he saw the divine Mother in her majesty, and how she imposed silence upon
the singing nymphs, of whom she assigned three apiece to Polia and to him.
Afterwards Cupid wounded them both, and the goddess sprinkled them with the
water of the fountain, and Poliphilo was clothed again. Last of all, Mars
coming, they obtained leave and departed.**

With the most fitting reverence and the highest honour, the graceful Polia and I
knelt devotedly before the mysterious fountain of the divine mother, and I felt
an imperceptible sweetness spreading through me and troubling me, so that in that
moment I did not know what to do. For what with the delightful place, incredibly
pleasing and beyond all belief lovely in its greenness and spring adornment; and
the little birds coursing through the purest air and chattering as they flew
among the new leaves, most joyfully, to the outward senses; and the hearing of
those most beautiful nymphs singing their melodies together with their unfamiliar
instruments; and the sight of their divine gestures and most modest movements,
which drove me ardently toward the extremity of pleasure; and the careful,
curious weighing of a fabric of such dignity of conception and such elegant
arrangement; and the greedy drinking-in of a fragrance I had never known — by
immortal Jove, I truly did not know to which of my senses I should firmly and
steadily apply the object of my attention, distracted as it was by so many
different delights, by so excessive and happy a solace and voluptuous pleasing;
and I accused myself of not knowing.

And all these beautiful and most sweet things offered me an enticement so much
the more gracious and desirable, in that I knew that Polia — heavenly Polia — was
placidly taking her share of the delight in them.

In this place, then, and marvelling too at the novelty and excellence of this
astonishing fountain.

---

## Notes

- **`Eutrapela`** — Greek εὐτράπελος, "graceful, ready in speech, well-turned."
  Colonna uses it as an epithet for Polia rather than a name. Rendered "the
  graceful Polia."
- **`Uranothia`** — a Colonna coinage from οὐρανός, "heaven." Rendered "heavenly
  Polia," to keep the pattern of him renaming her with a new compound each time
  he looks at her.
- **The chapter argument** is set in full capitals in the original, as the running
  argument of every chapter is. Kept as a bold heading rather than shouted.
- The final sentence is deliberately unfinished: the argument runs straight on
  into the next page's description of the fountain. Left hanging, as the original
  leaves it.
- **`per il purissimo aere discorrendo`** — *discorrere* here in its older sense
  of running or coursing through, not conversing.
