> SCARCE HAD POLIPHILO TERMINATED HIS NARRATION, THAN POLIA TELLS HIM OF HER VEHEMENT LOVE, INTIMATELY WOUNDED, AND (THAT
> SHE IS) MOST-AVID TO LOVE HIM, WITH VARIOUS EXEMPLIFICATIONS. AND, TO MANIFEST HER URGENT AFFECT, SHE GAVE HIM A MOST-

"Equally, I know not, with what remuneration, most-loving my Poliphilo — save with sincere faith, and with veracious and
ardent love, and with sweet and choice pity, conveniently supplying (it) — to recompense the cruel injury inflicted (on
thee); with equal vicissitude, and with no less pity, remunerated, of the Hyades. For, no less does thy honest petition
commove and provoke me, than thy presence, for me languishing — from hour to hour successively, judging thereby, without
fail most-certain, to be the effect. Not otherwise tormenting appearing to me than Hector, slain, dragged through the
flying dust, with great runnels of fuming blood, and with the blond hair cruented, and with the face besmeared and
dusty, before the tear-bearing eyes of the dolorous and most-beloved Andromache. O my heart! O sole my good! O sweet
hope! — thy tormented and transfixed heart being saddened, by my ferocity of soul perverse, savage, dire and impious,
and, of error deceived, so protracted a time embittered — drawing the woeful life in unceasing weepings and plaints —
and, at present, full and nautically stuffed of tribulous insults of love, to my tear-weeping eyes represented. And, to
wish to follow the greatness of thy noble and worthy soul, and, of excellence of love fervidly adorned — which now shall
not find deaf and inane audience — for this, in a little hour, thou shalt see put a mode, and salubrious end, to thy
griefs. For, a blind desire having, licitly, domesticated itself, from thy edacious eyes, of my heart, in the most-prompt
breast — I too find myself not immune, nor void, but, participant, I commune with thy ills. For which thing, I intend not
to spare my life (which lies in thy arbitrament and will), and my florid and unhurt girlhood, to thy ardent desires and
gracious vows to defer (yield). And, not using now that which, before, I reasonably ought (i.e. not persisting in my
refusal), I could easily incur the inevitable ires of my lord Cupid — where —"

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** **Polia's answering declaration of love** (chapter XXXI): she will repay the cruelty she did him
"with sincere faith and ardent love"; his suffering (like Hector dragged before Andromache) moves her; she now "communes
with his ills," will not spare "my life… my florid girlhood" to his desires, and would herself "incur Cupid's ire" if she
refused. All secure; her formal reciprocation.

- **The reciprocation — love returned in kind.** Polia answers Poliphilo's petition (that she be made to love him) with her
  own declaration: she loves him, will yield "my life, my girlhood" to him, and repays "the cruel injury" she did with
  "equal vicissitude." The "dissimilitude" he asked the priestess to equalise (p. 430) she now equalises herself — the two
  loves made coequal, two hearts one. Book II's reunion is complete in her words.
- **Hector and Andromache.** She figures Poliphilo's love-tormented state as Hector's corpse dragged before the weeping
  Andromache — casting herself as the grieving wife watching her beloved destroyed. The image of a wife mourning a
  slaughtered husband is her acknowledgement that she *killed* him (chs. XXVI, XXIX); her love is inseparable from her
  remorse.
- **fear turned to love's servant.** She notes she would now "incur Cupid's ire" if she *refused* — the same god whose
  wrath the visions threatened for her cruelty now compels her love. The conversion is sealed: Cupid's power, once her
  terror, is now her ally. Refusing love, not loving, is the punishable sin.
- Her vow and the kiss-pledge complete the declaration on pp. 433+.
