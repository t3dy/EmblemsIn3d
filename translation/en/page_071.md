Then the whole space enclosed between the little columns, gorge-moulding and beam, hollow and
excavated, held an elegant Nymph carved within. And beneath the Cyma was the other quarter, which at
the footing waved with Tori, Torus-moulding and Scotias, and a Plinth.

Which most beautiful Nymph lay sleeping comfortably upon an outspread cloth. And beneath her head it
was beautifully stuffed and folded into a cushioned mound. And a part of the said cloth was then most
aptly brought to cover that which fittingly ought to stay hidden. Lying upon her right side, the arm
beneath drawn back, with the loosed hand she idly propped her head beneath the cheek. And the other
arm, free and without office, stretched itself over the left loin, coming down open to the middle of
the fleshy thigh.

From the little buds — as of young girls — of her breasts there gushed a thread of most fresh water
from the right; and from the left it rose hot. The fall of both fell into a porphyry vessel, with two
receivers joined together in one solid piece, set six feet apart and distant from the Nymph, before
this fountain, upon a stone pavement fitly placed.

Between the one receiver and the other was a little channel set in, in which the waters met, their
edges cut in the middle of the one and the other receiver, where the waters made their meeting. Which
waters, mingled then, flowed away fallen into a water-furrow, or little rill. Whereupon the one being
tempered by the other, they made every greenness sprout; the hot one leaping so high that it did not
hurtfully hinder the other for him who set his lips to the right breast to suck and to drink, nor at
the crossing.

Now this most notable statue the craftsman expressed so exactly that I should truly doubt whether
Praxiteles had carved such a Venus — for which Nicomedes, king of the Cnidians, offered (so fame
flies) all the substance of his people. And so comelily beautiful did he express her that men, burning
in sacrilegious longing for her, violated the image. But so far as I could rightly value it, I judged
that such an image was never so perfectly feigned by chisel or graver; so that I almost reasonably
suspected that in this place she had been turned to stone from the living, and so petrified.
