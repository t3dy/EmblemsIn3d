*Poliphilo in this place aforesaid saw the four triumphing six-horse cars, all of various stones and of
most precious jewels. With the mingled multitude of blessed young people, greatly worshipping in praise
of the highest Jove.*

Reasonably, I judge it in no way difficult for the gods above to do; nay, any effect whatever readily
shows itself easily to be done at their will, and in every place, and above everything created. For
which cause they are duly called almighty. Perhaps whoever shall sometimes hear told the marvellous and
stupendous — nay, divine — works, might marvel exceedingly. For art, the rival, strives as much as it
can to imitate natural things. But divine things, without doubt, cannot aptly be feigned or copied by
any created wit and intellect without their help and inspiration. So for such a reason no one ought to
let himself be taken up by any doubt, but quietly taking note let him set in his mind that to the gods
above every making unaccustomed to us is possible — such as I saw thus.

*The description of the first triumph.*

The first of the four wondrous and divine triumphs had its four swift wheels of finest stone of the
greenest Scythian emerald, sparkling with atoms of a coppery colour. The rest, then, of the car I
beheld, astonished, made all of tablets not of Arabian nor of Cyprian, but of iron-sparkling Indian
diamond, defying the hard emery and the steel, victorious over active fire, its scorner and stubborn
foe, but quiet and tameable to the warm blood of a goat, welcome to the magic arts. Which slabs,
divinely worked with a sunken carving in relief, were carved and wonderfully set and enclosed in the
cleanest gold.

In the right-hand tablet I beheld expressed a noble and royal Nymph with many of her own age in a
meadow, crowning the victorious bulls with manifold garlands of flowers. And one clinging to her, made
very particularly familiar with her.
