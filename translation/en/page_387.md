*(Chapter argument — Chapter XXVI:)*

> POLIA, STRUCK BY A PESTILENT DISEASE, VOWED HERSELF TO DIANA; AND, CONSECRATING HERSELF, BY CHANCE POLIPHILO SAW HER
> IN THE TEMPLE. WHERE, ONE DAY AFTER, HE FOUND HER ALONE, PRAYING; TO WHOM, HE NARRATING THE GRIEVOUS PAIN AND THE
> MARTYRDOM THAT, LOVING HER, HE SUSTAINED, AND CALLING FOR MITIGATION — SHE, PERSISTING UNMERCIFUL, SAW HIM SWOON TO
> DEATH; WHENCE, LIKE A MALEFACTRESS, THENCE SHE TOOK SWIFT FLIGHT.

Universally, in those days, a great slaughter of the mortality of humans, and of whatever age, promiscuous — there
being, through the infected air, corrupted by a contagious and internecine pestilent disease, an extreme multitude
dying. And, atrocious terror and fright already come upon the diseased earth, and the men, of terrific mortal (fear)
concussed, finding themselves — each, solicitously, out of their cities, taking flight, to the suburban and rural places
fled. Whereupon, a horrible extermination of people being, it was almost suspected that the fetid Austrine (south)
breaths, from dewy Egypt, had not brought it — when, by superfluous increment of the turbid Nile, in the fields it
generated manifold animals, which then, putrefied, stinking, in its decrement, infected the air; or, that the sacrificer
of Argos had not yet lost the oxen to sacrifice to Juno; and there befall that which came to Aegina; and then Aeacus's
fair desire; and the projection made on Mount Parnassus by Deucalion and Pyrrha. Whence, by my feeble and malign lot, of
a **gland (bubo) in the pudic groin** I felt myself struck — it pleasing, by chance, the highest Gods, for my better
success (outcome). And, the pestilent inguinal invasure being multiplied, gravely it fatigued me.

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The close of chapter XXV (Polia's coldness — she gave Poliphilo "not the least sign of love")
and the opening of **chapter XXVI**: its argument (Polia's plague, her vow to Diana, Poliphilo's declaration in the
temple, her merciless flight), and the onset of the **plague** — a great pestilence, and Polia struck by a **bubo in the
groin** (the Black Death). All secure.

- **The plague — the turn of Polia's fate.** A pestilence (plainly the bubonic plague — the inguinal bubo) devastates
  the city; Polia is struck, and it "pleases the gods, for her better outcome." The disease is the instrument that
  drives her to vow herself to Diana (p. 388) — the hinge of her whole story. Colonna gives it a learned aetiology (the
  Nile's putrefaction, the plague of Aegina) but the detail (the groin-bubo, the flight to the countryside) is the real
  fifteenth-century plague.
- **Polia's coldness, confessed.** She admits she gave Poliphilo "not the least sign of love… such as would not appear
  in hard flint," her mind "totally indisposed" to love. This is the cruelty she promised to reckon with (p. 382),
  stated plainly: not malice at first, but a heart simply "remote to the amorous fires." The plague and the vow will
  harden it into active rejection.
- **the argument's arc.** Chapter XXVI is the dark centre of Polia's story: plague → vow of chastity → Poliphilo's
  temple-declaration → her unmerciful refusal → his swoon → her guilty flight. The consecration to Diana (chastity)
  becomes her justification for cruelty. It is the crisis her later punishment and conversion resolve.
- **"for my better success."** Polia, narrating in hindsight, reads the plague as providential — it led (through the
  vow, the cruelty, the punishment) to her eventual love. Book II is told from the far side of that redemption.
