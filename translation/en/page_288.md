lovers; and temper somewhat your burning torches, and modify your noxious arms, and slacken your death-
bringing bow — for of love I am all racked.

"For which I reasonably argue: that if, in your very self, savage and pitiless, you wounded yourself — what
equability of soul persuades that I should not fear that, without pity, toward me, more harsh and unbridled
and most-savage, you would not show yourself?" And so, exasperated, concitatedly I dared; and with diverse
petitions, and prayers, and fabricated complaints, and feigned satisfactions, I somewhat softened the
strong invasion, and the frequent impulse of the wicked and eyeless love. But for all this my enkindled
heart was not condignly reconciled, nor really satisfied to my disjointed appetite. And that which then I
prayed him: only that he put an end, at least, to my tormenting and long-lasting hoping, with the molesting
expectation of a prison-like issue.

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Polia's tender urging that Poliphilo enjoy the song, the surpassing-comparisons for
how welcome she is (the rivers to burning Troy, the boar's head to Atalanta, the elephant to Hannibal), and
then **Poliphilo's prayer to Cupid** — reminding the god that he too was wounded by love for Psyche, and
begging him for pity and, above all, for an *end to the waiting*. All secure.

- **Poliphilo prays to Cupid for pity — and for an end to deferral.** The theological turn of the chapter:
  he argues that a god who wounded *himself* for Psyche will surely not spare a mortal, and pleads that Love
  "slacken the death-bringing bow" and, most of all, "put an end to my tormenting hope." The pain named here
  is not desire but *delay* — "every hostile love contends toward the expected end." This sets up Polia's
  answer (p. 289): perseverance.
- **the Cupid-and-Psyche back-story** — Colonna has Poliphilo recite the myth (the envious sisters, Cupid
  lamenting on the cypress) as the ground of his plea. The whole Cythera sequence is framed by Psyche's
  story (she presides over the triumph, ch. XXII); here it is invoked as precedent.
- **"perseverance" foreshadowed** — the complaint about deferral is answered, at the chapter's very end (p.
  289), by Polia's maxim that "perseverance alone wears the victor's diadem." The chapter is structured as
  plaint-and-consolation.
- **the surpassing-gifts** — Meleager's boar's-head to Atalanta, Jove's gift to Alcmena, Hannibal's
  elephant: a catalogue of famously-welcome gifts, all less welcome than Polia. The topos, applied to the
  beloved's presence.
