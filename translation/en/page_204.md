Newly, above the topmost flat of each Pilaster, of wondrous make, was set a candelabrum of illustrious
brass (orichalc), the orifice of which was dilated into a shell-form, in which, unceasingly, of
unconsumable material, an inextinguishable fire burned — which neither by winds nor by rain could be
extinguished. Which marvellous candelabra, of a proportioned and matching tallness, were seen, fittingly
handled (with handles).

From the one and the other handle of which, marvellously, a festoon hung, ensnared and un-knotted in
many parts, retaining itself with most-subtle artifice, of fronds, and flowers, and diversified fruits,
with due pregnancy curving in the middle, and thonged, and passably (openly) excavated. Upon the little-
basket-like binding of the thongs, on the middle Tube, brooded a lively and inane (hollow) Eagle,
grasped-fast, with wings outspread, in the area to the sight showing conspicuously the perfection of the
natural — of the material of the candelabra, with a ballast weight set under, all hollow and cast of most-
subtle surface. Foliage, hollow fruits, and florets, and the other works, subtly reduced. The soffit of
the inner curved-vault, between the columnation and the outer wall, of wormwork, of gilt tessellation
with graphic (design) of most-explicated art, with concurrent colouring, was depicted.

Of the height treating: it is not completable to have only concluded this universal rule — that a round
temple raises itself as much as is its diameter. But it concurs, regularly, to find the height of the
binding above the peristyle (that is, of the topmost line of the cornice): for, the line deduced from the
centre to the circumference of the first circuit, so much presents that height. Then, all the diameter
divided into six divisions, four of those rectified will likewise give the last surface of the upper
binding.

The rule of the descent of the roof ought not to be neglected: one takes the intercapace from wall to
wall, where the ridge-slip ought to be placed, and reduced into two perfect squarings as much as they can
come; and, the diagonal extended, cutting the line, discriminating the two squares — thence, beautifully,
the slope is exacted.

Universally, the Symmetry of this wondrous fabric, by the outstanding Architect elegantly disposed, with
consentaneous bindings, inner and outer, congruous. Much more diffusely the regulation could, to all
conveniences, to the corollaries of the solid, be manifested — and, by those figures of the area, to find
the Height of walls, which here stood straight, as best one could do without obliquity, or straightness,

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The **inextinguishable-fire candelabra** on the pilaster-tops (shell-mouthed,
festoon-hung, each with a hollow bronze eagle), the gilt-mosaic soffit, and then the **proportional rules
of the temple** (round temple: height = diameter; entablature-height by dividing the diameter in six and
rectifying four; roof-slope by the diagonal of two squares). All secure; the rules are a genuine
architect's method.

- **The eternal flames.** Each pilaster is crowned by a brass candelabrum with a shell-shaped mouth
  holding an "inextinguishable fire" that neither wind nor rain can quench — a ring of eternal flames
  around the temple's exterior. A striking, renderable feature: the world's temple can carry perpetual
  fire-bowls on its piers.
- **the hollow bronze eagles** — each candelabrum's festoon is centred on a cast eagle "showing the
  perfection of the natural," hollow and ballasted: the same casting-virtuosity as the vine-dome (p. 201).
- **the temple's proportional canon, stated.** Colonna gives the design rules explicitly: (1) a round
  temple's height equals its diameter; (2) the entablature height is found from the radius and a six-part
  division; (3) the roof-pitch from the diagonal of a double square. These are exactly the kind of ratios
  the world's temple should be built to — Colonna hands them over.
- **"O unhappy times" (leads into p. 206)** — the passage tips toward the famous lament that the moderns
  have forgotten antique architecture; the theory here is polemical as well as practical.
