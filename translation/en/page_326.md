# Page 326 — Chapter XXII

*(Chapter argument:)*

> HAVING COME OUT FROM THE LITTLE BOAT, TO MEET THEM INFINITE NYMPHS CAME,
> SUPERBLY ARRAYED WITH TROPHIES. POLIPHILO NARRATES ALSO THE MYSTERIOUS
> MANNER IN WHICH THEY OFFERED THE DIVINE INSIGNIA TO CUPID, AND WITH WHAT
> HONORARY PROCESSION, HE BEING SET TO SIT UPON THE TRIUMPHAL VEHICLE; AND
> POLIA AND POLIPHILO, BOTH BOUND, FOLLOWING BEHIND, WITH GREATEST TRIUMPH
> CAME TO THE GATE OF THE MARVELLOUS AMPHITHEATRE — WHICH, BOTH WITHOUT AND
> WITHIN, HE FULLY DESCRIBES.

Sweetly, with a mild breeze breathing zephyr, the comely and golden little wings
of the divine boy softly vibrating, and by his tranquil spirit conveyed, we came to
the ebbing shore. Many and infinite gift-bearing demigoddesses, and notable Nymphs,
of conspicuous beauty, came out with us from the fateful little boat. Straightway, to
meet the divine and winged boy, with a chosen troop, with great apparatus of
ornaments, and of pomps, and sumptuous vestments, with a splendour and cultivation
more than regal, with a most exquisite adornment, especially and solemnly venerating,
of tender and most flourishing little age — most delightfully in pyrrhic dance, with
virginal allurements, and heavenly and illustrious aspect, humbly, and with most
decent attendance, obsequious, all bounteously offered themselves.

And before them all, the sacrificing shrine-bearers, the tower-bearers, and the
jubilant fore-dancers went preceding, with trophies of military ornaments arranged on
gilt spears: with the cuirass of furious Pyroeis; with the other conquered armours;
and with the bow, hanging crosswise, retaining the cuirass; and with the barbed quiver
and the axe bound to the extremities of the bow; and, beneath the cuirass, the
spread-out net; with, set below, the face of a little winged boy, and a twin one; and
an apple affixed to the face, transfixed through the middle by the spear; and at the
summit, the starry helmet.

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The chapter-argument (the whole triumph of Cupid in précis) and
the landing: the nymphs come out with trophies to meet the winged boy, and the first
**trophy of arms** is described piece by piece (cuirass of Pyroeis, bow, quiver, axe,
net, apple, starry helmet, all on a gilt spear). All secure; the trophy-vocabulary is
antique-military.

- **The triumph of Cupid begins.** Chapter XXII is the *triumphal procession* on
  Cythera: the nymphs bear trophies to Cupid, seat him on a triumphal car, and lead the
  whole train — with Polia and Poliphilo bound as captives behind — to the gate of the
  central amphitheatre. This is one of the book's four great Triumphs, and the one that
  frames the world's approach to the theatre of Venus (ch. XXIII).
- **the trophies of arms.** Each nymph bears a *tropaion* — arms hung on a spear in the
  Roman manner. The first carries a cuirass (of "furious Pyroeis," one of the Sun's
  horses, here a figure of martial ardour), bow, quiver, axe, a net, an apple, and a
  starry helmet: the disarmed panoply of Mars, taken by Love. The apple is Venus's.
- **pastophorae, pyrgophorae, antiludiae** — shrine-bearers, tower-bearers, and
  fore-dancers: Colonna's Greek-coined names for the ranks of a sacred procession. The
  triumph is staged as a religious rite, not a military parade.
- The following pages (327–328) itemise the rest of the trophies, several carrying
  emblematic inscriptions (**QUIS EVADET?**, **NEMO**).
