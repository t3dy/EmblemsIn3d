intrepid monitress made all the virgins rise up; and, taking a golden little rod in the purified hands,
ordained that my most-choice Polia — from the ritual book open before her, held by the little priestess,
reading, and according to the rubrical orders — should take of the remaining ashes from the cremated
sacrifice. Which, with singular veneration taken up, in a golden sieve prepared for such ministry, upon the
venerable step of the dedicated Altar, sifting, most-aptly she riddled, with so shrewd a promptitude as if
another had ever, most-devoted, operated (it). Where the most-erudite monitress, the other fingers of the
left hand contracted, made her extend the ring-finger; and in the holy ash she expressed certain characters,
with most-exact diligence, such as, in the pontifical ritual volume exemplar, marvelling, limpidly she painted.

That the most-diligent Polia had done this — the sage monitress made Polia, and all the others, again upon the
precious pavement humbly genuflect; and, most-accurately upon the ritual index marvelling looking, likewise she
too, with the golden little rod, superstitiously, in that same ash, signed some mysterious figures.

For which thing I, stupefied and totally alienated, and made all timid — insomuch that on my head not a hair
remained that was not raised — with the soul much suspended, doubting, thought-heavily, in this solemn and
sacred propitiation, that my ingenuous Polia was not stolen away (like Iphigenia), and some other animal, or
damsel, put in — and of losing, in a moment, all my desired good: whence the heart smitten, and into itself
concluding all the little spirits, without vital vigour almost I found myself. Nay, so strongly it shook me,
as the mobile reeds, wind-blown by the impetuous and wanton breezes; and more tremulous than the wooden
shavings, and with the mind vibrating, more than the thin sedges in the marshes, by forceful winds —

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The apparition and its aftermath: the winged spirit (myrtle-crown, **fire-arrow**,
diamond crown — a little Cupid) circles the altar thrice and **dissolves into smoke**; then Polia, at the
priestess's direction, **sifts the sacrificial ash** and traces mysterious characters in it with her ring-
finger, the priestess adding her own signs; Poliphilo, terrified, fears Polia will be **substituted like
Iphigenia** and he will lose her. All secure.

- **The Cupid-epiphany.** The spirit from the smoke is Love itself — crowned in myrtle and diamonds, bearing
  the fire-arrow — who circles the altar three times and melts into the air. The sacrifice has summoned the
  god; his brief appearance blesses the union. A radiant, animatable moment for the world's rite (a winged
  light rising from the altar-smoke and dissolving).
- **the ash-writing.** Polia sifts the ashes and traces "mysterious characters" with her ring-finger (the
  finger of the *vena amoris*, running to the heart), copying the ritual book; the priestess adds her signs.
  A cryptic, occult act — love-magic written in the remains of the sacrifice. Colonna leaves the characters
  undescribed: the mystery is meant to stay sealed.
- **Poliphilo's Iphigenia-terror.** His fear that Polia will be swapped at the last moment "like Iphigenia"
  (snatched by Diana, a deer left in her place) is the chapter's suspense: having just won her (p. 218), he
  dreads losing her to the very rite that sanctifies her. The anxiety drives toward the miracle (the rose-bush)
  that resolves it (pp. 228+).
- **the reed-and-sedge similes** — Poliphilo trembling "like reeds… like marsh-sedges in the wind": the
  book's stock nature-similes for fear (cf. the pheasant-heart, p. 197). His body registers the dread.
