## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** **Poliphilo's death** (his soul flees, and he "dead remained" prostrate in the temple), and
**Polia's revival of him** at dawn (repentant, embracing, kissing, and "revoking his soul" with her tears — the same scene
she narrated, ch. XXIX); then the argument of **chapter XXXV** (his spirit, having been at Venus's throne, returns joyfully
to revive him) and the spirit's **address to his own body** ("exult, my little body… to the won victory attend"). All
secure; the death-and-revival from his side, and the start of his near-death vision.

- **The death-and-revival converge.** This is the exact event Polia gave (ch. XXIX, pp. 420–422) — his death and her
  reviving embrace — now from his side. The two narrations meet at the miracle: she calls his soul back with her tears; his
  soul returns from Venus's throne. The double account of the resurrection is the emotional and structural climax of the
  whole double-book design. Both lovers tell the same salvation.
- **the soul's journey to Venus.** Poliphilo's near-death experience: his soul, freed at death, is "up-borne to the high
  throne of Venus" — a *katabasis*/anabasis where the goddess grants the "obtained grace" that permits his return. His
  death is not an end but a passage to the goddess, who sends him back reconciled. Venus's grace (Book I's whole cult)
  operates the miracle. The vision at her throne (his complaint, Cupid's promise) fills pp. 456–457.
- **the spirit addresses the body.** A striking device: Poliphilo's returning *soul* speaks to his own *body* ("O my little
  body, gracious mansion… exult"), announcing the victory. Colonna dramatises the reunion of soul and body as a joyful
  reconciliation — the resurrection told as a soul rejoining its home.
- **love as won victory.** The spirit frames the revival as a *triumph* — "the won victory, the attained Trophy" — the
  reward of perseverance (the chapter's theme, p. 436). His death was the last trial; his revival, the triumph. The
  Cupid-triumph imagery of Book I (ch. XXII) here becomes his personal victory.
