## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The end of the trophy-train, then **Psyche**, Cupid's divine wife,
leading the file of companion-nymphs, whose sumptuous jewelled silk dress — wind-pressed over
the body, gold-scaled breastplates, pearl-stuffed *phrygium* bands over the breast — is
described in the book's characteristic erotic-textile detail. All secure.

- **Psyche appears, as Cupid's queen.** The soul-bride of the *Cupid and Psyche* myth walks at
  the head of the companions in "a queenly robe… crimson gold." Her presence sets the triumph
  under the sign of the reconciled love of Soul and Love — the myth Apuleius tells, and the
  frame for Poliphilo's own initiation.
- **the wind-pressed dress, again.** As with the boating-nymphs (p. 313), Colonna dresses the
  companions in silk so fine the spring breeze reveals the body beneath — "crisp pleats… over
  the virginal hips." The eroticism is consistent and deliberate: Love's triumph is sensuous.
- **the *phrygium* over the breast** — a pearl-stuffed embroidered band spiralling up to the
  breast-band, "such as was never invented in Phrygia": Colonna's habitual surpassing of the
  famous craft, here the Phrygian embroiderers.
- **"Hermaria and Malicha of Hesperia"** — obscure place-names for fine cloth (the western
  lands); their weave cannot match Psyche's robe. The surpassing-topos on textiles.
