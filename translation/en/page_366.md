## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The famous wounding is secure and rendered as close to
the pitch of the original as English bears; two textual weak points are flagged.
The passage ends mid-sentence on the Hermaphroditus image — which is the whole
book's central figure — and continues on p. 367.

- **`amthook`** — placeholder. The transcription reads *l'amento*, which is not a
  standard word; likely *l'ammento* or a form for the notch/loosing of the arrow
  from the string (cf. *amentum*, a throwing-strap). Rendered "the string's
  [?loosing]" in sense; **marked and to be checked against the facsimile.**
- **not the Gortynian bow / not the Ityraean** — Gortyn in Crete and Ityraea
  were both bywords for archery. Cupid's bow is neither earthly kind but "the
  divine one." The Cretan **dittany** that cannot heal this wound is the herb
  Virgil's Venus uses to heal Aeneas — so the one remedy that works in epic is
  denied here on purpose.
- **`pyrrothricha` / red-haired Polia** — πυρρόθριξ, flame-haired. A rare
  concrete detail of Polia's appearance, kept.
- **Hermaphroditus and Salmacis** — the nymph Salmacis embraced the boy
  Hermaphroditus in her spring until the gods fused them into one two-sexed body
  (Ovid, *Metamorphoses* IV). Poliphilo can describe being pierced by love only
  as *becoming another form* — and reaches, inevitably, for the myth of the
  hermaphrodite. This is the **coniunctio** the whole fountain is built to argue
  (the seven columns' sexual program, p. 360), stated now in the first person:
  the alchemical union is what falling in love feels like from inside. The mark
  the annotators chase through the book lands here.
