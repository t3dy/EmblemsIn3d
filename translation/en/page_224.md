# Page 224 — Chapter XVII / (argument of Chapter XVIII)

Finished the holy and sincere oration, all, singing, made the responsory: "So be it." For which thing, having
I, with most-devout auscultation, venerating, heard, and the holy oration clearly understood, with the utmost
sincerity of heart I stood attentive, and, into myself all that revoked, with scrupulous diligence, and with
exploring eye, immovable, such mysteries I considered. And likewise I, genuflected, observed the expertise of
the antiquarian and sacral ceremonies of the divine Priestess, excessively commending, above all, with what
elegance of promptitude Polia adapted herself to such and so great a *mystagogy* (initiation). Most-intent,
nonetheless, upon that which of it ought to follow.

---

*(Argument of the following chapter — Chapter XVIII:)*

> POLIA DEVOUTLY OFFERS THE TURTLEDOVES. THEN A LITTLE SPIRIT FLIES OUT. WHENCE THE PRIESTESS SAID THE
> ORATION TO DIVINE VENUS. THEN SHE SCATTERED THE ROSES, AND, THE SACRIFICE OF THE SWANS BEING MADE, FROM IT
> MIRACULOUSLY GERMINATED A ROSE-BUSH WITH FRUITS AND FLOWERS. BOTH OF THEM TASTED OF THESE. THEN THEY CAME,
> HAPPY, TO A RUINED TEMPLE — OF WHICH POLIA TELLS HIM WHAT RITE IT HAD, PERSUADING POLIPHILO THAT HE GO THERE
> TO CONTEMPLATE MANY ANTIQUE EPITAPHS. AND, HAVING RETURNED TO HER WITH FRIGHT, AND RECREATED, SITTING
> TOGETHER, POLIPHILO, GAZING AT THE IMMENSE BEAUTIES OF POLIA, ALL INFLAMED HIMSELF IN LOVE.

---

## Notes

**Confidence: high.** The close of the Graces-prayer and of **chapter XVII** (the responsory "So be it";
Poliphilo attentive, admiring Polia's "elegance of promptitude" at the initiation), followed by the
**argument of chapter XVIII** — which previews the sacrifice of the doves and swans, the little spirit that
flies out, the miraculous rose-bush that springs from the sacrifice, and the coming of the lovers to the
**ruined temple** with its antique epitaphs. All secure.

- **Chapter XVII complete.** The chapter has run from the four-Seasons altar and Priapus rite (the Dallington
  seam, pp. 193–194), through the whole architectural ekphrasis of the **Temple of Venus Physizoa**
  (pp. 196–212), to the **rite at the well, Polia's self-revelation and the lovers' union** (pp. 216–218), and
  the inner sacrifice at the gem-altar with the invocation of the Graces (pp. 219–223). It is the pivot of
  Poliphilo's story: the beloved named, the love returned and sanctified. **All of it now in English past
  Dallington's stop.**
- **the argument sets up chapter XVIII.** The next chapter (pp. 225–241) delivers the *miraculous rose-bush*
  (springing from the swan-sacrifice, its fruit tasted by both lovers — a communion of love) and the turn to
  the **ruined temple of antique epitaphs**, which occupies the long chapter XIX. The argument is Colonna's own
  chapter-summary, rendered whole so the parallel-text page keeps the book's structure visible.
- **"mystagogy"** — Colonna calls the whole temple-rite an *initiation* (mystagogy): the union of the lovers is
  framed as a mystery-cult ordination, Polia the initiate, Poliphilo the witness. This is the interpretive key
  to the temple sequence — love as sacred initiation.
- Chapter XVIII begins on p. 225 with the offering of the turtledoves and the spirit that flies out.
