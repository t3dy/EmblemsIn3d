And, treating the gaping wound, the more it exacerbated in asperity, and the more re-doubled the wound-dealing and mortal
grief — but, the hope of re-healing the amplified wound ever having, in him, non-hesitant confidence (that, I being his most-
vowed subject and servant, and his opulent prey, bondman, captive, plunder, and spoil, and his copious Trophy), that same
medicament which his most-pious Mother, and my Lady (Venus), made to the wounded Aeneas, still to me, following the maternal
pity, would aid. And, again, being his most-devoted — that same patronage he should lend which holy Vesta lent to her
handmaid and subject Tuccia, benignly, by the miracle of the sieve (carrying water in a sieve), the perpetrated fault
occulted, freed (her) from public opprobrium and infamous supplice.

Whence — as, to lovers, is wont manifoldly to befall — thus, desperate, in grave litigation, without judge and adverse
party, I condemned both (Love and Fortune), conjured to my deadly harm, with querulous laments weeping, inculpating them as
guilty, and choice enemies of every pious humanity; (then) now, glad and festive, I revoked in me the sentence. Some time,
excited, like a rabid and impatient dog, biting at its retaining chain, I wished to avoid and flee the hard knot of the
amorous but molesting snare, and to un-armour myself. Then, vainly imagining, I feigned many and delectable solaces and
pleasures, false vengeances, temerarious insults, perturbing perils, and impavid death — I found myself, afterward, more
tightly knotted, and solidly armoured. By such altercation, and abortive appetites, consuming my tribulous life — and,
among sighs and bitter sobs, half-consumed — there remained no place that, by me, with solicitude, perennial care, and
scrutatory vigilance, was not indagated and lustrated, ratched, and re-searched; no way, and blind-alley, and almost even

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Poliphilo's devotion to Cupid (hoping the god will "draw the same cruel bow in her" and heal his
wound as Venus healed Aeneas), his oscillation between cursing and blessing Love and Fortune, his straining against love's
"chain like a rabid dog," and his **searching everywhere** for Polia. All secure; the impasse before he finds her.

- **Hoping Cupid will wound her too.** Poliphilo prays that Cupid "draw the same bow in her" — that Polia be struck as he
  was, so their love be equal. This is the exact prayer he later makes to the priestess (p. 430), and — the reader knows —
  it is *granted* (Cupid strikes Polia, p. 419). His whole suffering is the petition the plot answers.
- **Venus healed Aeneas; Vesta saved Tuccia.** His learned hopes: as Venus cured her son Aeneas's wound, and Vesta saved
  the Vestal Tuccia (who carried water in a sieve to prove her chastity), so Love's "maternal pity" will aid him. Colonna's
  antiquarian consolation — the gods help their devotees. Poliphilo trusts the divine machinery that will, in fact, deliver
  the miracle (his revival).
- **love as a chain he cannot break.** He strains "like a rabid dog biting its chain," imagines fleeing, and finds himself
  "more tightly knotted" — the inescapable-captivity theme (pp. 447, 451) restated. His constancy is bondage; he cannot
  not-love. The search that follows leads him to the temple and his death (pp. 453–454).
- **the exhaustive search** — he ransacks every street and alley for Polia: the lover's obsessive pursuit (which Polia,
  from her side, felt as a "siege," p. 389). The double narration's two views of the same hunt — his desperation, her
  resentment — meet as he finds her.
