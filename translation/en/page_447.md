# Page 447 — Chapter XXXIII

— a few days thence, this second little epistle — sedulous and intently superseding to my health — I sent to tell her:

*(Poliphilo's second letter to Polia:)*

"If less were my harsh torment than thy accustomed cruelty, most-beautiful Nymph and Polia — a slaughterer of virtue —
to my long afflictions, with blandishing hope, it would persuade me to patience. But clearly, now, I recognise (by my
depraved and unpropitious Star) thy crude savagery and ferocity, whatever my incendious martyrdom to overcome and beyond
surpass. Then, what avails, what boots it, to Love, to increase and increment, every hour, to my already-consumed little
heart, so sweet a fire, if ever more atrocious and frigescent thou show thyself, more than rigid ice, and thy breast more
frigorific than are Dirce and Nomè, founts, and more than the Salamander, cold, which, with (its) contact, extinguishes
the fire? To my ancillary and servile patience, and to my notified vows and indicated affect — so much the more kindled,
as the more the contrary opposes itself, of thy displeasingness — nonetheless, I cannot unbind the amorous and solid chain,
which, under so soft and pressing a yoke, angariously (oppressively) holds me. Nay, the more I re-kick, so much the more I
implicate myself, taken and captive in this amorous fish-trap (Nassa), like a little fly involved in the inextricable work
of Arachne (a spider's web). And thus, tightly re-bound, and enslaved and captive, not valid nor apt for flight,
constrained am I, that I, bending, incline myself to thee — because in thee alone consists my precious liberty, and my every
necessary good.

"Whence, if thou openly understandest, my Lady, so sincere and consummate affection, and so much voluntary subjection, and
so active and laborious love — why, then, wilt thou not accept so freely these things given to thee (with all my life
offered), which, in thy delicate hands, ambivalent, hangs? Alas, sweetest and beautiful little Polia — succour, I pray thee,
and leave and concede to penetrate, a little, these my (not superb, not arrogant) but devout words, into thy heart; and
arouse in thee somewhat of compassion; receive the warm sighs; auscult my domestic and familiar laments; recognise the
cordial benevolence; attend to so faithful and meek a subject. For, avidly (visibly), I die, I consume myself, of thy
immoderate love — insomuch that all the world could not retract me, nor remove me (more firm than Milo), from (this): that I,
above every other most-precious thing, excessively love, worship, and revere thee, and that I, bowed, adore thee — O effigied
and true simulacrum of a Goddess before my eyes, and to my sight, publicly, so conspicuous and notable represented — in which
most-limpidly I see depicted all my health, and expressed all my peace, delight and content."

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Poliphilo's **second letter**: he now recognises Polia's "crude savagery" (her breast "colder than
ice, than the salamander that quenches fire"), yet cannot "unbind the amorous chain" — the more he struggles, the more he
is "caught in love's fish-trap, like a fly in a spider's web"; he begs her to "let these devout words penetrate thy heart"
and "arouse somewhat of compassion," worshipping her as "a true simulacrum of a Goddess." All secure; the second, more
desperate plea.

- **Love as inescapable trap.** The second letter's image: the harder Poliphilo struggles against love, the more he is
  "caught in the fish-trap, like a fly in Arachne's web." His captivity to love (the recurring theme, pp. 240, 341) is
  now total and acknowledged — he cannot flee even Polia's cruelty. This is the perseverance of love as *bondage*: not
  chosen constancy but inability to leave.
- **the salamander-cold heart.** Polia's breast "colder than the salamander that quenches fire with its touch" — the
  perfect antithesis to his fire. The whole book's fire/ice contrast (Venus's warmth vs Diana's cold, the Hell-fresco,
  p. 250) is compressed into the lovers' two hearts: his flame, her salamander-cold. Only her conversion (fire winning,
  p. 426) resolves it.
- **the beloved as goddess-image.** He worships Polia as "a true simulacrum of a Goddess before my eyes" — the idolatry
  of love (cf. "the only shrine in my heart," p. 440). She is his religion; his letter is a prayer. Its failure (she
  unmoved) shows even worship cannot move the stony heart — grace must.
- The second letter concludes on p. 448, and the argument turns to the death-and-revival (from his side).
