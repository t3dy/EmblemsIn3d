# Page 461 — Chapter XXXVI

*(Chapter argument:)*

> POLIPHILO SAYS THAT, NO SOONER (HAD) THE SOUL, FALLING SILENT, (THAN) IN THE ARMS OF POLIA, ALIVE, HE FOUND HIMSELF.
> PRAYING THEN THE PRIESTESS THAT, PERPETUALLY, BOTH SHE SHOULD BIND OF LOVE — THEN HE MADE AN END. AND POLIA CONCLUDES
> HER NARRATING TO THE NYMPHS, HOW ENAMOURED SHE WAS, AND, OF HER, POLIPHILO.

"Venerable and holy Matron, of this Sacred Temple Priestess preclear and most-worthy: perhaps incredible it appears, and
alien of faith, that — no sooner had the soul put an end to its salutiferous reasonings, (than) in me regressed the
appetible life, (and) suddenly I found myself in the tight and sealed embraces, and juicy and most-savoury kissings, of
this Nymph, virginal flower redolent. And, with the observed order (which she, as charming, festive, eloquently has
narrated), with wondrous and amorous fomentation grew, intimately, our affection, up to the present case. Whence, being
now before thee, notable religious, and president of this sacred, holy place — to thee it appertains decently to divert,
obliquate, and, removing, avert the ill; to prosper the good; and the humble and low things to sublimate; the nodding to
direct and prop; the obscure to lucify; and the adverse to emend and correct. Then, we beseech, make equally an
indissoluble ensnaring, and, coupling, constrain our soul into one concord will, and into one desire; and confirm and
stabilise our united and concrete love — perpetually disposed to succumb and handmaid, serving to the high Empire of the
Divine Mother." And there Poliphilo made silence.

The Divine Priestess, without delay, together made us amorously kiss, and said: "As, to the immortal Gods, it has pleased,
no otherwise be it. Whence, holy and just to me it seems, that you, from the first state, ought to migrate to a more
laudable (one). Be, then, by me, blessed; and live happy, amorous; and, sedulous, visit this Holy temple, for your all
refuge and secure presidium of your mutual love and equal affection. And whichever of you shall be cause of impeding this
fatal love — be (he/she) persecuted by the noxious and fearful arrows and jaculable darts of Cupid; and wounded, the one,
of the golden (arrow), and the other be fixed of the funereal leaden."

"This, then, was the case and primordium of our falling-in-love, in the burning Cupidine flames equally burning, glorious
Nymphs — as, perhaps, fastidiously, at length I have narrated." (And, in this saying, Polia — almost weary of the prolix
sermoning — made an end.)

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The reunion completed: Poliphilo, revived, finds himself "alive in Polia's arms," and prays the
priestess to **bind the lovers eternally**; the priestess **blesses and unites them** ("be blessed, live happy… visit this
temple, your refuge"), warning that whoever breaks the love be struck by Cupid's arrows (gold and lead); and **Polia
concludes her narration** to the nymphs ("this was the primordium of our love"). All secure; the formal union.

- **The priestess unites them — Book I's rite, from Book II's history.** The priestess "makes them kiss, binds them into
  one concord will," blesses them, and appoints the temple "your refuge" — this is the union Book I dramatised (the well-
  rite, ch. XVII, pp. 216–218), now reached at the end of Book II's back-story. The two books close on the same union: the
  outward rite (Book I) and its whole human history (Book II) meet. The circle is complete.
- **the double curse-and-blessing.** The priestess warns that whoever *impedes* the love be struck by Cupid's arrows — one
  by the golden (to love), one by the leaden (to be spurned): the two arrows (p. 411) invoked to *protect* the union. Love's
  weapons, once the lovers' torment, now guard their bond. The whole apparatus of the book turned to blessing.
- **Polia's narration ends.** "This was the primordium of our love" — Polia closes the story she began at p. 381. Her half
  of the double narration (childhood, cruelty, conversion, union) is done; Poliphilo's completes with the framing (p. 462+).
  The book's own account of its love is finished.
- **"migrate from the first state to a more laudable."** The priestess frames the union as a *rise* — from the lovers'
  first (troubled) state to a "more laudable" one (blessed love). The conversion's reward: not just union but elevation.
