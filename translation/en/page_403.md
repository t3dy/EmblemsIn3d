# Page 403 — Chapter XXVII

"'Alas, this exceeded — without measure, wretched me — of all the beasts the atrocity and rage, that the most-savage
animals the lying prey, piecemeal disserping, excarnified (tore the flesh from).' O spectacle of incredible acerbity, and
of cruelty notable! O unheard and unwonted calamity — a scene horrend to watch, of considered (thing) miserable, to feel
formidable and frightful, and, of pondered (thought), spurnable and to-be-fled! O sad me, and wretched, grieving —
whither, without hope, to these mortal perils, am I thus come? Alas, afflicted and unconsoled me — what things are these,
maledict and furiable, that I really and openly see?

"For which thing, invaded by mortal fright, doubting the statuted and decreed death to be approached to me, I began then
dolorously to weep, with copious frequency of tears, and frequent and suppressed sighs, and non-sonorous groans —
awaiting lest, to me too, a similar dilaceration be made; with drawn gaze observing, lest the irate and atrocious Boy,
with the hostile arms, and with savaging severity, connive not (perceive) me to be there, lurking. Then to the glittering
and chaste breast of mine the tearful eyes somewhat I inclined — which I believed henceforth converted and liquefied into
irrigating tears. With anxious words, vacillating, interrupted by sobbed panting in the breast, tumid of frequent groans,
contending to break the encloistered sighs, with the debilitated voice and impeded tongue, tacitly I said:

"'O day unlucky and funereal! O day formidable and horrend — for all my life, to mourning and —'"

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The peak of Polia's terror in the vision: she watches the beasts tear the flesh from the dead
girls, fears "the statuted and decreed death" is coming for her, weeps in her hiding-place lest Cupid spot her, and
begins a lament ("O day unlucky and funereal, for all my life…"). All secure; the terror that will break her.

- **Terror as the agent of conversion.** Polia's cruelty could not be moved by Poliphilo's pleas (pp. 390–396); it is
  broken by *fear* — the sight of what awaits the cruel. Her weeping here (she who shed no tear for the dying Poliphilo,
  p. 396) is the first crack in the frozen heart. The vision does what love could not. This is Book II's mechanism: the
  hard heart thawed by horror, then by grace (the dream and the nurse's counsel follow).
- **the tears begin.** Polia notes her eyes now feel "converted and liquefied into tears" — the melting of the stone-heart
  (the porphyry, p. 389; the wax-on-wet-rock, p. 399) literally beginning. The Venus-rite's prayer to "re-kindle her
  gelid heart" (p. 216) is being answered from within, through terror. The two books' imagery converges precisely.
- **the lament** — "O day unlucky and funereal" opens her formal complaint (continued p. 404): the rhetoric of grief that
  replaces her former cold silence. Polia, who would not speak one word to the dying Poliphilo, now cannot stop lamenting.
  Her voice has been unlocked.
- Her flight home and her dream of the two executioners (the chapter's argument, p. 398) follow, driving the conversion
  toward the nurse's counsel.
