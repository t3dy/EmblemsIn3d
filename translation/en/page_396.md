## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Polia's own account of her **absolute cruelty** as Poliphilo dies: rigid, deaf, wrinkle-browed,
refusing even one word — "harder than Daphne, more wicked than Medea… more harsh than Anaxarete to her Iphis" — while he,
weeping and pleading, pales, sinks, and dies at her side. All secure; the nadir of her self-condemnation.

- **Anaxarete and Iphis — the governing exemplum.** Polia measures herself against **Anaxarete**, the cruel maiden who let
  Iphis hang himself for her love and was turned to stone by Venus (Ovid, *Met.* XIV) — the exact myth of a beloved punished
  for a lover's love-death. Polia is enacting Anaxarete's crime, and (as Book II will show) faces Anaxarete's punishment.
  The self-comparison is her clearest confession that she has become the stone-hearted killer of legend.
- **"harder than the flint… as if I had drunk of the river."** Her heart is stone (cf. the porphyry-freeze, p. 389), her
  pity petrified. The stone-imagery is literal foreshadowing: the cruel are turned to stone (Anaxarete), and Polia's stony
  heart is what the Venus-rite (Book I) will have to thaw. The two books meet here — this is the frozen heart the fountain-
  torch prayer (p. 216) addresses.
- **the death, plainly told.** After all the rhetoric, the death itself is stated with terrible simplicity: "beside me he
  died." Polia does not ornament it. The plainness is the confession's honesty — she watched, unmoved, and he died. Her
  disposal of the body and her flight follow on p. 397.
