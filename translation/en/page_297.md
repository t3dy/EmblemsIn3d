# Page 297 — Chapter XXI

I beheld also one of most teeming olive, and a fig-grove of all the kinds with
copious yield. And a most delightful poplar-wood, and likewise one of
*hippomelides* with the Egyptian pods, with the weeping *metopon* — such as, at the
oracle of Ammon, dripping gum-ammoniac, would not be found.

Which little trees were of most elegant and most artful distribution and order —
which the aspect of the sky there did not want, but without repugnance of nature
everything stood best placed. Whereby that ingenious nature there manifestly
avowed all the delights which, scattered through the universe, she had shrewdly
produced. Here heaped together, and with especial study, one may conjecture all to
be created. The ground too was grassy and flowery and with springing and shaded
fountains, of glassy water most clear, moist with waters sweeter than the fountain
of Salmacis. And there they suffered neither the frost-bearing Bear nor the
cloud-born South Wind, but the air most salubrious, most purged, and most pure,
and by far most lucid, freely pervious to the eyes, most light and even, and
invariable, with great loveliness and a temperate sunniness of place and sky, that
never suffers turbulent alteration; but dissipated, and every cloud driven off,
that liquid sky shows itself, the bold winds shut out — the crackling east winds,
the whistling north, and the malignity and asperity, with great crash, of the
raging storm, and every iniquity of weather — not subject to the tumultuous changes
of waters nor to the cold Balance. But everything may be regarded lustrous, and the
desirable light, glad and pullulating in the station of the woolly Ram drying his
fleece in the illuminating and Herculean Phoebus, with no falling but a perpetual
green, celebrated by the manifold song of birds — as, flitting in the air, the
little crested lark, and together the singing nightingale, made all the air resound
with singing.

At the outermost boundary of this semitertio of a mile, all distributed into little
woods toward the centre — because a circumference of circular figure is of as much
measure as are three of its diameters, and so much the more that in eleven
partitions, one diameter deducted, there are two portions; therefore the diameter
of this voluptuous island stood at one mile, with two of the eleven partitions
added — there went round an excellent enclosure, of a height of eight paces and of
a foot's thickness, so densely leafy that the least stem did not appear, with paired
pervious windows, and, in order at the fitting places and passages, arching open
gates, made and compactly conducted of orange, lemon and citron trees, with lustrous
greenery in the mature leaves, and of new fronds, of the first and mature fruits,
and of the most fragrant flowers dispersedly adorned — so much the more pleasing and
admirable to the aspect, as it is most rarely wont to be granted to human eyes to see
an equivalent work.

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The close of the *bosco* catalogue, the hymn to the
island's perfect climate, and the citrus-hedge that bounds the middle zone are all
secure. The pi-approximation embedded in the boundary sentence (below) is a
literal rendering of Colonna's own arithmetic.

- **The bitter-orange espalier** — "an enclosure of orange, lemon and citron
  trees, eight paces high," bounding the outer ring from the middle: this is the
  *espalier of bitter orange* Segre's plan gives as the divider (GARDENS.md §5),
  and the citrus ring the world's Cythera renders. Colonna's own detail: paired
  windows and arched gates cut through the living citrus wall.
- **the pi-approximation** — "a circumference is as much as three of its diameters,
  and so much the more that in eleven partitions, one diameter deducted, there are
  two portions." Colonna is giving π ≈ 3 + 2/11 = 3.1818…, the standard medieval
  value (22/7 stated as 3‑and‑a‑bit). From it he derives the island's diameter as
  "one mile plus two-elevenths." He is doing the geometry in the open, as
  everywhere on Cythera.
- **"the station of the woolly Ram drying his fleece"** — the sun in Aries: spring,
  the season of the whole dream. The perpetual-spring climate (no frost, no storm,
  no burning) is the mark of the earthly paradise, and Colonna spends a full
  periodic sentence denying each kind of bad weather in turn.
- **the two mulberries and the sweeter-than-Salmacis springs** continue the motif
  of water; Salmacis's spring (the Hermaphrodite's, ch. XXIII) recurs as the
  benchmark for sweetness.
