And then, incontinently, an enucleated admiration succoured in the warm memory: that, it being natural
that whiteness disgregates (scatters) the visual power, this (whiteness), with so much delectation, drew
and congregated all the seeing into itself — not only not harming it, but, to the utmost solace and
extreme pleasure, that so precious object, viscidly retaining it bound to itself, and taken, occupied it.
Whence, the Sacred Woman's devout prayers to the Gods Forculus and Limentinus, and to the Goddess Cardea,
being sincerely finished, and (all) righting themselves, the beautiful Nymph (I alone persisting, with
eyes fixed on her voluptuous operation) — nor for all that would I ever have moved myself, had not the
most-subtle cloth returned to re-cover the divine delights.

Then, suddenly, by the libation-woman the Bolt removed, those twin valves — not with a strident noise, not
a grave rumble, but an acute and grateful murmur, reflecting through the vaulted temple — hissed sweetly.
And, animadverting this, I recognised (it), by seeing under the extreme part of the ponderous valves, of
the one and the other, a voluble and turned little Cylinder, which, by the axle fixed in the valve, upon a
terse and evened plate of hardest Ophite inverting itself, and by the friction, made a most-acceptable
tinkling.

Beyond this, reasonably I was stupefied that the valves, each by its very self, without any impulse, opened
themselves. Whence, then, all having entered, of a sudden — without looking elsewhere, there affirming
myself — wishing to investigate whether the said valves, so timely and moderately, by a counterweight were
drawn, or by some other instrument: whence I beheld a divine device. For in that part where, one with the
other, the valves met in the tongued closure, from the inner part, was a plate of fine steel soldered upon
the metal, most-terse.

There were then, marvellously, two little Axles of a third (foot) in breadth, of best Indian Magnet (to
which the Diamond does not dissent — of Callistone the lover, to human eyes outstanding, by the *scordeon*
made mortal (weakened)) — singularly opportune to navigators — which showed, of their fit colour, cerulean,
smooth and illustrious, fixed most-polishedly in the thickness of the opening of the marble wall (that is,
in the jambs, contiguous to the antae of the artful door). Then, in this manner, by the violence of the
rapacity of the Magnet, the steel plates were violated (drawn); and consequently, by themselves, the valves,
with temperate slowness, unbolted themselves. An excellent and most-exact work — not only to see, but beyond
measure of subtle device. What improbity (audacity) of an artificer's investigation!

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The **self-opening magnetic door**: the priestess prays to the Roman door-gods
(Forculus, Limentinus, Cardea), the bolt is drawn, and the gilt valves swing open *by themselves* — Poliphilo
investigates and finds **lodestone (Indian magnet) axles set in the jambs** that draw the steel-plated valves
open; the doors also ring softly on serpentine-stone rollers as they turn. All secure; the magnet-door is one
of the book's celebrated marvels.

- **The magnet-door.** Two lodestone blocks in the doorposts pull the steel-faced valves open "with temperate
  slowness," no hand touching them — a genuine (if fantastical) piece of magnetic automation, and one of the
  Hypnerotomachia's most-discussed devices. A striking, animatable feature for the world's temple: doors that
  open themselves as you approach.
- **the door-gods.** The priestess invokes **Forculus** (of the door-leaf), **Limentinus** (of the
  threshold), and **Cardea** (of the hinge) — the minor Roman deities of a doorway, exactly right for opening
  a temple. Colonna's antiquarian precision (from Augustine/Tertullian's lists of these gods).
- **the singing rollers** — the valves turn on serpentine-stone cylinders that make "a most-acceptable
  tinkling": the door sounds as it opens (cf. the wind-bells, p. 211). Even the door is a musical machine.
- **the whiteness "scatters the visual power"** — the page opens with Poliphilo's optics again (cf. p. 208):
  the nymph's white calf ought, by theory, to dazzle and disperse sight, but instead draws it — beauty
  overriding the physics of vision. The erotic and the scientific fused, as ever.
