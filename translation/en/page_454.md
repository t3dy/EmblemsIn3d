Panthea's to her consort; and, with more amiable affection than Pylades' toward his Orestes.

Now, to the last, wishing to dispose (her) tractable, and to conduce, I persevered, her sylvestrous and ferine heart, to
mansuefy, and to domesticate to some humanity and sweetness — which hardened itself, persisting, uncontaminated, untamed,
immovable, and crudely lapidescent (turning to stone): no otherwise ignorant of gentleness, and exempt of pity, than if she
were born in Hyrcania, or in the wood of Ida, obstruse of shadows, among the rough and knotty oaks and valid robles; or on
Mount Ismarus; or among the Anthropophagi descended; and among the horrid furies of Cyclops, and in the intercaved cavern of
Cacus a nursling, and among the Syrtes.

For which immanity, constantly persevering in the tormenting exasperations, and non-simulated ache and mourning — newly, and
more noxious, began the raucous sighs in my flammid breast, more than the lowing of a famished, or fever-sick, Lion, in a
sonorous and hidden Cavern, or cave. Re-cogitating, in vain, my every fatigue performed — by her pertinacity rightly
pondering that an imperforate cask cannot be emptied — almost diffident and desperate of so arduous an undertaking, and, in
the weeping eyes, with frequent scaturigine (gushing), infinite tears heaping, more dolorously than the tormented Myrrha,
distilling through the hard bark — whence, more than the just, improbly, without measure, beyond the beginning and quickening
of this my affectionate and invalid sickness, I found myself in the state, with multiplied increment, and re-doubled
augmentation, of my unceasing —"

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Poliphilo's final plea **fails**: no love, no tears (more than Isis wept for Osiris) can soften
Polia's heart, which "hardens, uncontaminated, crudely lapidescent (turning to stone)" as if she were "born among the
Cyclops and the man-eaters"; he offers a love greater than Alcestis's, Portia's, Panthea's — and she is unmoved; his grief
redoubles, weeping "more than Myrrha through the bark." All secure; the impasse just before his death.

- **The stone-turning, from his side.** Poliphilo watches Polia's heart "harden, crudely lapidescent, turning to stone" —
  the *petrification* the whole book fears (Anaxarete, the Propoetides, pp. 396, 410). He names it as it happens: his love
  does not soften but *stones* her (his insight, p. 449). The reader knows she will be un-stoned only by the visions and
  Cupid — not by his love. His helplessness is total.
- **the surpassing-catalogue of wives.** His love exceeds Alcestis (who died for her husband), Portia (who swallowed
  coals), Panthea, Pylades — the great exemplars of devotion. Yet all this love cannot move Polia: the point is that
  *love's greatness is powerless against a stone heart*. Only grace converts. The catalogue measures the futility.
- **"an imperforate cask cannot be emptied."** His despairing maxim — a sealed vessel cannot be poured out; a closed heart
  cannot be reached. He is "diffident and desperate," his undertaking (the Agon) seemingly lost. This is the nadir before
  his death — which, from Polia's side (ch. XXVI), we saw: he dies here. The two narrations meet at the death.
- **Myrrha weeping through the bark.** His tears exceed Myrrha's (the girl turned to the myrrh-tree, weeping resin through
  the bark): the metamorphosis-of-grief image. His account of dying (the collapse Polia witnessed, p. 395) completes the
  chapter (p. 455).
