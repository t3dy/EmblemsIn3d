# Page 388 — Chapter XXVI

For which thing, deserted by all, and relinquished, I was — save by my piteous and best Nurse, who remained to aid, and
to see was the last sigh and exit of my spirit. And already, often, implicated by the grave disease — incomposite words,
and frequent laments and little groans, variously raving and vacillating, I returned to myself. And there, better than I
could and knew, sincerely, from the Divine **Diana** succour I invoked. Wherefore, then, to me of other Numina was no
notice, nor any cult, save that Goddess. And with multiplied prayers, with the tremulous voice purely exorating, I
prayed: to her holy and gelid chastities, tormented by grave sickness, promissory, suppliant, I vowed myself, and,
religiously, to serve ever her sacred temples, with tenacious chastity, if she, pitying me, freed me from the mortal
contagion and disease — with firm and persevering purpose in my mind. And with so much the better hope, as I remembered
the benign favour that the said Goddess to **Iphigenia** lent, while Agamemnon, by Apolline admonition, wished to immolate
her in sacrifice; and, the piteous parents hard weeping, moved by this and pitying, she interposed a fume-bearing cloud,
reserving Iphigenia; (and) the hind was found (in her place). Then, by such-made similitude, I, almost secure, her holy
aid and defence hoped. And therefore there was no dilation of time, that I was cured, and, miraculously convalescing, the
pristine health restored.

For so much, to the high and spontaneous promises, and solemn obligations bound, to the execution I intently exposed
myself, and, sedulous, my professed vows to fulfil — with no lesser purpose to conserve myself inviolate than the Matrons
in the Thesmophoria, sleeping on the strewn leaves of the Agnus tree (chaste-tree); nor with lesser devotion and religion
than Cleobis and Biton had. And, admitted into the holy temple, and into the consortial convent and society of many other
virgin girls received (who to that chaste and pure Goddess served), I began, I too, sedulously, with them, to visit and
humbly the Dianal Altars to venerate. Whence, the most-beautiful part, almost, of my most-flowering girlhood and pleasant
age, consuming in the chaste colds — it intervened that **Poliphilo**, our fervid and insolently enamoured (one), all this
intervalled time (which was a year and more), the wretch ever stood discontent, and in heart-grief. After that, more (of)
my aspect, and the blond hair, by any pact he could not re-see; and, having become distanced from my cold heart, and more
divided than Abila from Calpe (the Pillars of Hercules); and, from my sterile breast, of love scraped out, and totally
diluted, and from my reminiscence obliterated, never came he into my mind — neither more nor less; nay, not so obliterated

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Polia's **vow to Diana** and cure: abandoned by all but her nurse, she prays to Diana (the only
goddess she then knew), **vows perpetual chastity and temple-service** if healed, is miraculously cured, and enters
Diana's temple among the virgin devotees — while Poliphilo, unable to see her for "a year and more," is utterly forgotten
by her, "obliterated from her mind." All secure; the vow that sets her cruelty.

- **The vow of chastity — the root of the refusal.** Polia's plague-vow to Diana (chastity and temple-service) becomes
  the ground of her rejection of Poliphilo: consecrated to the virgin goddess, she "obliterates" him from her heart, "of
  love scraped out." Her later cruelty is thus framed not as mere coldness but as *religious duty* — she is Diana's, and
  will not be Venus's. Book II's drama is the war between the two goddesses over Polia (as the temple of Venus Physizoa
  drew her "from the colds of Diana," p. 229 — here is the Diana-side of that story).
- **the Iphigenia hope.** Polia prays trusting Diana will save her as she saved Iphigenia (the hind substituted at the
  altar) — the same Iphigenia-substitution Poliphilo feared for Polia at the Venus-rite (p. 227). The myth bridges both
  temples: Diana's mercy here, Venus's claim later.
- **the male-excluding temple.** Diana's temple bars all males (even "written and painted male animals" on the walls),
  and Polia's heart is as closed to Poliphilo as the temple to men: a precise image of her consecrated coldness. The
  Bona Dea's male-exclusion (a real Roman cult-rule) grounds it.
- **Cleobis and Biton, the Thesmophoria, the chaste-tree** — the exempla of chastity and pious devotion (the Argive
  brothers, the matrons' chaste festival): Polia arms her vow with learned parallels. Her voice keeps the antiquarian
  register even in her most personal turn.
