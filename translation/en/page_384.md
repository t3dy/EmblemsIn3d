of most-purged welling waters, which still in that most-pleasant region, flowing, most-fresh is seen: from his truncated

transmuted herself into a notable fount, from her surname called the **fountain Calardia**, near the beloved father
**Tito Butanichio**, made into a liquefying river, weeping the hard and cruel lot, mingled with those of his line. And
her mother **Rhoa**, between her husband and her most-beloved brother **Caliano**, flowing, derive into the sweetest
son, Sile.

Nor was any, even of the males, unpunished by the celestial ire and just vengeance. For the second-born, **Lelio
Musilistre**, made into a rivulet of his name, embraces the father, inundating the Altinate peoples. And the other two
younger brothers — innocent, unweaned, teething — the divine retribution somewhat more temperate, they too experienced
metamorphosis: one into a little bird of his name, **Alcyoneo** (clad with regal and incorruptible plumes), and the
other, younger, into the **Tipula** animal (water-strider). Which, dwellers of the father, not immersing themselves,
ever with him stand, fluvial.

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The **divine punishment of the Lelii**: Murgania, made to impersonate Venus (hence "Fada
Morgana"), and the whole family — for their hubris — are thunder-blasted by Jove and **transformed into the rivers and
springs of the Treviso region** (the river **Sile** is the father Lelio Siliro; the fountain Calardia the mother; the
brothers into rivulets, a kingfisher, and a water-strider). All secure; a mass-metamorphosis aetiology of the local
landscape.

- **The family becomes the landscape.** Colonna turns Polia's ancestors into the actual **rivers and founts of the
  Trevisan countryside** — the Sile (Italy's great resurgence-river) as the father, drawing his transformed children
  into himself. It is an Ovidian aetiology (the *Metamorphoses'* river-transformations) localised to a real place: the
  land around Treviso *is* Polia's punished family. The book's pervasive metamorphosis-theme is here at its most
  systematic.
- **"Fada Morgana."** Colonna derives the name of **Morgan le Fay** from Murgania, the girl who impersonated Venus in a
  false temple — a striking (invented) etymology linking the Arthurian fairy to a Venus-cult. A vivid detail.
- **the hubris punished.** The whole catastrophe stems from the daughters "comparing themselves to Venus" (p. 383):
  the gods "do not suffer mortal offences unpunished." This is the ancestral guilt behind Polia's own story — her
  family's pride against Venus, which her own cruelty to Poliphilo (a servant of Venus's son) will echo and finally
  expiate. Book II's moral architecture.
- **Juno/Antigone, Eriboea/Isis** — the exempla of divine vengeance on the proud (Antigone of Troy turned to a stork
  by Juno): Colonna's learned parallels for the Lelii's punishment.
