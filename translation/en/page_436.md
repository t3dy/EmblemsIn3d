"Reverend and holy Priestess: Virtue is, in the arduous and funereal fatigues, and turbulent incommodities, and ungrateful
expense — the knowing to conserve oneself, and, with suasible hope, to the distempered soul, a bridle and temperature, with
probity and shrewd mode, to put; and not, impatient and inconsulate, to precipitate and fall, but, suffering,
perseveringly, to present oneself to the undertaking — however most-strait and difficult it be — and, to the voluble and
obstinate lot, and to its mockeries, and insidious versutia, with dissimulation to cede. Because not with fortitude (force),
but with virtue and wit, one conquers — as Bellerophon, persevering, succeeded to glory. Because, to all the stipends and
prices, the strenuous soldier prefers the glory.

"Wishing, then, I, legitimately, the honour (which is the due and expected prize of my amorous Agon) to consequute — firmly,
valorous, I disposed myself to endure (as an) object at which the violent Cupid might make, of me, a despicable, opprobrious
inconstancy. Judging it dementia and levity to be, timid and vecordious, to accede to the battle; and no thing to present
itself more valid than the fortitude of the soul; and no mediocre shame and verecundity persuading me — to the soldier
befalling that, in the begun contest, he turn tail and show the shoulders. But above all, never, to the militant, does it
appertain to despair, and, in the initiated battle, to defect (fail) — because it is better not to begin, than, having begun,
to leave the undertaking. For, if I err not, it seems to me that one cannot truly be called happy, if he has not somewhat
sustained his opposite (adversity) — because thence is born insolence, is generated confidence, from which proceeds the
unhappy exit, as to Polycrates. And therefore the perfection of the touchstone-trial is so much the better felt, by its
contrary, as, upon the index Battus, clearly is comprehended."

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The priestess's blessing done, **Poliphilo begins his narration** (chapter XXXII) with a **praise
of perseverance**: virtue is to endure the "arduous fatigues" of love without despairing, "not with force but with wit and
perseverance one conquers" (like Bellerophon); the good soldier never turns tail, for "it is better not to begin than,
having begun, to leave the undertaking." All secure; the theme of the whole book stated.

- **The praise of perseverance — the book's moral, from his side.** Poliphilo's constancy through Polia's cruelty
  (chs. XXVI) is here made explicit doctrine: *love is won by perseverance, not force*. It is the male complement to
  Polia's maxim "perseverance alone wears the victor's diadem" (p. 289) and the nurse's counsel — and the argument's
  heading names it. This is the value the whole Hypnerotomachia commends, and the through-line of both books: Poliphilo
  endures, Polia converts, and the union rewards perseverance. Prime thematic material for the world/novel.
- **love as an *Agon*.** Poliphilo casts his courtship as a *contest* (Agon) he must not abandon — "the soldier never
  turns tail." Love is a trial of endurance, its prize (the "honour," Polia) won only by not despairing. The imagery
  (soldier, touchstone, Bellerophon) frames constancy as heroism. His whole suffering (chs. XXVI, retold now from his
  view) is the Agon he won by persevering.
- **Book II's structure completes.** Polia told her story (childhood → cruelty → conversion, chs. XXV–XXIX); now Poliphilo
  tells his (the festival, the letter, his torment, chs. XXXII+), as the argument (p. 381) promised — the lovers narrating
  by turns. The remaining chapters are his courtship-account, filling in the human history behind Book I's dream.
- **"the touchstone felt by its contrary."** Happiness (and love) is proved by adversity, as gold is tested on the
  touchstone: Poliphilo's suffering *validated* his love. The book's consolation — trials are the making of true love.
