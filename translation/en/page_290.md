# Page 290 — Chapter XXI

**Having come most joyfully to the longed-for place, whose worthy loveliness
Poliphilo affirms, fittingly describing its plants, herbs, birds and dwellers.
But first the form of the little boat, and how, at the descent of the lord Cupid,
many gift-bearing nymphs opportunely presented themselves to meet him.**

The divine boy making sail with his spread wings — not from the wineskin of
Ulysses, but urged by the obsequious and dew-bearing breezes, daughters of
Astraeus and of rosy Aurora, of one unanimous consent — Polia and I, finding
ourselves kindled and headlong with eagerness to reach the destined end, with the
greatest delight of love that ever human sense could feel, nor foreconceive, nor
much less divulge. Which, however excessive it was intimately in the heart, was
nonetheless the more readily quickened by the deific presence, and by those most
witty rowing nymphs, and by the sweetest singing, and by the mysterious form of
the solid and unshaken little boat — a fit instrument organised of love — and by
the precious material, and by the sweetness and pleasantness of the place; and
much more by the near flame that Polia, with rare excellence, exuberantly breathed
into my burnable heart. For her amorous and most refulgent eyes, gliding
lightning-like through mine to my inmost heart, seditiously stirred up a most
bitter conflagration; by which, heated through and prostrately wounded, frequent
sobs gushed forth — not otherwise than the cook pours out over the burning and
excessive fire through the lips. Just so, neither more nor less, the boiling gasps
compelled from my fervent heart abundantly boiled up, showing themselves
resounding; and the importunate fires I fittingly mitigated with the sole beauty
of my lovely Polia, my guide. But how it was, I most abundantly experienced such
pleasure that I was wholly absorbed and consumed — more than ever my most decent
tongue could adapt itself to such expression.

Finally, most joyful, glad and triumphant, to the island extremely desired, with
our proud and oar-wandering ship of six banks — not ballasted, but light — which
was compacted of a form thus.

Of a fourfold partition, two were used up in the poop and in the prow, thus alike
in lineament or form, the one as the other. The two other partitions were
dispensed in the remaining hollow body; and —

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The rapturous approach is secure; the boat-description
that begins at the page's end is dense nautical vocabulary and continues onto p.
291, where the confidence note for the vessel belongs.

- **Chapter XXI** opens the arrival at Cythera. The frame is the voyage in Cupid's
  boat, rowed by singing nymphs, that the Dream mode already summarises; here it is
  given in full.
- **"not from the wineskin of Ulysses"** — Aeolus gave Odysseus the winds tied in a
  bag; Cupid needs no such thing, his wings and the willing breezes suffice. The
  breezes are "daughters of Astraeus and Aurora" — in Hesiod the winds are
  Astraeus and Eos's children. Learned, and exact.
- **"as the cook pours out over the fire through the lips"** — Colonna's startlingly
  domestic simile for Poliphilo's sobs: a cook blowing on a too-hot fire. The book
  repeatedly undercuts its own high style with a homely image (cf. the ship's
  bell "as if the mint of the Queen of England," in Dallington). Kept.
- The six-banked boat (*exeres*, a hexareme) is described in full on p. 291.
