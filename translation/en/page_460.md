# Page 460 — Chapter XXXV

Being, then, in such a manner rapt and sublimated, and, of beholding the celestial works, stupefied — and re-dried the flux
of the solicitous tears, and benignly ausculted my miserable laments — the Divine Lady Mother, with an ineffable majesty and
sanctimony, and with an unheard and venerable voice, soothing (enough to re-serene the beclouded heavens, to take the noxious
armature from Enyalian Mars, and the thunderbolts from the hand of the jaculating Jove, to in-youth the old Saturn, to
Ethiopise (darken) the most-beautiful Phoebus, to in-stammer the eloquent Cyllenian (Mercury), and to defile the chaste
Diana) — never by the terrestrials heard such (a voice) — proffered divine words, with divine afflation, and with such
Harmony, affably, as never, to the empty Syrinx (pipe), the divine lips of the talaric Mercury, (playing) to the eyed Argus,
breathed — by the sweetness of which, whatever Cotic whetstone-rock of Libya, nay, whatever Indian Diamond, contaminated and
immuted, would have been softened to every softness, tender and fresh.

And, in this manner speaking, she made me secure of my health, and of my prosperous love, and of this my most-jocund
*postliminium* (homecoming), and to thee (Polia) to return. And, with a most-charming little laugh, she said to her offspring:
"And thou — if, for the wounded Virgin girl, perhaps to turn-tail from this amorous office, and to relinquish this present
soul, thou shouldst fore-attempt — thou shalt be surety (bail)." — "Then, my little body, my lodging: remove from thee all the
most-harsh griefs, and every passion, and accept me thus entire into thee, as ever, with thee, conjoined I was — with that
most-celebrated name, in me impressed, by which, from thee, I receded; which, otherwise, is carved and impressed, and sealed,
within me, vegetable and fecund — (it) was not that of Oenone and of Paris, sculpted in the ramose trees and rugose barks,
nor thence ever shall it be erased, nor to be deleted, but eternally consigned, conservable. Now, most-loving host, receive
me, thy indigene (native), who, to remedy thy grave and insupportable tribulations, have penetrated and passed through so
many waters of weepings, and through so much fire of love, and through so many supreme fatigues; and, finally, up-borne
where thy likes cannot be, and have attained so much divine benignity, that I, thence timely sequestered, bear thy most-
valiant and most-whole health."

And I, to my re-turned and re-gathered Genius (soul), responded: "Come, indigene inhabitant, and Lady of the supreme citadel
of my mind, best rational portion. Come, my heart, domicile of irritable ardour. Come, extreme part where makes residence my
exhorter Cupid — and let us make, then, the festive Soteria (thanksgivings) for thy retrograde reformation (return)."

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The vision's climax: **Venus assures Poliphilo** of "his health, his prosperous love, and his
homecoming," in a voice that could "soften a diamond"; she makes Cupid **surety** for the union; and Poliphilo's **soul
returns to his body**, bearing "the sealed name" (of Polia, "carved eternal, not like Oenone's on the bark") — the reunion
of soul and body welcomed with joy. All secure; the end of the throne-vision and the revival.

- **Venus's diamond-softening voice.** The goddess's word is so sweet it "would soften a diamond to tenderness" — the exact
  power needed on Polia's stone/diamond heart (p. 389, "frigid Diamond"). Venus's grace *is* the softening of the hard
  heart: what the arrow does to Polia, the voice does to the vision. The whole book's problem (the stone heart) is solved by
  divine sweetness. A resonant image.
- **Cupid made surety for love.** Venus binds Cupid, on penalty, to complete the union — "thou shalt be bail." The god who
  caused the trouble is legally bound to fix it. The union is now *guaranteed by heaven*, not left to the lovers' efforts.
  Grace, not perseverance alone, secures the ending (though perseverance earned the grace).
- **the sealed name.** The soul returns bearing "the most-celebrated name (POLIA) impressed, carved eternal, not like
  Oenone's on the bark" — a name that cannot be erased. Love engraved in the soul, permanent where the tree-carvings of myth
  fade. (The acrostic of the whole book spells "Poliam Frater Franciscus Columna peramavit" — the name-in-the-soul is the
  book's own device.) Polia's name is the soul's indelible seal.
- **the soul rejoins the body.** Poliphilo welcomes his soul home ("Come, Lady of my mind's citadel… best rational
  portion") — the resurrection told as a joyful reunion of the self. The near-death vision ends; he revives (in Polia's
  arms, chs. XXIX). The two accounts of the miracle are now complete from both sides.
