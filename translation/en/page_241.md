# Page 241 — Chapter XVIII / (argument of Chapter XIX)

For which thing — by this way wishing to this incentive and frequent stimulus to put a finish — lo, that, of
other most-savage fires, my scarred heart, all from the beginning, to burn candently I felt. "Alas, how wilt
thou do here, Poliphilo? Somewhat cogitate of the violence done to Deianira, and to the chaste Roman
(Lucretia) — badly and unhappily turned out — and of many others. Consider that the omnipotent gods of earthly
loves have received resistance — let alone a lacerated and most-abject man. Revoke into memory that all long
time accrues to him who can wait; and that the fierce lions, again, by continued days, are made gentle, and
each other sylvan and cross animal; and the grain-bearing ant, again, by assiduous journey — however most-tiny —
in the hard flint imprints its trodden (path): let alone that a divine form, hidden in a most-human little body,
ought to take in itself the vestige of fervent love."

And thus, observed, reprobating, I confuted (refuted) so great a harm — the vexing passion taming — hoping to
attain the amorous fruits, and coveted effects, and triumphant contest. In memory questioning the holy orations,
and sacrifices, and libations, and the extinction of the torch — in which divine offices herself, and her
Poliphilo, she had intently and especially commemorated with commendatory entreaties — for so much I thought, by
suffering, a more efficacious reward and requital, and the implored (thing) to attain, than that, with perilous
improbity, to profit my most-bitter languors, and lose thence every hope.

The Nymph Polia, aware of the versicolour (changing) of my face, and its varying — more than the illustrious
*Tripolion*, or *Teucrion*, that three times a day changes the colour of its flower — and seeing me altered, and
solicited certainly by the intimate love (so many warm and frequent sighs), pitifully, with her adulating
regards, timely tempered and delineated (dissolved) the impetuous movements and irruent agitation. And so, and
not otherwise, my soul burning, in these continual flames, and burnable asperity, love stimulated me patiently to
hope — that, as the Arabian phoenix, in the aromatic twigs, in the aspect of the ardent Sun kindled, from the
arid ashes hopes to renew itself.

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Poliphilo **masters the temptation**: recalling the disasters of forced love (Deianira,
Lucretia — "the chaste Roman"), the resistance even the gods met, and the maxims of patience (the tamed lion, the
ant wearing the flint), he resolves to win Polia "by suffering" not "by perilous improbity," remembering the
sacrifices and the extinguished torch. Polia, seeing his face change colour "like the *Tripolion*," soothes him,
and his soul burns on "like the phoenix" hoping to be renewed. This ends **chapter XVIII**; the argument previews
**chapter XIX** (the epitaphs, the Rape of Proserpina, and the boat). All secure.

- **Restraint chosen — the fidelity-thread's sharpest test passed.** Against the assault-impulse of p. 240,
  Poliphilo reasons himself back to patience: force ends badly (Deianira's shirt, Lucretia's rape), even gods are
  resisted, and constancy wins in time (the lion tamed, the ant's path worn in stone — the maxim of Polia's own
  "perseverance," p. 289). He will earn Polia "by suffering," honouring the torch he extinguished. This is the
  moral resolution of the whole temptation — love as patient service, not conquest.
- **the phoenix.** His unquenched desire is figured as the phoenix burning to be reborn: love that consumes in
  order to renew (echoing the fruit's "transmutation," p. 234). The chapter ends not with desire ended but with
  desire transfigured into hope.
- **the argument sets up chapter XIX.** The next (and longest) chapter takes Poliphilo *into* the ruined
  Polyandrion to read its **antique epitaphs** of dead lovers — culminating in the **Rape of Proserpina**, which
  so frightens him (fearing Polia lost like Proserpina) that he runs back to her; then the **god of Love** comes,
  and Polia leads him to the boat and the crossing (which chapter XX, already translated, continues). The whole
  Book-I arc now joins up.
- **Chapter XVIII complete.** From the incense-rite and rose-bush miracle (pp. 225–233) to the Polyandrion and
  this temptation-and-restraint: the chapter has carried the lovers from the temple of fulfilled love to the tomb
  of love's victims, and tested Poliphilo's constancy on its very threshold.
