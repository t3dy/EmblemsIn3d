After I was drawn out of these long and anxious thoughts and fantastic imaginings, remembering
exquisitely all the wondrous, holy and divine things seen by me until now nakedly and openly, at last I
knew that they were not deceiving illusions, nor false magics, but truly things imperfectly grasped.

Looking intently, then, with these thoughts, the most excellent Nymph at my side and so close by,
wounded my sad heart unceasingly, dying as I was, with her eyes packed with amorous and busy darts. By
which she at once stirred up every wandering and straying thought of mine, and, all of them gathered
and hardened into that one fixed object, she called back my deadened soul vigorously to renew itself in
its first fires.

Which suffered bitterly for not daring to ask whether she were my divine and most desired Polia. Seeing
that she had before given me a somewhat ambiguous and suspected knowledge; and doubting deservedly
(since my rude and untilled speech would not have been fitting at all) that I should shamelessly offend
her, my warm voice having come many times to my silent lips, for such a reason I pressed it down.

But whatever it was, being beset by a marvellous astonishment (like deceived Sosia by the counterfeited
son of Atlas), I found myself greatly in suspense; above all, examining the heavenly workings with
subtle glances and heartfelt weighings, invaded by an ardent desire — so that beyond measure I longed
thus, saying within myself:

Here I would gladly be reckoned a perpetual citizen; and if it could be, no toilsome trouble could seem
heavy to me, no threatening peril would frighten me. However deceitful fortune should set itself
against me, I would then, without further thinking, lay out my dear and precious life entire. Not
refusing to take up the laborious and heavy choice of the two doors shown to the son of Amphitryon, and
to use up my sweet youth and my pleasant years through the mortal perils of the raging sea, and through
the fearful places of Sicily, with greater and highest labours and terror than were borne by wandering
Ulysses, being in the darkened and pathless cave of the horrible Cyclops Polyphemus, son of Neptune, and
