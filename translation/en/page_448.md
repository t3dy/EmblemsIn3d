# Page 448 — Chapter XXXIII / XXXIV

"Now, my hope — deny not to me, wholly thine, who thus piteously supplicate pity, and, to my urgent fire, a temperament —
that, without that, to live I know not, nor can; and, if I could, I would not. Because securely I hope, in that angelic
semblance, in those most-modest and ornate customs, in that graceful and illustrious aspect, (to find) help some time —
which, without doubt, is a special and preclear token that the supernal Jupiter, with exquisite and supreme diligence, has
skilfully-made thee, a miraculous ostentation, of all excellent beauties polished; which, without doubt, of all the
beautiful damsels of the world, transcend, outstanding, and, in thee alone, complexively (all together) perfect. For which
thing, in the least I doubt not that, again, that same Artificer — who so much good, and celestial gifts, in thee has feigned
and carved, benignly, of his similitude — I hold, then, a certain opinion, that likewise some fragment of clemency, in thy
human heart, he has, by every mode, collocated; and has not created thee among the Hyperborean Griffins, nor of mother
Niobe, nor of the sylvan and rough father Apulus, nor generated of the cruel Thracian Diomedes, nor of the furious Orestes,
nor of the malign Phaedra — but of most-humane parents, and, perhaps, beyond-worldly. And this is that which, chiefly,
conserves and sustains me, in so much fervour rosy and fluid. Otherwise, the carbuncled heart, and the soul, indignant,
would, henceforth, have fled. Succour, then, auxiliary and health-bringing (one) — for I supplicate not the insolent desire
of Midas, nor that of Pygmalion, but that, propitiated, thou lend favour; subvene to the need; show thyself pious; placate
thy ire; settle the soul; tranquillise the mind; mollify thy heart; embrace the amorous affect. Rule (as Lady) him who
wishes, thy most-faithful servant, eternally serving, to serve thee. Farewell."

> POLIPHILO CONTINUES HIS DOLOROUS HISTORY; AND HOW, POLIA NOT COMMOVING HERSELF FOR THE TWO EPISTLES, HE SENT HER THE
> THIRD; AND THERE, SHE STILL ENDURING MORE IN HER CRUELTY, BY CHANCE POLIPHILO FOUND HER ALONE, PRAYING, IN THE TEMPLE OF
> DIANA — WHERE HE DIED. THEN, IN HER SWEET EMBRACES, HE RESUSCITATED.

"Lady most-whole in the sacred (things), and Divine Priestess: since, of my dolorous, unpolished and inelegant narrating,
to thy holy and benign sight, up to now, it has not irked (thee) — henceforth, drawing to the end my prolix haranguing, I
shall follow briefly. Daring —"

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The **end of the second letter** (Poliphilo hopes Polia's beauty proves her born "of most-humane
parents, not of Niobe or Diomedes," and begs her to "mollify thy heart, embrace the amorous affect… rule thy faithful
servant"); then the **argument of chapter XXXIV** (the third letter, and — from Poliphilo's side — his **death in Diana's
temple and revival in Polia's embrace**), and the start of his final narration. All secure. Book II is about to retell,
from his view, the death-and-revival Polia narrated (chs. XXVI, XXIX).

- **"Some fragment of clemency."** Poliphilo argues from Polia's *beauty* to her presumed *mercy*: God, who made her so
  fair, must have "collocated some fragment of clemency in her human heart" — she cannot be born of cruel stock (Niobe,
  Diomedes, Phaedra). It is the letter's last hope, and a poignant misjudgement: her heart *is* stone (till grace melts
  it). The argument-from-beauty-to-kindness fails, because beauty and cruelty coexisted in her (her family's hubris,
  p. 384) until she was converted.
- **the two failed letters.** Both letters (chs. XXXIII) leave Polia "not commoving herself" (argument). Poliphilo's
  perseverance in writing is heroic but futile — rhetoric cannot convert. The third letter (ch. XXXIV) will also fail, and
  only his *death* and her *visions* accomplish what words could not. The book's insight: love is won by grace and
  suffering, not eloquence.
- **the convergence.** Chapter XXXIV retells the temple death-and-revival (Polia's chs. XXVI, XXIX) from Poliphilo's side —
  the two narrations meeting at the same event, the structural climax of the two-books design. His account of dying and
  waking in her arms completes the mutual telling.
- **"drawing to the end my prolix haranguing."** Poliphilo signals the narration is nearly done — Book II approaches its
  close (the reunion, the priestess's blessing, and — after XXXVIII, the awakening — the end of the dream).
