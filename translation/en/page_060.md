Above which most polished binding the soffit began to arch, its vaulted bend conforming to that of the
gate. Of such a fertile and delightful conceit it offered itself, with such excellent carving, neatly
full of little water-monsters in the feigned water, and among the moderate wavelets half-men and
women, with spiralling little fish-tails. Upon these, near the back, some sat fitly; some of them
naked, embracing the monsters with mutual entwining. Some pipers, others with fantastical instruments.
Some drawn along, sitting in strange Chariots. Crowned by the ever-swimming Dolphins with the cold
flower of the water-lily; some clothed in its own leafage.

Some with manifold vessels of copious fruits, and with crowded horns of plenty. Others with little
bundles of sweet-flag, and of flowers of the Silvan's beard, struck at one another. Some were girt
with reeds. The other part, riding upon hippopotami, wrestled. And other divers and hateful beasts,
with tortoise-shell for shield. And here they gave themselves to wantonness, and here to various games
and feasts, with lively strivings and movements excellently carved and expressed. Completely, from the
one end and the other, they adorned it.

In the turning of the vault I saw a most diligent worm-like work of exquisite little glass tesserae,
with a gilded surface and of every most pleasing colouring. And first there presented itself a frieze
two feet broad, which, going about, adorned the enclosed extremities of all the vaulted space, from the
bindings recited upward, and along the length of the vault's pinnacle, with a companionable joining,
existing in lively colourings. Nor otherwise than as if they had been composed just then: with natural
foliage of emerald greenness, with the reverses of a Punic colour, with flowers of Cyan and of Phoenician
and of adulterate hue, with gracious entwinings and knottings.

In the space contained by these, I beheld this ancient history painted.

The young Europa swimming to Crete upon the counterfeited bull. And to her sons the edict of King
Agenor: to Cadmus, Phoenix and Cilix, that pursuing they should find their violated sister. And not
finding her, at the rising spring they strenuously slew the scaly Dragon. And thereafter, having taken
counsel, they determined by Apollo, with their companions, to build a city where the lowing Heifer
should stop. Whereupon that country to this day eternally keeps the name of the Lowing bellow. Cadmus
therefore built Athens; the other brother Phoenicia; the third Cilicia. Most excellently disposed, and
carried out in a drawn-out order, of feigning, of painting in natural colours as it required — the
acts, the places, and the fitting expression of the history — was this mosaic picture.
