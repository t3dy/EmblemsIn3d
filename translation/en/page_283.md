> POLIPHILO NARRATES THAT THE NYMPHS, HAVING RESTED THE OARS, BEGAN SWEETLY TO SING. AND POLIA,
> SINGING IN COMPARISON (in answer), FELT A GREAT SWEETNESS OF LOVE.

With the utmost auspicious, superb and great pomp, insolent triumph, with unthinkable joy and voluptuous
gratification — with stimulating darts savagely fixed in the scarred heart, at the amorous eyes of Polia,
and at the arrow-frequency of Cupid, an opposing and most-firm target — with an amplified heart, more
furnace-like burning, ever more avid to augment the ardour, through the solicitous ministry of the
insatiable and impatient eyes (to which reasonably I pardoned it, considering the cause that made them so
cupidinous, and supremely applied to it, and sedulously intent): like the simulacrum of Apis, which
toward the sun ever turns, gazing — so, and not otherwise, were my eyes directed upon her, who was a
conspicuous and exciting object, from that much-admired irradiating face, in the world proscribed of
equivalent beauty, and by every means interdicted.

But more noxious, and much more iniquitous and vexing slaughter, did the furtive and wandering thoughts
experience of this valorous lord — best of such matter the shrewd quaestors, and of the gain the most-apt
artificers, to fabricate and compose, of fire and of flames, so sweet a torment, so venerable an idol, so
shapely a simulacrum, so outstanding a form, in the workshop of the imaginative faculty, and solacingly
to feign. Oh, how much — molesting, and refusing all temperament, declining to succumb — did these
unbridled and public cut-throats of my repose and quiet show themselves, and insatiable and raging of the
unseen beauty of my golden-haired Polia; sometimes sweet, and sometimes bitter, sometimes glad, and more
often sad; many times desirable, and often to be fled, they accused themselves. What most-valid forces,
then, could have imprisoned the incontinent senses — reluctant, to dissent; and dissenting, to repugn;
and repugning, to drive off; and driving off, to spurn —

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The end of ch. XIX and the argument and opening of **chapter XX** (the singing at
the oars). The opening is Poliphilo's interior monologue — his fixed gaze on Polia (like the sun-following
Apis) and the "furtive thoughts" that Love forges into torment. All secure; a psychological page, its long
periods kept long.

- **Chapter XX placed.** In the work-order this is the boat-journey to Cythera that *precedes* the landing
  (ch. XXI) — the nymphs rest their oars and sing, Polia answers, and Poliphilo's love intensifies. The
  world's approach-to-Cythera (the digit-0 sailing) corresponds to this chapter; its imagery (the
  *Amor vincit omnia* standard, p. 285) belongs to the boat the player rides.
- **the sun-following Apis** — the Egyptian bull-god's image "ever turns toward the sun, gazing"; so
  Poliphilo's eyes turn always to Polia's face. A characteristic Colonna simile drawn from Egyptian cult
  (cf. the Serapis of p. 344).
- **Love as a forger of torment "in the workshop of the imaginative faculty"** — the same idea as the
  "siege-engines of beauty" (p. 337): desire is manufactured, an artifice worked on the heart. The book's
  recurring analysis of its own eroticism.
- **"golden-haired Polia" (xanthothrica)** — Polia's epithet; her fair hair is a fixed attribute (cf. the
  portrait, p. 337).
