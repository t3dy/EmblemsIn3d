chemise, most-tenuous and shining, bound in the decent part, with silken little snares doubled
into armlets, or let into flattened gold circlets, and pointed with pure gold, with pendent
little knots, with exquisite virginal (art). For there desire, and the thing coveted, with the
knowing and the power, reconciled itself with the will. So much, by Jove, to the amorous eyes was
it most-pleasant, that one would truly desire nothing else but to be able perpetually to gaze —
and Nymphs so comely, and of such shapeliness and delight, outstanding, with their bright-eyed
aspects so sumptuous, and wantonly comely, and so, with snowy garments of the utmost polish,
pre-shining.

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The end of the costume-ekphrasis: the sleeves (upper arm to elbow, then a
gold-woven under-sleeve to the hand), laced with gold cords, the sheer cotton chemise puffing out
at the elbow-slashes; and then Poliphilo's first exclamation — that these nymphs are so pleasing
to "the amorous eyes" one would wish only to gaze forever. All secure.

- **The slashed sleeve, precisely.** Arm covered to the elbow, a gold-woven under-sleeve to the
  wrist, laced at the elbow so the "snowy cotton chemise" pulls through the slash in a puff — the
  exact slashed-and-pouffed sleeve of high-Renaissance dress, described at its point of origin. A
  concrete costume detail for the world's figures.
- **"desire… reconciled itself with the will."** The hinge line: the sight of the nymphs makes
  *desire* and *will* agree — the beginning of the meditation that page 337 develops, on beauty as
  the instrument that overcomes the guarded heart. Colonna is setting up the moral turn.
- **"bright-eyed" (elicopidi)** — *helikōpis*, Homer's epithet for beautiful, quick-glancing eyes;
  applied to the nymphs, and next (p. 337) to Polia herself.
- After this the register changes entirely: p. 337 leaves the catalogue for Poliphilo's anxious
  reflection and the start of Polia's portrait.
