# Page 240 — Chapter XVIII

— in the season of Orion at setting, under the *pissatile* (?) body of the placid monster of Pan. Among which
(beauties), voluptuously, I beheld a delicious little valley, where was the delicate sepulchre of my soul — such
as Mausolus had not, with all his having, collocated. Being, then, I, for this, content — and the lacerated
heart conscious that the eyes, distracting it into whatever of those most-elegant parts, dispensed it moribund —
nonetheless I could not, for this, so much bridle the amorous and inflamed sighs, and so covertly castigate
(them), that I did not concede them their simulated sound to express itself.

By this such-made accident, immediately she too, provoked by the contagious love, and stirred, the lewd regards
most-placidly (special envy to the Sun) into me converted; and throughout I felt an irritating fire, itchingly
diffusing itself, into the lowest and interstitial parts, and even through all the capillary little veins,
seminarily to scatter itself. Whence, in her illustrious and notable features continually to contemplate, a
mellifluous sweetness, and solace, sweetly I heaped up. And there, some time, collided by disordered and
insatiable appetite, and by fiery and importunate stimulus gravely oppressed, with piteous words, full of
suasive and vehement prayer, secretly imploring, I appetited within me the desired kisses, sipping and fluid,
and sweetest, with vibrating (like a viper) and succulent little tongue — imagining to feel the extreme
sweetness of the savoury and little mouth (breathing-hole of odorous air, and mossy spirit, and freshest
breath) — and, feigning to enter into the hidden treasure of Venus, and there, mercurially, to steal the most-
precious jewels of parent nature.

Whence — alas, sighing — by that divine mother surrounded I found myself, and by the flame-bearing son circum-
walled, and by so most-beautiful a figure invaded, all henceforth morbid and infected by such notable
circumstances ornated and comely, and by the golden-fleeced head enticed (that whatever hair offered itself to
me a constricting snare, and chain), and beset by these torturing knots, and by the plenitude of such pleasant
pastures, and of amorous sweetness viscously baited — I could not, with whatever shrewd effort, resist the
accessory and invading ardours, and irritating thoughts; and, in me, the arrow-bearing love, having forced
itself, wholly disposed me — so insupportable a burning (patience overcome) to extinguish — and, spurned every
repugnant reason and mature counsel, in the solitary places, with Herculean audacity, to make assault, and the
divine and untouched Nymph, unbridled, to fore-attempt. But first, with sighing and precatory voice, of pity
impetrable, thus to say:

"Alas, divine-born Polia — in the present, for thee to die, eternal praise I esteem; and death more tolerable
and soft, and more glorious, with these thy delicate and swelling hands, would be to me the last end and demise.
For, the soul circumfused by so many tormenting ardours, ever more savagely quickening themselves, it, languid,
scorching, without intermission and pity they burn — so that never is left me to take a quiet hour, nor peace."

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Poliphilo's desire mounts to a **crisis of temptation**: gazing on Polia, his whole body
afire, "invaded" by her beauty and "ensnared" by her golden hair, he is moved by "arrow-bearing love" to make an
"assault with Herculean audacity" on the "untouched Nymph" — but checks himself, and instead begins a piteous
plea ("for thee to die I esteem eternal praise"). All secure; the psychological turn of the chapter.

- **The temptation, named honestly.** After the sanctified union and the blessing, Poliphilo is nonetheless
  overwhelmed by physical desire — imagining kisses, "entering the hidden treasure of Venus," and even an assault
  on Polia "in the solitary places." Colonna does not pretend the sacred rite has neutered desire; it names the
  crisis plainly. The restraint that follows (p. 241) is the point — the fidelity-thread tested at its sharpest.
- **beauty as snare, again.** Her hair "a constricting snare and chain," her sweetness "viscously baited": the
  recurring image of beauty as a trap (pp. 333, 341, 239) reaches its most dangerous pitch here, because now it
  tempts Poliphilo toward force. The plea that interrupts him is the beginning of his self-mastery.
- **"the delicate sepulchre of my soul"** — even in desire, Poliphilo calls Polia's body the "sepulchre of his
  soul" (surpassing Mausolus's tomb): the Polyandrion's funerary theme bleeds into the love-language. Love and
  death entwined, on the very shore of the tomb of love's victims.
- Poliphilo's restraint, and Polia's soothing, complete the chapter on p. 241.
