# Page 361 — Chapter XXIII

— the ready columns were raised, there were cut, perfectly excavated, letters of
the most ancient Greek, the shaft being three-quarters of a foot. In the hollows
of these there shone, laid in, words of refined silver. On the front face only
two letters were to be seen, intersected with companion emblems of gold and
elegantly polished; and then following, on the other faces, three and three,
they said this:

> ΩΣΠΕΡ ΣΠΙΝΘΗΡ ΚΗΛΗΘΜΟΣ
>
> *as a spark, so enchantment*

Each face was three feet, and from the golden bases up to the architrave the
height stood at seven feet. Of the artifice of this thing, marvellous and most
highly finished, I judge that its dignity will be more fittingly preserved by my
keeping silent than by my discoursing of it poorly and meagrely.

There, between the sapphire and the emerald column, hung on bent rings with
knotted loops the most beautiful little veiling curtain that fertile nature could
ever have devised to produce as a thing more pleasing to the gods — so beautiful
a weaving, and of such a material, that I should never know how to describe it;
but of a sandalwood colouring, woven with the loveliest flowering, and with four
Greek letters of gold subtly embroidered over it, **ΥΜΗΝ**, stretched out most
becomingly and heavenward. Let the marvellous curtain the Samians sent to Delphi
rightly yield place here. This appeared to my Polia as a most precious treasure,
and gracious; and it was this that veiled and hid the majestic and divine
presence of the venerable mother.

And so, both of us — Polia and I — kneeling face down upon our bending knees, the
divine lord Cupid gave the golden arrow to the nymph Synesia, and shrewdly made
her a sign that she should offer it to Polia, and that Polia with that fearful
arrow should rend and split the most noble curtain. But Polia, as though grieved
at the command for such a cutting and breaking, however subject she was to that
divine authority, seemed unpractised, and refused to consent. The lord in that
same moment, smiling, charged the nymph Synesia that she should hand it to the
nymph Philedia, and that she should then present it to me; and that what the
honeyed and most upright Polia did not dare to do, I — [?spellbound], and most
greedy to look upon the most holy mother — should carry out.

Whereupon no sooner had I taken hold of the divine instrument than, ringed about
with a blind flame and not refusing, but rather with an urgent and headlong
passion, I struck the little curtain. And as it split I saw that Polia was almost
saddened by it; and the emerald column, cracking, seemed as though it must
shatter entirely.

And behold, all at once I plainly see the divine form in the salt fountain,
brought forth out of that venerable majesty from which all beauty deliciously
flowed. Nor sooner had that unlooked-for sight —

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The narrative — Polia's refusal, the arrow passing from
Synesia to Philedia to Poliphilo, the tearing, the cracking column, the
revelation — is secure and is the point of the chapter. Three softer places: the
inscription's letter-count does not close (below), `thelithoro` is a coinage I
cannot place and is marked, and `salso fonte` is either a real detail or a slip.
**A reviewer should start with the inscription and with `thelithoro`.**

- **ΩΣΠΕΡ ΣΠΙΝΘΗΡ ΚΗΛΗΘΜΟΣ** — *hōsper spinthēr kēlēthmos*, "as a spark,
  enchantment": the smallest kindling grows to a rapture that holds you
  spellbound. It is the fountain's motto and a fair summary of the whole book.
  **The arithmetic does not close**: two letters on the front face plus three on
  each of six others gives twenty, and the phrase has more. Either a face
  carries more than Colonna says, or the transcription has lost something.
  Translated as given; flagged for the facsimile.
- **`thelithoro`** — a Colonna coinage, probably built on θέλγω, to charm or
  bewitch, and so "charmed" or "spellbound"; possibly instead on θέλω, "willing."
  Marked `[?spellbound]` rather than resolved.
- **`salso fonte`** — "the salt fountain." Venus is sea-born, so salt water in
  her fountain is meaningful rather than careless, and it makes the *fonte* the
  sea in miniature. Kept literal.
- **ΥΜΗΝ** — Hymen, god of marriage, embroidered on the curtain that hides the
  goddess. Colonna says four letters, which is right for ΥΜΗΝ.
- **Synesia and Philedia** — σύνεσις, understanding, and φιληδία, love of
  pleasure. The allegory is exact and worth not flattening: Understanding offers
  the arrow to Polia, who will not tear the veil; Cupid smiles and routes it
  through Love-of-Pleasure to Poliphilo, who will.
- **The curtain the Samians sent to Delphi** — a famous votive hanging; Colonna's
  habitual move of ranking his invention above the best-attested real object.
- **`copellaceo argento`** — cupelled silver, silver refined by cupellation, the
  purest available. The letters are inlaid metal in cut grooves.
