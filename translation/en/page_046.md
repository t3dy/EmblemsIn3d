Beneath the aforesaid beam ran down the top, or pinnacled summit, of the frontispiece of the preserved
gate. Where, between its slope and the lining of the beam, that space imitated the figure of a
stairway, which shows a triangle of unequal sides, or ribs.

Beneath the beam, in the space between the columns, there was sustained upon marvellous modillions,
with an artificial hollow between — in this figure described, as much as could embrace the greater
space — two roundels hollowed out, in imitation of a dish bound about at the lips with waving-work,
gullets and scotias; where in the topmost stepping, in the middle of the lineaments drawn about it,
there swelled a Torus, nobly clothed in oaken foliage, joined one beneath another, bound about with
grooved thongs, with fruits scattered.

Within which resided two venerable images, freed from the vase, that is to say from the hollow, from
the midriff upward. The breast part covered with a mantle over the left shoulder in an antique knot.
With shaggy beards and laurelled brows, with a bearing worthy and majestic.

In the square projection of the Zophorus above the columns written of, in front, was this carving. An
Eagle with wings spread, with clawed talons, rested upon a swelling little bundle of fronds and fruits
hanging in the middle. The slender extremities of which, on this side and on that, were held suspended,
bound about with straggling tendrils, of a working almost pierced through.

Therefore the conspicuous Gate, standing free in the flat of the levelling between the columns, of
marble jointly panelled, was situated with the highest approbation. For which cause, the required
showing of the more principal members of the said magnificent gate being somewhat fitted, it seems to
me opportune in what follows to explain its pleasing and most comely ornaments. Because for the
architect the *being* presents itself as harder than the *being well*. That is to say, that first it
belongs to him above all to dispose the solid, and to define in his mind (as I said above) the
universal fabric, rather than the ornaments, which are accessory to the principal.

Therefore to the first, the fertile skill of one alone is required. But to the second many handworkers,
or unlearned operatives — called by the Greeks Ergatai — necessarily come together. Who, as has been
said, are the instruments of the Architect.
