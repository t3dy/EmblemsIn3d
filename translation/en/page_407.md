# Page 407 — Chapter XXVII

— unfortunate Polydorus to Aeneas the Pious. And with greater agony than Andromeda at the shore finding myself, and with
greater terror than Aristomenes seeing Panthia and Meroe (over him). Incontinently, on me stretching the malefic and
sinewy arms, sacrilegious and profane, with the hands sanguinary and filthy, and polluted and besmeared — by my blond
hair, dishonouring, snarling, dishevelling me, they began impiously to draw (me), without any clemency (which, in them,
not the least was prompt). More fright and terror they put in me than, to the chaste Lucretia, Sextus Tarquinius, with the
unsheathed sword in hand, the opprobrious death threatening. Whence, without spirit, beyond belief bewildered, at one hour
marvel and fear struck into me the dire and sanguinary men — insomuch that, evacuated and emptied each vein, to the
dolorous and mourning heart (the blood) concurred. Made more timid than a doe, and more fearful than the long-eared and
timorous hare (that, among the dense shrubs and rushy tufts lurking, hears, round about it, the barkings of the savaging
and fierce dogs) — for which thing, without delay, torrentially I began, with high 'wah!', to weep, and, dishevelled by
them, 'O me! O me!' to cry. And, wishing to resist the incendious dragging, I, as much as I availed — their arms seized,
slackening — forced myself, and, with the hurt and all-debilitated forces, strove to mitigate the violent drawing of the
furious men (harder than Sciron, son of Neptune, and harsher than Phineus, and than Polydectes of Seriphos). And, by no
prayer and supplication, would they cease, but intended, thereby, to draw me from my already-wet bed. But, 'O me! O me!'
— for God's (sake), mercy and succour and supplies calling — and with the naked feet, and with both the hands, resisting.
And they, more violent, rabidly angered, threatening, offending my smell, with great displeasure, of an emetic putor
which, moving, exhaled from the offscouring of their rancid and putulent flesh, extraneous and insupportable, that,
nauseous, evaporated — with an odious aspect, with the streaked forehead, funereally terrifying me.

At last, enduring, anguished and by mourning afflicted, in this long contrast and altercation wearied, and in bitter
weepings strongly perturbed and lifeless — and, perhaps agitating myself, and turning myself over my uncontaminated
little bed — so much that my piteous **Nurse**, who soporously slept, feeling, by chance, my somnolent movements, and
ill-understood stammerings, awoke; and, expergefacted, roused me from the furial sleep and unquiet nocturne. Without
delay, into her arms she embraced me, removing me from my assiduous (torment), and exciting me: "Polia, my beautiful
daughter — Polia, my dearest little soul — Polia, my life, and my little blood," saying, "what things are these that
thou feelest?" Suddenly removed from my eyes the dolorous, maledict, and unhappy sleep, and the fearful vision —

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The dream's climax: the executioners **drag Polia from her bed by her hair**, and she — "more
fearful than a hare among the hounds," struggling, screaming "O me! O me!" for mercy — is at last **woken by her nurse**,
who embraces her ("Polia, my beautiful daughter, my life, my little blood") and rouses her from the nightmare. All secure.

- **Dragged by the hair — the punishment begun.** The executioners seize Polia by the very blond hair that was her pride
  (p. 386) and her family's hubris — the beauty misused for cruelty becomes the handle of her punishment. She screams for
  the mercy she denied Poliphilo (p. 396): the *contrapasso* is exact, and she feels it. Her terror ("more than Lucretia
  under Tarquin's sword") is total.
- **the nurse's rescue.** The nurse wakes her at the last instant — the human intervention that breaks the vision before
  it can "execute" her. Her tender address ("my daughter… my life… my little blood") is the first *love* Polia has
  received (or been open to) in the whole account: the warmth that begins to thaw her (cf. the tears, p. 403). Grace
  comes through the humblest human bond.
- **the grotesque, sustained.** The executioners' stench and filth (p. 406–407) make love's punishers physically
  repellent — the sensory opposite of the perfumed, beautiful world of Book I. Polia's conversion is driven by disgust
  and terror, then completed by the nurse's counsel (p. 408).
- Polia wakes, and the nurse's saving counsel closes the chapter (p. 408).
