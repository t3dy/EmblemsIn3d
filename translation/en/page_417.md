## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The end of the nurse's counsel (**go to the priestess of Venus, confess, and be cured**) and the
opening of **chapter XXIX** — its argument (Polia begins to fall in love, revives the dead Poliphilo, is driven out by
Diana's nymphs, and finds him at Venus's temple), and the dawn after the night of visions. All secure; the turn to action.

- **The prescribed cure: confession to Venus's priestess.** The nurse's practical counsel: Polia must go to the priestess
  of Venus, "openly confess her contumacy," and receive "salutiferous instruction." This is the therapeutic structure of
  Book II — the cold heart, thawed by fear (the visions) and reason (the counsel), is *cured* by confession and by love.
  It mirrors Book I's temple-rite (the priestess who unites the lovers, ch. XVII): the same Venus-cult, from Polia's side.
- **the argument's whole arc.** Chapter XXIX delivers the conversion in full: Polia falls in love, *raises Poliphilo from
  the dead* by her tears and embrace, is expelled by Diana's nymphs, and is reunited with him at Venus's temple. The dead
  lover revived by the converted beloved is the miracle that answers her crime (his death, p. 395). Book II's climax.
- **Hebe's warning.** The nurse's last exemplum — Hebe, who tripped serving the gods and was replaced by Ganymede — warns
  Polia to approach the gods carefully. Even innocent offence to the divine has consequences; how much more Polia's cruelty.
- The morning breaks, and Polia — alone — feels the first stirring of love (p. 418).
