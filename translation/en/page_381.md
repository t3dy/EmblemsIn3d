# Page 381 — Chapter XXV (Book II begins)

*(Chapter argument:)*

> POLIPHILO BEGINS THE SECOND BOOK OF HIS HYPNEROTOMACHIA, IN WHICH POLIA AND HE, CONVERSING, NARRATE BY TURNS
> IN WHAT MANNER AND BY WHAT VARIOUS CHANCE THEIR FALLING-IN-LOVE (CAME ABOUT).
>
> HERE THE DIVINE POLIA NARRATES HER NOBLE AND ANCIENT ORIGIN; AND HOW, BY HER PREDECESSORS, TREVISO WAS BUILT;
> AND OF THE LELIAN GENS (SHE) DESCENDED; AND BY WHAT MODE, UNAWARE AND IGNORANT, HER BELOVED POLIPHILO
> IMPROPERLY FELL IN LOVE WITH HER.

*(Polia speaks:)*

My feeble voice, O gracious and divine Nymphs, will come to your benign audience as discordant and inelegant as
the terrific raucousness of the diving Aesacus to the sweet song of the plaintive Philomela. Nonetheless, wishing
I, with all my slender efforts of the intellect, and with my scanty sufficiency, to satisfy your pleasant petitions,
I shall not desist from (my) power. Which (petitions) — every hesitation set aside — they, more than would elsewhere
be congruous, worthily merit a more copious river of eloquence, with far more rounded elegance and more adorned
polish of pronouncement than in me, by any pact, is found, to attain their gracious affect. But to you, celibate
Nymphs, and to me somewhat — however confusedly and inelegantly stammering — I shall, in some little portion, have
gratified much (enough), when, willing and devout to your desires and demand, I shall present myself rather with a
soul not mediocrely prompt, humble seeming, than with enucleated, terse and comely eloquence pleasing. The ancient,
then, and most-antique genealogy, and lineage, and my fatal love, garrulously I shall begin to weave. Whence,
already being in your venerable conventual sight, and seeing myself sterile and fasting of eloquence — and (before)
so outstanding and divine a company of you, O Nymphs, sedulous handmaids of kindled Cupid, and in so benign and
delightful and sacred a site, of sincere airs and flower-bearing breaths breathed-upon — I, fittingly compelled to
assume a venerable daring, and a tranquil fear of speaking. Then, before all, give pardon, O most-beautiful and most-
blessed Nymphs, to this my babbling, and to the female and earth-born and pusillanimous efforts, if it happen that,
in some part, I incautiously —

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The opening of **Book II** (chapter XXV): its argument (Polia and Poliphilo will now narrate,
by turns, how they fell in love — beginning with Polia's own account), and the start of **Polia's first-person
narration** — a modest *exordium* to the nymphs, disclaiming eloquence, promising to "weave" her ancient lineage and
"fatal love." All secure; the great structural pivot of the whole book.

- **The book turns to Polia's voice.** Book I was Poliphilo's dream, seen through his eyes; Book II is **Polia telling
  her own side** — her family, her cruelty to Poliphilo, her punishment and conversion, her own experience of the love
  the first book showed from outside. It is the earliest sustained *female first-person* narration in the Italian
  romance tradition. The register drops from Poliphilo's dense ekphrasis to a more direct (if still ornate) storytelling
  voice. Nothing in the 3D world depends on Book II, but it completes the translation.
- **the modesty-topos.** Polia opens with the conventional disclaimer of eloquence ("my feeble voice… stammering… female
  and earth-born efforts") before the nymphs — the humility-formula of a speaker, here gendered. Colonna gives his
  heroine a rhetorically self-aware voice.
- **the frame.** Polia narrates *to the nymphs* (the "handmaids of Cupid," at the fountain of ch. XXIII): Book II is her
  testimony within the dream's own setting. Poliphilo is her listener and, at intervals, co-narrator (per the argument).
- **Aesacus and Philomela** — the diving-bird's croak against the nightingale's song: Polia's habitual self-deprecation,
  and the transformation-birds (cf. p. 310) recurring in her very first sentence.
