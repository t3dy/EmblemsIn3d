## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Poliphilo **finds Polia alone in the temple** and — "swift as a lion at its prey," then "liquefied
like wax at the fire," lifeless — begins his final plea: he has loved and *adored her as a Goddess*, "immolated his
holocaust heart," yet she is "cruel, an expultress of my every good," worse to him than Juno to the Trojans, than hail to
tender leaves; and he strains "with every sweetness" to soften her "morbid, savage heart." All secure; the death-scene,
from his side.

- **The same scene, from his eyes.** This is the temple confrontation Polia narrated (chs. XXVI, pp. 389–396) — now
  from Poliphilo's view: his desperate approach, his collapse ("liquefied like wax… lifeless"), his final plea. The two
  accounts converge on the identical moment, the structural payoff of the double narration. Where she gave her frozen
  cruelty, he gives his melting devotion — wax to her stone, the two temperaments meeting at his death.
- **"my holocaust heart immolated."** He casts his love as a *sacrifice* — his heart burnt whole (holocaust) to Polia as
  to a goddess, "with per-injury of the (other) Gods adored." His love is idolatrous worship (cf. p. 440), and his death
  the completed sacrifice. The religious framing makes his death an offering — which Venus's cult (Book I) will accept and
  reward (his revival, the union).
- **wax to fire — the melting lover.** He "liquefies like wax at the fire" and collapses — the exact image of his soft
  heart (p. 438) against her stone. He melts (dies) where she hardens; her later warmth (p. 421) will re-melt him back to
  life. The wax/stone, warm/cold polarity resolves in the revival.
- His death and revival (already given by Polia, ch. XXIX) complete from his side on p. 454+.
