## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** **Poliphilo's plea to the priestess** begins: encouraged by Polia's changed aspect, he asks the
priestess of Venus — "the ultimate refuge… true remedy-maker," who exists "to re-unite discordant lovers into one will"
— to intercede with Venus so the two be bound "one sole bond." All secure; his petition for the union.

- **The priestess as the uniter of lovers.** Poliphilo names her office exactly: she serves Venus "to re-unite the inept
  and discordant souls, into one will and consent, the lovers." This is the very function Book I showed her perform (the
  well-rite that joined the lovers, ch. XVII, pp. 216–218). Book II gives Poliphilo's own petition that set that rite in
  motion. The two accounts of the same priestess dovetail.
- **the plea's structure.** Poliphilo asks the priestess to *pray to Venus on their behalf* (completed p. 430): that Cupid
  wound Polia's stony heart so she love him, making two hearts one. It is the request the whole dream-quest of Book I
  answers (the union at Cythera, the Fountain of Venus). His confidence ("I am come… because thou alone art able") frames
  the priestess as the lovers' advocate before the goddess.
- **love as inequality to be equalised.** Poliphilo diagnoses the lovers' trouble as "inequality" — he wounded, she not
  (Cupid's "lictorial lancing" fell only on him). The priestess is asked to *equalise* the love (make Polia love as he
  loves). This is Book II's insight in his mouth: love must be mutual to be whole, and the temple's rite makes it so.
- His full petition (Venus commanding Cupid to strike Polia) completes on p. 430.
