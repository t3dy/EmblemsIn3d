# Page 4 — Front matter

*(The rest of the anonymous elegy to the reader, begun on page 3. Latin.)*

> — symmetry, and whatever makes proud roofs.
> Here you will see the cultivated palaces of kings, the adornments
>    of nymphs, fountains, and outstanding feasts.
> Here is the two-coloured dance of robbers, and the whole life
>    of men set out in labyrinthine darkness.
> Here read what he says of the threefold majesty of the Thunderer,
>    and what he himself performed at the three gates.
> What form Polia had, how adorned; then behold
>    the four heavenly triumphs of Jove.
> Besides these, he tells the various affections of love,
>    and its works, and how fiercely that God rages.
> Here Pomona triumphs together with Vertumnus.
>    Here too are rites performed to the Lampsacan god.
> Here is a huge temple, more perfect than any art,
>    and the manifold rites of the ancient sacred things.
> Soon you will see another temple, spoiled by the teeth of time;
>    and here very many things pleasing to your mind:
> the seats of Tartarus, many epitaphs, and the boat
>    in which the boy of Venus is carried over the vast straits;
> and the highest honours reverently shown to him
>    by all the gods, as many as the waters hold.
> Here see Cythera, marked out with gardens and meadows,
>    in the middle of which curved theatres lie open.
> And here you may behold the triumph of Cupid.
>    Here is the fountain, and the venerable form of the Paphian Goddess;
> and you will read what yearly rites are kept around the tomb
>    of beloved Adonis, by Venus and by the Naiads.
> This is the series of matters contained in the first volume:
>    these are the new dreams of the divine Poliphilo.
> Where she was born, of what stock, of what parents sprung,
>    is Polia — the book that follows sets out thereafter.
> And who first founded the walls of Treviso.
>    From there it has the whole sequence of a long love.
> Lastly the book is furnished with a long appendix,
>    which I do not think, reader, it will be thankless to read.
> There are very many things besides; it wearied me to report them all.
>    Take what the horn of plenty has generously given.
> Behold, the book delights and profits. If you scorn both,
>    believe the fault is not the book's, but yours.
>
>                         *The end.*

## Notes

**Confidence: high.** Read against the facsimile by a second reader, 2026-09-20 (Siena
O.III.38, f. 2v). The Adonis couplet was mistranslated by the first pass and has been
corrected; everything else on the page stands.

- **The last couplet is the best thing in the front matter**, and it is pure Aldine swagger:
  *if you get nothing from this book, the fault is yours.* Set it against Crasso's careful
  reassurance on page 2 that the unlearned "should not despair". The publisher soothes; the poet
  dares.
- **This is a station list.** Nearly every item can be pointed at in the world this project has
  built: the palaces and nymphal fountains, the human chess match (*the two-coloured dance of
  robbers* — the ballet of black and white pieces, played as a game of *latrunculi*), the
  labyrinth of human life, the three gates and the threefold majesty of Jove, the four triumphs,
  Vertumnus and Pomona, the rite of the Lampsacan god (**Priapus**, whose cult was at Lampsacus),
  the Temple of Venus, the ruined Polyandrion with its Tartarus mosaic and its epitaphs, Cupid's
  boat, Cythera with its theatre, the fountain of the Paphian goddess, and the tomb of Adonis.
  **The elegy walks the same road the tour walks**, five hundred and twenty-seven years earlier.
- **"The two-coloured dance of robbers."** *Bicolor chorea latronum* — the chess ballet of
  chapter XI, played by dancers in black and white to three measures of music. *Latrunculi*
  ("little robbers") was the Roman board game; the Italian summary on page 5 calls it *gioco de
  latroni* likewise. See `CHESSBOARD.md`.
- **"More perfect than any art."** *Omni perfectius arte*, of the Temple of Venus Physizoa —
  the building Colonna gives as a *rule* rather than a size (`DIMENSIONS.md` §4).
- **"Naiadasque leges" — corrected 2026-09-20.** The first pass read the last word of the elegy
  twice over, as the verb *leges* ("you will read") and as the noun *leges* ("laws") at once,
  and produced "yearly by Venus — and you shall read the Naiads' laws". There are no Naiads'
  laws anywhere in the book to point at, which is the first sign something is wrong. The
  facsimile puts the comma after *Venerem*: *Annua per Venerem, Naiadas'que leges.* So *per*
  governs both nouns — the rites are kept **by Venus and the Naiads** — and *leges* is the
  second-person future of *lego*, the main verb the whole couplet has been waiting for: **you
  will read** what annual rites are kept at Adonis's tomb. The proof is on page 7, because the
  Italian terza rima is this elegy translated by a contemporary: *che hor celebrato gli è lo
  anniversario / per le Naiade e Venus mischinella* — celebrated for him by the Naiads and poor
  little Venus. Both lines point at the same scene, chapter XXIV, where the nymphs tell
  Poliphilo about the anniversary.
- **The appendix.** The *long appendix* is Book II, in which Polia takes over the telling. The
  elegist treats it as an addendum; Polia's own account is a third of the work and contradicts
  Poliphilo's on several points, which is one reason the two-book structure has been read as
  deliberate rather than accretive.
