# Page 65 — Chapter VI

I gave urgent effort to escaping the peril to be dreaded, and to saving my brief and scant contaminated
life — or else, by this violence without any remedy, sorrowfully gasping to die, and without further
delay; for I did not know, most confused, what to do with myself, wandering, fugitive and wayward
through uncertain places and pathless byways. And my legs now weakened and numb, and every bodily
power shattered and languishing, half dead, and altogether bewildered and as it were ghost-ridden.

Brought nevertheless to this tearful pass, having suppliantly invoked (my last refuge) the gods above
and almighty, and my good Genius, with a guiltless mind — they having perhaps in this miserable case,
by their perennial pity, some fortunate care of me — behold, I began to discover a little light coming.
Towards which, alas, with what alacrity swiftly making my way, I saw a hanging lamp burning eternally
before a devout Altar. Which, so far as I could then discern in a moment, was five feet high and twice
as broad, with three golden images sitting.

Here, disappointed by the condition of the light, I was struck with no little religious horror at those
venerable darknesses, in which little was seen, however much the illuminating lamp burned — because
light is an enemy to thick and bad air. And always with intent ears, and never empty of my familiar
terror; but somewhat the blackened statues appeared, and round about there offered themselves the vast
and uncertain openings and fearful Passages underground, or under the mountain, supported here and
there, and distributed in infinite places: many greatest four-square and six-square piers, and in other
places eight-sided props, scarcely discerned by the weak light, aptly set beneath in proportion to
bearing the excessive vastness of the pressing Pyramid above.

Here, praying a little while, without delay I made above all things towards unknown flight. Whereupon,
thus half dead, no sooner had I run past the most holy Altar than there appeared to me besides a little
of the desired light, which shone faintly as through a most subtle breathing-hole seen as a funnel. O
with what feasting and with what gladness of my exhilarated heart I beheld it! And hastening merrily
towards it without other thought — perhaps with greater nimbleness than Canistius and Philonides — no
sooner did I see it with such unbridled gladness and longing than I called back most gladly my
repudiation of that thankless and troublesome life; successively clearing my disturbed mind and
fluctuating spirit, and somewhat refreshed and as it were reassured; and my emptied heart, drained of
love, somewhat recalled, quickened again with resprouting love, and altogether filled; and every lost
and exiled thought fitted itself again to its former work. And now more fixed upon my lovable Polia,
