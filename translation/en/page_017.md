There was my terror to hear from that quarter, now and again, the hissing ruin of trees, and a crash

in the density of the trees and the enclosed air.

Wishing therefore, I Poliphilo, terrified and afflicted, having escaped so much horror, to drink up
the longed-for waters upon those green banks, I lowered myself upon my knees; and drawing my hands
together into an enclosure, with the palm hollowed, I made of them a most welcome drinking vessel.
Which, dipped in the spring and filled with water, I made ready to offer to my raging and panting
mouth, and to cool the dryness of my burning breast. More welcome then to me than the Hypanis and the
Ganges to the Indians, than the Tigris and Euphrates to the Armenians; nor is the Nile so welcome to
the Ethiopian peoples, nor to the Egyptians its flooding that drinks into the baked soil; nor yet
Eridanus to the Ligurian peoples, as those most acceptable and fresh banks offered themselves to me.
Nor was the spring shown by the fleeing Ram so exceedingly welcome to Father Liber.

It happened that no sooner was I about to bring the awaited and desirable waters, enclosed in my
hollowed hand, to my open mouth, than in that instant I heard a Dorian singing — which I cannot
persuade myself Thamyras the Thracian ever found — penetrating through the little caverns of my ears,
and passing through to my restless heart so sweet, so soft and so harmonious. With a voice not of
earth, with such harmony, with such incredible sonority, with such unaccustomed proportion. Alas, how
could it ever be imagined? For without doubt this thing exceeds the power of telling.

The sweetness of it and the delight offered me far more pleasure than the little draught that was
offering itself. So much so that the water drawn up, held within the enclosure of the intervals of my
teeth — my understanding as it were senseless and already stupefied, and my appetite laid to sleep,
no faculty gainsaying it — the knots being loosed, was spilt upon the moist earth.
