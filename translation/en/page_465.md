# Page 465 — Chapter XXXVIII

*Poliphilo here ends his Hypnerotomachia, grieving that the sleep was no longer,
and that the Sun was envious, and made it day.*

So unlooked-for a delight snatched away, that angelic spirit taken from my eyes,
and the sweet and gentle sleeping drawn out of my drowsy limbs as I woke — in
that instant, alas, ah me, amorous readers, I ached all over from the hard
clasping of that blessed image, that happy presence, that venerable majesty,
which left me and deserted me between wondrous sweetness and piercing
bitterness. When from my gaze there departed that most joyful sleep and that
divine shade, the mysterious apparition broken off, scattered, and carried away
— by which I had been led and lifted to thoughts so high, so sublime, so
piercing.

And then, as it happened, the Sun, stirred to envy by so blessed a dream, coming
to plunder the glorious night like a public enemy and an informer against the
divine Mother, came suddenly with his shining splendours and painted the
whitening Dawn a rosy colour, the long night dispelled. And the new-made day
being lit and set between us, I was left crammed and utterly filled with a sweet
and subtle deception.

Consider, then, what livid spite he would have had, if I had truly felt, in the
enjoying of them, the very voluptuous delights of so shapely and divine a
damsel, so matchless a nymph — he who would not endure (because, I judge, with a
goddess it is not permitted) to grant me the long night of Alcmena. Ah me, why
did he not exchange a little of his swiftness for a little sloth, for the sake of
my restored rest, and overrun his appointed hour by just so much? And why was
the Stygian sleep out of curious Psyche's box not laid upon me then?

And there Philomela, weeping before daybreak, hidden among thorny brambles and
in thickets close with the dark foliage of small oaks wound about with twining
honeysuckle, was pouring out the violence of that adulterous and faithless
Tereus, saying with tuneful chatter: *Tereus, Tereus* — [?have I been wronged].
Sighing, risen and released from the sweet sleep, I woke of a sudden into the
small hours, saying: **Farewell then, Polia.**

*At Treviso, where wretched Poliphilo was held fast in the most comely little
thongs of Polia's love. 1467, on the Kalends of May.*

## Notes

**Confidence: high.** The narrative Italian here is unusually plain for Colonna
and the classical allusions (Alcmena, Psyche's box, Philomela and Tereus) are all
securely identifiable. The one weak point is the corrupt Greek noted below,
which is isolated to three words and does not affect the sense of the passage.

- **`eme ebiasato?`** — the transcription reads `Tereus Tereus, eme ebiasato?`
  and is corrupt here. The underlying Greek is almost certainly a form of
  ἐβιάσατο, "he forced/violated (me)" — Philomela naming her rape as she sings.
  Rendered as *[?have I been wronged]* pending a check against the facsimile.
  **Verify before this page moves from `drafted` to `verified`.**
- **`Omè Heu me`** — the Italian exclamation immediately doubled by the Latin.
  Kept as "alas, ah me" so the doubling survives.
- **`la longa nocte di Alcmena`** — Jupiter stopped the sun to lengthen the night
  he spent with Alcmena, fathering Hercules. Left unglossed; the allusion is the
  point.
- **`Tarvisii`** — Treviso, where the historical Francesco Colonna's Polia is
  placed, and the date the acrostic's love affair is dated to. Kept in the
  colophon's own voice.
