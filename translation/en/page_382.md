## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Polia's tearful preface: an invocation of the **sacred fountain** (the mirror of Venus's beauty,
where the nymphs sit — the fountain of ch. XXIII), her confession that her "first stock" included one metamorphosed into
a river (an "unhappy" origin), and her warning that she will tell **two marvels — her own cruelty (a "swinish ferocity")
and the greatest love in the world** — dating the start to the age of "Ilia Silvia" (Rhea Silvia, mother of Romulus). All
secure; the frame and stakes of Book II.

- **"Two marvels: my cruelty, and the greatest love."** Polia states Book II's theme: her own **cruel refusal** of
  Poliphilo (which will be punished), and the **immense love** it nonetheless produced. Book II is, in part, her
  confession and repentance — the female counterpart to Poliphilo's fidelity. The word she uses of herself — "swinish
  ferocity, feminine atrocity" — is startlingly self-condemning. This is the moral engine of her narrative.
- **the mythic dating.** Polia sets her family's rise in the age of **Rhea Silvia** (Ilia, the Vestal mother of Romulus
  and Remus), tying her lineage to Rome's origins — and, by the palm sprung from the Vestal fire, to the founding of a
  city (Treviso, p. 383). Colonna gives his heroine a legendary Roman ancestry.
- **the fountain as Venus's mirror.** The fount reflects "the most-excellent part of the goddess's body" — the mirror-
  motif again (Eros Katoptron, p. 266; the obsidian, p. 352), here the sacred spring where Polia testifies. Her tears,
  though, must be held back in this "place of felicity" where weeping is "interdicted."
- **the transformation-origin** — an ancestor turned to a river ("O deplorable metamorphosis"): Polia's family carries the
  Ovidian metamorphosis-curse from its root, foreshadowing her own near-transformation. The book's pervasive
  metamorphosis-theme is now given a personal, genealogical form.
