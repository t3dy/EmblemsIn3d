The incense-sacrifice finished, the infuled pontiff — on the breast ornated with a marvellous and mysterious
golden fibulation, with the adornment of a precious stone *Synochitis* — somewhat of the holy ash, to each,
with a little golden *simpulum*, then with much devotion gave. The ash accepted, in crowds they went out of the
temple, with observed veneration, to the rushy shores of the near sea (as thou seest). And, in a reed the
sacred ash placed, out into the deep they blew it, with religious superstition, with high-sounding voice, and
inelegantly exclaiming, and with feminine ululations confusedly intermixed, and saying: "Thus perish whoever
shall be the cause and conscious (author) of his lover's death."

Then, when in such manner they had scattered the ash into the sea, the reed thrown, having spat three times into
the said sea, three times saying "Fu, fu, fu," they returned, festive, with other roses — those, throughout all
the temple, disseminating, and especially upon the sepulchres (with funereal weeping), which, in order, in the
temple were situated — singing lugubrious sepulchral and tearful songs, sounding with sacrificial and kite-like
tibiae.

Newly, they set, each with their fellow-countrymen, in a circle upon the pavement, the tables and the viands, and
whatever edible by those brought, in one communicating exposed, with the Salian feast-fare. And there, with holy
rite, they made the *silicernium* (funeral feast); the superfluous then, the Manes being called, at the sepulchral
altars they left; and, beyond this anniversary, they held the secular games.

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Polia's account of the **ancient rites of the Polyandrion** — the yearly funeral festival
for those who died of love: sacrifices of black sheep to three-bodied Pluto, the *lectisternia*, flames quenched
with roses, ashes given to the mourners and **cast into the sea** ("thus perish whoever caused his lover's death"),
the triple spitting, the sepulchral songs, and the *silicernium* (funeral banquet) among the tombs. All secure; a
dense antiquarian reconstruction of a Roman festival of the dead.

- **The rite of the love-dead.** The Polyandrion's cult is the dark inverse of the Temple of Venus's: where
  Venus's rite sanctifies fulfilled love with a rose-bush and shared fruit (pp. 231–233), the Polyandrion mourns
  love's casualties with black sheep, sea-cast ashes, and a curse on those who caused a lover's death. Both use
  **roses** — there for blessing, here for the graves. The world's polyandrion carries this funerary meaning.
- **"Thus perish whoever causes his lover's death."** The festival's imprecation, cast with the ashes into the
  sea: the Polyandrion is not only a tomb but a tribunal — it curses the cruel beloved whose disdain killed the
  lover. A resonant line for the world/novel's polyandrion (it names the moral stakes of the whole love-quest).
- **the Roman festival apparatus** — *lectisternia* (couches spread for the gods), *silicernium* (the funeral
  meal), the Manes (ancestral spirits), the secular games, the reed-and-ash sea-rite, the triple spitting
  (apotropaic): Colonna reconstructs a complete *Parentalia*-style festival with scholarly precision.
- The priest's formula of dismissal ("Illicet") closes the rite on p. 238.
