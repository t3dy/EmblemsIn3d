# Page 177 — Chapter XV

*The Nymph eloquently declares to Poliphilo the multitude of the loving young men, and of the divine
amorous girls: who they were and how they were loved by the gods; and he saw the choirs of the divine
poets singing.*

No one of so unwearied an eloquence would ever aptly fit himself to come out and escape from setting
forth the divine secrets plentifully and fully. Nor could he expressly tell, and with words sufficiently
express, with how divine a pomp, unceasing triumphs, unfailing glory, festive gladness and happy
dancing, these four unvisited six-horse cars of memorable show were surrounded.

Besides the renowned young men and the thronging troop of countless and most joyful Nymphs — more
prudent and grave and shrewd than the tenderness of their years — with their most acceptable lovers of
downy and beardless cheeks. In some the first shining down crept over their cheeks; they kept festival
cheerfully and deliciously. Many having their torches kindled and burning. Some I saw bearing shrines.

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The rubric to chapter XV and the opening of the great crowd of lovers. The
prose is secure and *Pastophore* is rightly the shrine-bearers. Medium for two things: the chapter
rubric is again set as an italic paragraph rather than in capitals, as on pp. 94, 117, 147 and 153;
and the page's long opening period — a rhetorical disclaimer that no eloquence could tell what
follows — is turned into two English sentences whose logic ("would ever aptly fit himself to come
out and escape from setting forth") does not survive the transposition.
