# Page 219 — Chapter XVII

Which thing the infecund and fasting tongue would not know how to gather, nor so many fit words to beg,
that I could condignly express even a little of what the enkindled heart made of it, in so sweetest a flame
as, in every part, o'erwove it. I remained, then, like one fallen of the Epilepsy. Ultimately, these
amorous and holy gestures and ceremonial rites — in so singular and choice a sweetness and incredible
delight, by love, expedited — I found myself almost in an unthought experience of dying content.

The Hierophant said: "Let us proceed, Polia, then, to the completory of the inner sacrifices of our
sacral undertaking." Now toward the round and blind Sacello — situated directly opposite the door of the
magnificent temple, and with it artfully joined and contiguous — all, composedly, went. Fabricated of
antiquarian and unwonted make, and noble material.

Which was all of stone, diligently re-squared to the form, of precious **Phengite** marvellously built,
with a cupola'd and round roof, of a single and solid rock of the said stone. Such was not, of such
marvel, the Sacello of the isle of Chennim in Egypt, nor that of the celebrated Sacred (place) of Ravenna.
Which stone, of such miraculous nature, that — not being windowed, but all obtuse (closed), and having only
the golden valves — throughout, clearly, it was illuminated (a secret absorbed by it, hidden from our
cognition, from its parent (the sun)); and therefore thus is it denominated. In this interval, two of those
virgins, unburdened, and by precept gone, carried, with sincere veneration: one, a pair of whitest male
Swans, grateful in the auspices, and a most-ancient little urn with sea-water; and the other, a pair of
candid Turtledoves, by the feet bound together with crimson silk, upon a wicker basket full of vermilion
roses and oyster-shells — and, near the golden valves, upon a sacred and quadrangular *anclabris* (offering-
table) disposed, devout and venerating, let them repose.

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The move to the inner rite: Poliphilo near-swooning from sweetness ("like one fallen
of the Epilepsy… dying content"); the priestess leading all to the **round blind Sacello** — the Adytum,
built of self-illuminating **phengite** stone (windowless yet luminous); and the offerings borne for the
inner sacrifice: white swans, sea-water, and turtledoves on a basket of roses and oyster-shells. All secure.

- **The self-lit sanctuary.** The Adytum is a domed rotunda hewn from *phengite* — a translucent stone
  (Pliny: Nero's phengite-lined temple that glowed shut) — so it is fully lit though windowless, "a secret
  hidden from our cognition." A gorgeous, buildable feature: an inner sanctum that glows from its own walls.
  It answers the translucent *sogobrina* windows (p. 199) and the alabaster theatre (p. 350): light through
  stone is the temple's signature.
- **the offerings of Venus.** Swans and turtledoves (Venus's birds), sea-water (the sea-born goddess), roses
  and oyster-shells (her flower and her shell): a precisely Venerean sacrifice-set, laid on an *anclabris*
  (the Roman offering-table). Ready iconography for the world's sanctuary.
- **"like one fallen of the Epilepsy"** — Poliphilo's swoon of sweetness after the union (p. 218): the
  emotional aftermath, rendered in the period's medical simile (the "sacred disease"). The rite's climax has
  physically overcome him.
- **the *completorium*** — the priestess calls the inner rite the "completory" (completion) of the sacrifice:
  the union at the well (chs. XVII) is now sealed by an inner offering in the Adytum (Polia's prayer at the
  gem-altar, pp. 220–221).
