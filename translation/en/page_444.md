> THE FIRST EPISTLE, WHICH POLIPHILO NARRATES HAVING WRITTEN TO HIS POLIA; AND SHE, NOT MOVING IN THE LEAST, HE SENT HER

*(Poliphilo's first letter to Polia:)*

"Most-avid, and supremely most-cupidinous, to reveal somewhat the not-mediocre flame of the impatient Heart — which, for thy
preclear and singular love, greatly and validly inflamed, languishing, consumes itself — O, of veneration preclear, and
most-worthy Nymph, unique in earth of beauty, marvellous and perfect exemplar: with these — not little words, but profuse
and unimpeded little tears — blotting the present papyrus, I have taken this tolerable and honest daring (not temerarious,
but, beyond belief, strongly impelled by the continual stimulus, and by the molesting assiduity of infest love), to reveal
and declare my incredible passion, and sincere affection, which I, for thee, and to thee, bear — my sweet good, and my
sweet hope, and sole refrigerium of my unknown afflictions, and, by thee, unthought languors. To whom, with piteous voice,
and reverent words, and humble prayer, my estate, in peril, I recommend; and, of my arrow-shot and wound-dealt heart,
supplicating succour, to moderate the disordered burning.

"O Polia, divine light, and my venerable Goddess — insurd not thyself, I pray, now, to my opportune mendicables (beggings),
and (things) besought, with the face lowered, a deprecator, of most-warm love fervescent; thee I call, thee I invoke — that
thou hasten, timely, salubrious aid, efficacious comfort, necessary relief. Wherefore, my heart being, with the rapacious
hooks of thy stelliferous eyes, from my breast divulsed (torn), is originated the cause of this my inept and inelegant
writing — by me confused, and by love disposed. And, already in the preterite days, I would have attempted the like; but
never have I been able to find a mode so convenient and arcane. Then, for such respect, this my tormenting torment,
tacitly, I restrained, deferring. For, at present — since no more slow and suspended (was) my desire, intruded and
encloistered, I could not bridle it. For the violence of my love thus wills, and my depraved lot, to this my found mode, and
sweetest exordium, urges me, draws me — O egregious Nymph, and, of whoever ever was, most-beautiful. Then attend, and
commove thyself, pious; present thyself benign; render thyself, with placation, unible, to so much benevolence —"

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Poliphilo's decision made, he sends the letter — and **chapter XXXIII gives the first love-letter**
in full: an *avowal-and-plea* (he reveals his "incredible passion," calls Polia "divine light, my venerable Goddess, sole
refrigerium," his heart "torn out by the hooks of her eyes," and begs her to "commove herself, pious… present herself
benign"). All secure; a set-piece courtly love-epistle. *(The argument notes she was unmoved, and a second letter followed,
pp. 448+.)*

- **The love-letter as genre.** Chapter XXXIII is a formal *epistola amatoria* — the courtly love-letter, a recognised
  Renaissance genre (Boccaccio's, the *ars dictaminis*). Colonna gives Poliphilo's suit its written form: avowal, praise,
  self-abasement, and plea for mercy. It is the substitute for the "Socratic breast-window" (p. 440) — writing opens the
  heart that could not be shown. A distinct register within Book II (direct address, no narrative frame).
- **the heart torn out by the eyes' hooks.** His governing image: Polia's "stelliferous eyes" have "hooks" that tore his
  heart from his breast — the beloved's gaze as a grappling-hook (cf. the eyes-as-arrows, p. 427). Her beauty is the wound
  and the cause of his writing.
- **the plea unanswered.** The argument tells us in advance that this letter *fails* — Polia "not moving in the least"
  (her cruelty, chs. XXVI, now shown receiving his letters). His perseverance (writing again, the second letter) is the
  chapter's point: love that keeps writing though unanswered. The world/novel's persistence-theme.
- The letter's plea completes on p. 445.
