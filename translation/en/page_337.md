# Page 337 — Chapter XXII

With such provocation that to bitter death a man would offer himself, of his own accord and
willing. O contagious artifice! O alluring ministry, O assaulting siege-engines — which any heart,
however sound, most safe, valid and quiet, and free, and resisting, you easily infect, suddenly
subvert and depopulate, without delay prostrate, without respite perturb, totally subjugate, and
without struggle drag away. To lay low, scatteringly, all moderate, incredible and stubborn
continence. These are those true and infallible satellites of laborious Love, with an amplifiable
number of most-monstrous executioners, ambushing the immune, remote and disjoined reflections, and
excluding those inclined to such subjection from being able by any means, nor with a daring spirit,
to prevail — and, by the presented, inevitable and drawing sweetness, with so shrewd and diligent
study ensnaring, and with subtle wit invented so most-exquisite a working, and to so beautiful and
most-shapely a work by fecund nature herself, an artifice found for destroying, and a choice
adjunct and accessory of torment. Alas — which reasonably ought to be feigned, and used, for marble
statues, and not for human and fragile hearts. For even that noble and divine sex, despoiled and
denuded, supremely torments and infects the imperilled, let alone with this voluptuous addition of
pernicious snares devised. But this alone accrues: that they do not consider it enough to perish of
love naturally, without adding so much increased increment of excogitated modes, to conduct the
wretched and soft lovers to the death of heart-grief more easily, and continually to excite little
sparks extremely incendiary, to quicken the amorous and fervid little flames to consumption —
divine objects, to refill and confound the warm breast with bubbling little sighs, and to ferment
the heart to love. Now I would not know how duly to express by what means so great a foundation of
love, stable and sincere, firmly placed in Polia, felt itself somewhat shaken by these inevitable
and ready ambushes and impetuous violences. Alas, then, within myself I silently repeated: O my
dear little beautiful Polia, guard your won prey! For a great peril is the passage through so many
piratical fallacies, and manifest cut-throats, and little ambushing plunderers — which, against
all sincere rectitude, are for their amorous offence commended and laudably approved, and by the
sad, offended ones so much the more desired, and reverently the more sought, and the more sweetly
loved.

Now, on her most-joyous forehead, under two half-circles like threads of subtle silk, and blackest
brows more than shining amber, two arrow-shooting eyes festively shone, more than the most-
irradiating stars resplendent in the most-limpid sky. And in her aspect more beautiful than
incarnate and fresh roses; and the cheeks not rouged, but with a most-pleasing and genuine redness,
than which —

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The moral turn of the chapter: Poliphilo's apostrophe to beauty as "the
satellites of Love" — siege-engines that overthrow even the soundest heart — his confession that his
love, though "firmly placed in Polia," felt itself shaken, and his silent charge to Polia to "guard
her won prey." Then the register shifts to the beginning of **Polia's own portrait** (brows, arrow-
shooting eyes, unrouged cheeks). All secure; the meditation is elaborate but its sense is clear.

- **Beauty as siege-engine — the chapter's moral core.** After eleven pages of costume-catalogue,
  Colonna turns on it: all this adornment is "contagious artifice," an "assaulting engine" that
  subjugates the free heart. It ought to be used "for marble statues, not for human and fragile
  hearts." This is the book's recurring ambivalence — the erotic marvel is also a danger — stated
  most directly here.
- **"O my dear little beautiful Polia, guard your won prey!"** Poliphilo's fidelity beat again (cf.
  p. 314): confronted with the nymphs, he feels his love "shaken," recovers, and addresses Polia
  directly. The *adepta praeda* — the prey she has won — is himself. His heart is her spoil, as the
  gods' arms are Cupid's.
- **Polia's portrait begins.** The catalogue's technique is now turned on Polia herself: silk-thread
  brows, amber lashes, star-bright "arrow-shooting eyes," rose cheeks "not rouged but genuine." The
  portrait continues on p. 338. This is the emotional pivot from the anonymous train to the beloved.
