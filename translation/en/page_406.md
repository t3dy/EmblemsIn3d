## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Polia's dream: **two grotesque executioners** — described in repellent, caricatured detail
(basilisk eyes, rotten teeth, goat-hair, taloned bloody hands, ropes and lictors' axes) — burst into her chamber, calling
her "proud, nefarious, rebel, enemy of the immortal Gods, negligent of her own pleasingness," and threaten to **tear her
like the two damsels** she saw. All secure; the nightmare that completes her terror.

- **"Negligent of her own pleasingness."** The executioners' charge names Polia's exact sin: she was "negligent of her
  own pleasingness" — she refused to *use* her beauty for love, spurning Venus's gift (her family's hubris, p. 384,
  answered). The crime punished at the Polyandrion (coldness to love, p. 250) and by Cupid's chariot (p. 401) is now
  charged against her by name in her own dream. Her conversion requires her to admit it.
- **the grotesque executioners.** Colonna's most repellent portrait — the antithesis of the book's beautiful nymphs and
  gods: love's punishers are hideous where love's servants are lovely. The contrast dramatises the stakes (be lovely in
  love, or be seized by these). A vivid, stageable nightmare (the dark inverse of Book I's gorgeous visions).
- **the dream as verdict.** The executioners pronounce the "divine vengeance" the whole chapter foretold (p. 398): Polia
  is condemned, in her sleep, to the fate of the cruel. What saves her is that this is a *warning* dream — she wakes (the
  nurse rouses her) before it is executed, and the nurse's counsel turns her to repentance. Terror completed, grace
  begins.
- **Pelias and Jason** — the one-sandalled Jason whose arrival doomed Pelias (the oracle): Polia's dread of the
  executioners exceeds even that fated terror. The learned simile persists into the nightmare.
