## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The meditation on **Love's cosmic fire** — a flame that penetrates the heavens and the abyss, which
no sea-god can resist, and "in which mortals, dying with supreme sweetness, live"; then the marvel of how it burns unquenched
in Polia's snow-white breast and eyes; and Poliphilo's apostrophes to Cupid ("how sweetly thou nestlest in my soul") and to
Polia's eyes ("O sweetest executioners"), invoking Apuleius's Lucius (longing for the roses) and Psyche's helpers. This ends
**chapter XIX**. All secure.

- **"The fire in which mortals, dying with supreme sweetness, live."** The definition of Love the whole crossing has been
  building to: a paradoxical flame that consumes yet nourishes, that kills sweetly and thereby gives life — the *physizoic*
  fire of Venus (the temple's "life-conserving" love, p. 229), and the exact opposite of the Polyandrion's Hell-fire that
  merely torments (p. 250). Love's fire is generative; death's fire is punitive. The two temples, and the two fires, are now
  joined and distinguished. A key statement for the world/novel.
- **the Apuleian frame closes.** Poliphilo names Lucius (the ass who longed for the roses that would restore him) and
  Psyche's four helpers (ant, reed, eagle, Cupid's prick) — the *Golden Ass* and *Cupid and Psyche* — the classical love-
  romances the whole Hypnerotomachia rewrites. His desire for Polia exceeds even theirs. The book signs its literary ancestry
  as it ends the chapter.
- **"O sweetest executioners."** Polia's eyes are Love's arsenal, girding Cupid's quiver from Poliphilo's own heart-wounds:
  the beloved's beauty is Love's weapon, and the lover its willing victim (the fidelity-as-captivity theme, pp. 341, 240,
  brought to its tenderest pitch). The direct address makes it a spoken love-lyric — voiceable.
- **Chapter XIX complete, and Book I's arc closed.** The whole later half of Book I — Temple of Venus (XVII–XVIII), the
  Polyandrion (XIX) — is now translated, and the narrative flows straight into **chapter XX** (the crossing and the singing
  at the oars, already done) and on to Cythera (XXI–XXIV, done). **All of Book I from the Dallington seam (p. 193) onward is
  now in English**, except chapters not in the earlier work-order. The remaining task is Book II (chs. XXV–XXXVII).
