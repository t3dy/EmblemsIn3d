## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The six rowing-nymphs described in the book's fullest costume-manner: their pearls (surpassing
the one Julius Caesar gave Servilia), flower-wreaths, jewelled collars, and breast-bands, and their **named identities**
in three luxurious pairs — **Aselgia & Neolea** (cloth-of-gold), **Chlidonia & Olvolia** (Babylonian sea-purple),
**Adea & Cypria** (slashed *melledarum* with bared arms) — their sheer wind-revealed dress and silk shoes. All secure.

- **The six nymphs named.** The rowers of the Cythera-boat are individuated — their Greek names carry meaning:
  **Aselgia** ("wantonness"), **Chlidonia** ("luxury/daintiness"), **Cypria** ("of Cyprus," Venus's isle), and their
  companions Neolea, Olvolia, Adea. As with the triumph's masque (pp. 338–340), the crew of Love's boat are
  personifications — the sensuous aspects of Venus's service. The world's crossing can name its six singer-rowers from
  these.
- **the surpassing pearl.** The nymphs' pearls exceed the famous pearl Julius Caesar bought for Servilia (Cato's sister,
  his mistress — Suetonius): the surpassing-topos, on jewels (cf. Cleopatra's pearl-tradition). Colonna's erudition
  even in a costume-list.
- **the wind-revealed dress, once more.** The breeze pressing the sheer cloth to disclose "the round belly… the fleshy
  thighs… the trembling buttocks" is the book's insistent erotic motif (the boating-nymphs, p. 313; the triumph-
  companions, p. 331), here on the boat's crew. The crossing is sensuous throughout, before the singing lifts it (ch. XX).
- **the costume-catalogue as pleasure** — Poliphilo confesses he "usurps a soft pleasure" merely from remembering and
  describing them: the ekphrasis is itself an erotic act. The chapter ends (pp. 278–282) with the boat's departure and
  the sea-gods' homage to Cupid, into the crossing of chapter XX.
