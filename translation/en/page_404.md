# Page 404 — Chapter XXVII

"'— most-bitter weeping consecrating. Alas, dolorous and unhappy — in what calamity am I implicated and entangled, in what
state I find myself, I avail not to explicate! Who ever saw the subtle fortune with so malign and atrocious an aspect? —
that in such a manner (O holy Lady Diana, whom I serve) these my female and virginal flesh must be impiously slaughtered
and consumed, and the flower of my pleasant age, in this bushy and sylvestrous grove, perish; and, with so much torment
and savagery, the sweet life end?'"

Henceforth I felt the feminine virtues withdrawn, the dear little spirit to flee from its place; and, thence almost
removed, at this pass — alas, alas, bitterly weeping, with most-rich rivulets of tears (a peculiar humour, without fail,
fecund and ready) irrigating the face, and the breast made-wet — the hands, desperate, in the blond hair I put, with
hatred of my adornment, and, the tresses scattered, I wept, and the most-beautiful face, with the hostile nails reddening,
I defiled. And, beyond measure tribulating and afflicting myself, this increment to my grave grief accrued: not to be
able to exhaust the anguished laments and groans, and, in so afflicting and lost a lot, and in so many tormentable pains,
not being able to unseal the cloister of my grief, in the heart impatient more to contain itself. And much more, that
there was no mode I might avail to investigate this most-turbulent case — and that, unadvertent, with an innocuous
conveyance deported, fearful and trembling, weeping, I found myself unharmed, and, without more hoping, in that same
place, whence I was seized and carried off.

O me, heaven-dwelling Nymphs, cogitate how alacritous and content I found myself — let no intellect present itself to
explicate — and my mind alienated from the compassionable case (which I held of little (account)) of the wretched
Poliphilo, lifeless. Because, from the vexed memory wiped and scraped, in itself it kept him not; but only to the devoured
girls, and impiously killed, and most-harshly agonised, held occupied and implicated my mind, and to so most-iniquitous a
butchery. Finding no prohibition to the sobbing and frequent sighs, nor an apt mode to settle the anguished mind — with
ill difficulty the flowing tears sequestered — at length I returned, no more alive than dead, to the desired and secure
mansion, the befallen immanity tacitly re-kindling in the heart, repeatable.

And ardent Phoebus beginning the round back of his swift and flying (steeds) Pyroïs and Aethon to Hesperia to show, and
the golden hairs in the saffron splendour re-dyeing, the serene sky beginning ornately to paint with irradiating stars,
and, to the long and diurnal fatigues, already —

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Polia's lament of grief (tearing her hair, clawing her face — "with hatred of my adornment"),
her bewilderment at finding herself unharmed back where the whirlwind seized her, and her return home "no more alive than
dead" — her mind now wholly occupied by the tortured girls (Poliphilo still "wiped from memory"). All secure; the vision's
aftermath, at nightfall.

- **Grief turned against her own beauty.** Polia, whose beauty was her pride (p. 386) and her family's hubris (p. 384),
  now tears her hair and claws her face "with hatred of my adornment": the vanity that doomed her begins to be undone. The
  self-punishment of grief prefigures her conversion — she attacks the very beauty that made her cruel.
- **still not for Poliphilo.** Crucially, Polia notes her terror is *not yet* remorse for Poliphilo — he is still "wiped
  and scraped from memory"; she weeps only from fear for herself. Her conversion is incomplete: fear first, then (through
  the dream and the nurse's counsel, and the second temple-vision) true love. Colonna traces the stages precisely.
- **the tears "fecund and ready."** A wry aside — tears are woman's "peculiar humour, fecund and ready": Polia's self-aware
  voice again (cf. her near-vanity, p. 386). Even in anguish she observes herself.
- The dream of the executioners (the chapter's second vision) follows on pp. 405–406, completing the terror.
