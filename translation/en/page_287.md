# Page 287 — Chapter XX

By which, of over-rich pondering, the anxious mind, with a refreshment of spirit, fed itself; and with
little curious regards, and lewd appetites, the manifest and perfect shapelinesses (and rarely such a
habit of appearance) of Polia most-avidly I beheld, refusing every other thing worth watching, avertible
of protection. But singularly, then, most-pleasing to me was her glittering breast, wondrously painted
with purpling roses, and with milky lilies, in their first opening — in the tearful dawn, without
obstacle to my eyes, a most-placid spectacle, openly conceded, and without an impeding medium, but
sufficiently coloured — to that most-admired object which was her face, so enticing and marvellously
beautiful, graceful, and perspicuous, that such does not appear, with its ornament Hippe, in the pure sky;
with little tendril-curls above, and the rosy forehead and flat temples trembling, which, with the utmost
adornment, down the snowy neck and whitening shoulders flowed. The wanton and spring-bearing breeze
choicely re-blew them. As much as ever the most-high Jupiter can imagine, to confer on the nature of it,
and benignly to produce and skilfully-make — nor ever could Apelles have painted a like one, and much less
Aristides, who feigned human souls with his little brush.

Of which sight I could not sate myself — neither more nor less than the murmuring bees do not sate
themselves of the odorous thyme and *amellus*, and the wanton she-goats of the flowering *cytisus*, and of
the tender fronds. And with willing soul, and with incredible pleasure, I would have unlocked my most-
amorous heart, judging it no way grievous — and tessellated (it) — that she, thence, having seen it,
should have received by proof the token of what are the qualities that, loving, one endures. As to Caesar
the stigmatised Antipater (showed his scars), and as my soul was promptly seduced by her sweet little face
and notable figure, and reduced into servile surrender — not otherwise making pervious my breast,
lacerating, fenestrated, than the most-pious Egyptian Pelican, dwelling in the solitude of the turbid and
headless Nile, to its raging chicks, for hunger, with piercing and cruel beak splits and shatters itself,
disembowelling the pitiful and maternal heart. Which, not to Dionysus, but only to her, perpetually is
most-dedicated, excitable; and, diffusing itself, flowed in me the insane and Lernaean loves, and the
fiery desires; and the incitative thoughts it feigned in the consenting heart, composing, to consume me,
and to destroy my very self, an ardent and most-burning flame, by me wholly conceived. And with greater
miracle the lethal and death-bearing dart, innocuously transfixed, poised itself in my wounded heart, than
the pendent lethal dart in Ephesus, without a noose, in the temple of Diana. The soul therefore
interdicted, to vivify me fully availed not.

Therefore, for such-made cause mortified, beyond measure ardent — if not her delectable regards, refreshing
me, recreated me, and the amorous nods —

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Poliphilo's rapture at Polia during the song: her rose-and-lily breast, her face
"such as Apelles could not paint," and the great self-sacrifice image — his heart offered to her like the
**pelican that pierces its own breast** to feed its young. All secure; a page of erotic-devotional
similitude, its long central sentence kept whole.

- **The pelican-heart — the emblem of Poliphilo's love.** He would tear open his own breast to show Polia
  "the qualities that, loving, one endures," as the Egyptian pelican splits its heart to feed its chicks.
  The pelican-in-piety (a standard emblem of sacrificial love, later of Christ) is here turned to erotic
  devotion: his heart is "dedicated, not to Dionysus, but only to her." A vivid image of the fidelity-thread
  the whole Cythera sequence sustains.
- **surpassing Apelles and Aristides** — the two greatest painters of antiquity (Apelles of form, Aristides
  "who painted souls"); neither could render Polia's face. The surpassing-topos applied to portraiture —
  fitting, since the passage *is* a verbal portrait.
- **the bee and the she-goat** — the insatiable feeding-similes again (cf. p. 284): the eye grazing on
  beauty like bees on thyme, goats on cytisus. Colonna's constant figure for desire that cannot be filled.
- **"the lethal dart… as in Ephesus"** — a dart hanging by no cord in Diana's temple at Ephesus (a marvel
  of balance from Pliny); Cupid's arrow poised in Poliphilo's heart is a greater wonder. Erudite hyperbole
  for the wound of love.
