# Page 378 — Chapter XXIV

When the nymph, with most gentle courtesy, had finished her kind persuasion and
most acceptable reminder, my golden-haired Polia, ready and most mild, rising with
her festive and most witty images or semblances, and with cheeks flushing and rosy
little lips suffused with an honest and reverent blush, made ready to satisfy in
every way — of a nature prompt to every virtue — and to give effect to the honest
petition. Not, however, that she could first conceal it, or hold it in at all,
that she did not inwardly sigh a little; which sweetest little sigh penetrated,
reflecting, into the depth of mine — nay, of her — heart, by the uniform accord,
as happens to two equally shared and concordant war-trumpets. And each, gazed
upon with a divine gaze, intrepidly, with those playful and flashing eyes fit to
shatter (ah me) adamant into a thousand little fragments — with pious and humble
voice, and with most elegant gestures becomingly revered by every one, she
returned to her solacing seat upon the thyme-grown ground, following the begun
work, seated. With fitting —

## Notes

**Confidence: high.** A single flowing sentence of preparation — Polia rising,
blushing, sighing, and settling to begin her tale — clear throughout. The only
interpretive touch is `litui` (war-trumpets) as the figure for two hearts in
accord, kept literal because the image (two instruments tuned alike, one sounding
when the other is struck) is the point.

- **The last beat of Book I.** Polia blushes, sighs once, and sits to begin. The
  chapter has brought Poliphilo from the wounding at the fountain, through the
  tomb of Adonis, to this threshold: the woman is about to speak. Everything to
  this point has been Poliphilo's dream of *her*; from here it is her account of
  *him*.
- **"two equally shared and concordant war-trumpets"** — Colonna's simile for
  sympathetic resonance: strike one tuned instrument and its twin sounds. He uses
  it for the two lovers' hearts, so that Polia's involuntary sigh rings in
  Poliphilo's chest. A precise, slightly martial image for a tender moment —
  characteristic of the book's refusal of easy sweetness.
- **`acrocoma`** — golden/fair-haired at the tips (ἄκρος + κόμη), one more of the
  Greek epithets for Polia's hair (cf. *pyrrothricha*, red-haired, p. 366;
  *abrodieta*, dainty, p. 376). Rendered "golden-haired."
