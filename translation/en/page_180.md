"O how many would most gladly wish that they could look a little upon what you now see clearly. And
therefore lift up your mind to high things, and look diligently, Poliphilo, at how many other noble and
renowned Nymphs show themselves deservedly companioned with their amorous young men, reverent and
generous." Who, with amorous and sweet-sounding notes and measured verses, praising them and their
unwearied lovers, and unceasingly keeping festival, alternating with an extreme delight, and extolling
the triumphs above, together and with the air full of the manifold and most welcome chattering of
various little birds.

In the first song, then, and glad dance, to the lofty praise of the first car in its rejoicing, the
most holy Muses sang, with their divine lyre-player going before. Then there followed the heavenly
triumph an elegant damsel of Parthenope, calling herself by the name of Leria, with her brow crowned
with undying laurel. Attending upon a most beautiful girl, Melanthia, embraced with her divine father of
chief beauties. Whose garments and voice showed a proud Greek. Upon whom the great Macedonian always
laid his heavy head in sleeping. She carried a shining lamp, which freely shared its light with the
companions following, sweeter than the others in voice and song.

And here the distinguished Nymph showed me most ancient Iphianassa; and then the ancient father of
desire, stirred with his beloved daughters into a sweetest music, joined with these a plentiful and
most easy Lycoris. And a matron singing between two Theban brothers, and with the fair Silvia. All
these and others went before the first triumphing car, singing sweetly with heavenly lyres and
sweet-sounding instruments, and dancing most readily, with brightness and magnificence.

At the second glory of triumph the notable Nemesis with Corinna of Lesbos, Delia and Neaera, with many
others more amorous and more wanton than the rest, gave high-sounding and immortal praise, with Crocale
of Sicily.

At the third pomp of triumph likewise the smooth Nymph, showing them to me, said: "Do you see those?"
Quintilia and Cynthia Nauta, with many others, spread their delightful verses of melody. "Look also at
the maiden Violantilla with her dove, and the other weeping for the sparrow."

To the praises of the fourth triumphal car there went before the noble Lyde, Chloe of Lydia, and
Neobule, with the comely Phyllis and with the fair Lyce; and Tiburtus and Pyrrha with a sounding lyre
rejoiced delightfully. Now after this fourth triumph there followed among the Maenads a notable damsel
