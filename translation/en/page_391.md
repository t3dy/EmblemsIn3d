# Page 391 — Chapter XXVI

"And so much did the urgent ardours, re-boiling in the intimate of my heart, exacerbate me — having no other good to hope
and desire, save thee alone, Polia, chief and most-valiant medicine. And I was unknowing of thee, deprived of thee,
relinquished by thee. And the more I excogitated of the ungrateful absence of the outstanding form, and of the celestial
beauty, and most-comely face, and of the elegant heap of thy choice virtues — so much the more increased in me pain and
bitterness, not being able to enjoy them. For which things — O wretched little lover me — with so much impetus,
inconsulate and precipitant, I accepted these horrend injuries and fallacious blandishments and subtle allurements of
love, veiling and sub-covering the bitterness, and most-unquiet agitation, that thence, some time (nay, most of the time),
invasively, must ensue. And therefore, purely having, and with so much tolerance, voluntarily, for thee, my Lady Polia
sweetest, these its most-rough ambushes received, they have made me then unjustly to remain (alas, sad me!) so great an
interval of time without re-seeing thee, all my good, all my hope, all my consolation, thee solacing prison-house of my
heart — and without the spectacle of the choice and venerable adornment of this thy most-beautiful head, without the
intuition of this so gracious aspect, and notable and wondrous simulacrum. As the Arbonensian lake of Africa, the Sun
absenting itself, the waters of which fervidly boil, and, in its presence at midday, freezing, cool themselves — so I, in
thy absentation, Polia, my most-irradiating Sun, all adust, infervescing, like liquable wax, melted myself, liquefying;
and now, in thy Solar presence, of horror I freeze. Whence ponder somewhat, Polia, my delight and my protection, that so
long a tract of time in supreme anguish, and formidable perils of my living, I have found myself — which living, for thy
love and perpetual service, willing I reserved, at greater peril than the blond and mature crops in the spacious acres
remain (at peril of the crackling lightnings, and coruscating thunders, and falling rains, and blowing gusts); and, in
similitude of the serpent and cross Ivy, embracing the old poplar, thence extirpated and torn — not, by itself, then,
able to climb — falling to the humid earth, lies soft and debile, and clinging, remains; and like the scandent vine,
without its footing and prop upheld, and without the grateful Elm, prostrate, leans. So, and not otherwise, without thee,
my most-firm column, and protection, pillar and most-constant post — to which I had, amorously, inflexibly, and with
obstinate purpose, propped my life — thy absentation, then, is cause that I, prolapsed to dying, so relinquished find
myself."

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The continuation of **Poliphilo's plea**: without Polia he is "the ivy torn from the poplar,"
"the vine without its elm," unable to stand; she is his "sun" (present, he freezes of awe; absent, he melts of longing),
his "column and prop." All secure; the love-speech's central conceits (the sun that boils/cools, the vine and elm), in
Polia's retelling.

- **The vine and the elm.** Poliphilo's governing image: he is the vine that, without its supporting elm (Polia),
  collapses "prostrate to the earth." The ancient emblem of married love (vine-and-elm, from Catullus and the
  emblematists) is here the lover's dependence on the beloved — buildable/quotable, and poignant in *her* mouth,
  since she is the elm that refused him.
- **the paradox of presence and absence.** Her presence freezes him with awe; her absence melts him with longing — the
  African lake that boils in the Sun's absence and cools in its presence (Pliny's Fountain of the Sun). Love as an
  inversion of nature: the same sweet/bitter paradox as the crossing (p. 279), now in the lover's plaint.
- **"solacing prison-house of my heart."** He calls her the sweet *ergastulum* (slave-prison) of his heart: the
  captivity-of-love theme (pp. 341, 240) as tender paradox. Polia, narrating, lets his devotion stand at full eloquence
  before recording her scorn (p. 392) — the honesty of her confession.
- Poliphilo's speech, and Polia's cold flight, complete on p. 392.
