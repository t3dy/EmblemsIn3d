So that my loving and sounding sighs echoed in this solitary and deserted place, in air made thick,
recalling to me my Goddess and immeasurably longed-for Polia. Alas, scarcely a little pause was
granted in which that loving and heavenly Idea was not figured in my mind, and a diligent companion
of my strange and unknown journeying. In which, firmly nested, my soul lay contentedly, as in the
safest garrison and an inviolate Asylum, secure.

Being therefore come in this manner to such a place, where my eyes were stolen and taken up with such
a sight by the copious and choice work of antiquity, above all I beheld a most beautiful gate, so
stupendous and of such incredible artifice, and so elegant in every lineament, as could ever be
wrought and polished. Which without fail I do not feel that I have knowledge enough in me to describe
perfectly, or even sufficiently. Chiefly because in our age the vernacular, proper and native words,
and those peculiar to the art of building, are buried and extinguished together with the true men.

O execrable and sacrilegious barbarism, how despoilingly have you invaded the noblest part of the
precious treasure and sanctuary of Latin, and that art so dignified, at present darkened and ruinously
injured by accursed ignorance! Which, associated together with raging, insatiable and perfidious
avarice, has blinded that most high and excellent part which made Rome both sublime and a wandering
Empress.

Before this excellent gate — judging this the first thing to say — there was left open to the sky a
four-square courtyard, thirty paces by its diameter. With a notable pavement of marble squares,
divided by one foot, set between with tessellation in various interlacings and bindings and
colourings. In many parts broken up by the fall of stones, and grown over with shrubs.

And at the extremities of the said courtyard, on the right and on the left, towards the mountains,
there were two orders of columns set to the level, with the exquisite interval of the wide-spaced
manner set between, according to the fitting requirement from one column to another. Where the first
run, or order, on both the one side and the other, began level with the edge, or outermost bound, of
the pavement at the front of the great gate. And between the one colonnade and the other was a space
of fifteen paces.
