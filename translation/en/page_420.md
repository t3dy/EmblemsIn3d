At this pass, most-celebrated Nymphs, bitterly afflicted, mournful and dolorous, the eyes, of tearful miseration,

myself, I too, upon the gelid body; and, most-tightly embracing him, I said:

"O most-cruel, terrifying and immature Death — of every good the devourer, and of every sadness the inevitable
truncation — lazy not, wish not to delay, at present, to unite me with this one, who, for me (of all the women of the world
the most-impious, and, of importunate impudence, malefactress), innocent and guiltless has migrated from this desirable
light. This one, who loved me excessively, sole his singular and destined good reputed (me). O me, iniquitous and fierce,
beyond every other savagery most-unmild, malign and guilty — more than the most-cruel Phaedra against the innocent
Hippolytus. Who, now, denies to give me the last demise of this most-turbulent and odious life? O accursed first light,
that to my eyes graciously appeared! O maledict vital airs — why do you last so little (why not end)? O odious spirit, at
present bridled — why findest thou not, now, exit and opening? ... O maledict eyes, that, alive, this one you would not see —
you make me, at present, behold him extinct! O tremendous lightnings of high Jove... where remain you extinct — not to
incarbuncle me, and, into little dust and ash deservedly, condignly, to reduce (me)?"

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Polia, converted, hastens to the temple, finds **Poliphilo lying dead, "colder than marble,"** and,
prostrating herself on the body (like Laodamia on the dead Protesilaus), pours out a lament — cursing herself as "the most-
impious of all women," worse than Phaedra, and begging Death to take her too. All secure; the lament over the corpse.

- **Grief where there was none.** The tears Polia refused the dying Poliphilo (p. 396) now flood her over his corpse. Her
  self-condemnation is total — "of all women the most-impious… more than Phaedra against Hippolytus" — and she begs to die
  with him. The frozen heart (p. 419) is now all fire and water: love and remorse together. Her conversion is complete in
  feeling; the miracle of his revival (pp. 421–422) will complete it in fact.
- **Laodamia and Protesilaus.** She casts herself as Laodamia (who died on her dead husband's body / begged the gods to
  restore him briefly): the myth of love reaching across death — and the precedent for the revival that follows. Colonna
  chooses the exemplum that promises the miracle.
- **the reversal, exact.** She now curses "the first light" of her own life and begs Jove's lightning to reduce her to
  ash — the punishments the visions threatened (pp. 401, 384), now *desired* as expiation. She wants to become the love-
  corpse she made. The self-punishing love is the measure of her repentance.
- She feels his faint pulse and begins the revival on p. 421.
