## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The **kiss** is exchanged (tongue-bite and all), and the **priestess**, moved to tears with the
watching nymphs, gives her response: since the lovers are "already, of love, mutually kindled… composed and unanimous,"
she need not intervene — **Love has united them himself**; but, delighted, she asks **Poliphilo to recount** how he fell in
love and how Polia refused him. All secure; the priestess blesses the union and prompts the next narration.

- **"Love has conciled you himself."** The priestess's verdict: the lovers have done the work of union themselves (through
  Polia's conversion and Poliphilo's constancy), so she need not "interpose." Love "who moves all like things" has joined
  them. This is the theological seal on the reunion — grace has already acted; the priestess only witnesses and blesses.
  Book I's rite (where she *does* actively unite them at the well) and Book II's (where she finds them already joined)
  are two facets of the same event: the outward rite and the inward reality.
- **the priestess weeps.** She and the nymphs are "moved to tears" by the lovers' kiss — the same weeping-for-sweetness
  as the temple company in Book I (p. 218). The witnesses' tears bless the union both times.
- **the prompt for Poliphilo's tale.** The priestess asks him to tell "how thou wast lancinated, and she refused so
  sweet an act" — cueing Poliphilo's own narration (chs. XXXII+), the male complement to Polia's story (chs. XXV–XXIX).
  Book II now gives *his* version of the courtship: the festival, the letter, his suffering. The two lovers narrate by
  turns, as the book's opening argument promised (p. 381).
- Poliphilo's narration, praising perseverance, opens on p. 436.
