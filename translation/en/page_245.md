# Page 245 — Chapter XIX

Over against this was the other circulation (panel). Within which I beheld such designs of egregious expression:
one anchor, again, in the diametral place, transverse; upon which sat an eagle with wings outspread, and in the
anchor's little shaft was intricated a bond (knot). Under these lineaments a soldier, sitting among some warlike
instruments, speculating, feared a serpent. Of this I made such interpretation.

With extreme voluptuousness contemplating these most-noble conceits, in such figuring expressed, I beheld also
the fourth, over against the first circle. I saw a triumphal Trophy in the lower part of the lance, of which two
in-transversed palms; and, item, to it knotted, two bounteous cornucopias raised themselves. In the middle, on
one side was an eye, and on the other a comet-star. This said (thus).

For the magnificence of which **obelisk**, I thought that such was not carried down to Thebes, nor in the Circus
Maximus erected. Then, into the front part returning, I found all disrupted the propylaeum; and at the ingress of

## Notes

**Confidence: medium** (the two remaining hieroglyph-panels; the inscription itself is on p. 246). The obelisk's
last two faces (an eagle on a bound anchor over a soldier fearing a serpent; a triumphal trophy with palms,
cornucopias, an eye and a comet), surpassing the obelisks of Thebes and the Circus Maximus; then, at the ruined
front door, a **fragment of inscribed frieze** whose text is given on p. 246. All secure.

- **The four-faced obelisk complete.** All four base-panels are now read: the Caesar-dedication and the concord-
  motto (p. 244), and here the eagle-anchor-soldier and the trophy-palms-comet. Together they make the obelisk a
  monument of *rule, vigilance, victory and concord* — imperial virtues set over the tomb of love's failures.
- **surpassing Thebes and the Circus Maximus** — the great Egyptian obelisks (of Thebes/Karnak) and the one
  Augustus brought to the Circus Maximus: this obelisk exceeds them. The surpassing-topos, on monuments (the
  world's polyandrion obelisk inherits this grandeur).
- **the anchor-and-eagle, the trophy-and-comet** — Colonna's rebus-symbols (an anchor bound = steadfast hope; a
  comet = a deified soul; palms and cornucopias = victory and plenty): the vocabulary of Renaissance emblematics
  in its cradle. Poliphilo's "interpretations" of these (unquoted here) model the humanist reading of images.
- The frieze-inscription — the Polyandrion's own dedication — follows immediately on p. 246.
