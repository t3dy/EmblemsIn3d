"O me, most-beautiful Polia, my most-precious daughter — the time of our life is more to be esteemed, and supremely
appreciated, more than the amplest treasures of Darius, and the riches of Croesus, and the felicity of Polycrates, and
above every thing of the world. And this our life, of little age, more perpetually and swiftly is a-running, and more
fugitive flees, than do the rapid Horses of Phoebus; and more it empties itself out than the most-tenuous evanescent
Bubble. For all this, we ought joyously to adapt the sweet years to facetious Love — opportune, and matured coming.
Because then, into the incommodious old age coming, still shrewdly we wish to feign the youth passed; and for so much we
give heed, intently, to extirpate from the hoary head the white hairs, and to infuscate and draw them to blackness, with
lye of litharge, and quicklime, or with barks of walnut — and to wish, by art, longly to feign and conserve that which the
natural denies: to illustrate the skin, and, painting it, to extend (smooth) it, and to conserve the shrivelled flesh
swelling and fresh. And, beyond measure, we are gnawn, mordantly, in the heart, of continual and unceasing pain, sighing
and weeping the transacted time, delicious, amorous, and solacing; and of the despising of that repudiated age, and of the
rare, much-desired regard, by the refuged youths interdicted — because the age asks parity of similitude. And,
commemorating the lifted (lost) loves and the amiable sweetnesses, and avidly desiring to live more than in the time of
the florid youth (which is not known, then, by remoteness from the end — but, the privation approaching, for this urges the
appetite of living), if possible it were, the years of Nestor, and of Priam, and the years of the Sibyl.

"Then, Polia, my dear little treasure — for as much as thou appreciatest the present life and little florid age, gracious —

tumefied, and already conceived, ires, perhaps, against thee. Whence, perhaps, to the supernal Gods thou believest, with

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The nurse's conclusion: **Medusa** turned to snake-hair for scorning suitors (her would-be lovers
fleeing her in horror when at last she wanted them); the *carpe diem* argument in full — **time is the greatest treasure**,
life flees "faster than Phoebus's horses," so "adapt the sweet years to Love in season," for old age brings only vain hair-
dye and unquenchable regret; and the warning that Polia's vision may **presage Cupid's wrath** against her. All secure; the
counsel's climax.

- **The great *carpe diem*.** The nurse's peroration is one of the book's most direct statements of its theme: **love in
  its season, for time cannot be bought back**. Life is briefer than a bubble; old age can only dye its hair and weep for
  the youth it refused; "the age asks parity of similitude" (love your equals in youth, not old men, too late). This is the
  positive creed of Venus Physizoa (generative love, p. 229) as practical wisdom — and the argument that, with the visions,
  finally turns Polia. Quotable, and the moral core of Book II.
- **Medusa — the last mirror.** Medusa, cruel to suitors, is turned to a snake-haired horror whom lovers flee when at last
  she wants them: the exact terminal image of refused love (petrification/transformation, cf. the Propoetides, p. 410;
  Anaxarete, p. 396). Polia, warned, sees her own Medusa-fate. The nurse's every exemplum is a mirror.
- **the vision as omen.** The nurse reads Polia's dream-terrors (chs. XXVII) as Cupid's *presage* of wrath: she must repent
  *now*. This turns the counsel toward action — the visit to Venus's priestess (the argument, p. 409) that begins the cure.
  Chapter XXVIII completes on the threshold of that turn.
- **Chapter XXVIII nears its end.** The nurse's counsel — the mythic warnings, the cautionary tale, the *carpe diem* — is
  the intellectual pivot of Polia's conversion: fear (the visions) plus reason (this counsel) plus, next, the priestess's
  cure. The remaining lines send Polia to Venus. (The chapter runs one line past; the next chapter, XXIX, is the visit.)
