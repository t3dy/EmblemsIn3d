## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The vision's resolution: **Cupid's flaming torch drives off Diana's ice-chariot**, whose freezing
car "melts and disappears" before Venus's fire — and Polia finds her chamber **strewn with roses and myrtle** (Venus's
tokens), takes it as the goddess's protection, and resolves to follow the nurse's counsel: **to go to Venus's temple** and
reveal her love. All secure; the divine confrontation decided in Venus's favour.

- **Fire melts ice — Venus wins Polia.** The chariot-duel ends decisively: Venus's fire liquefies and dissolves Diana's
  ice. The whole book's conflict (chastity's cold vs. love's warmth) is settled — Polia belongs to Venus. The roses and
  myrtle that fill her room are Venus's claim-tokens (her flower and tree), and Polia reads them as the goddess "fighting
  for her, most invincibly." The frozen votary of Diana is now under Venus's banner.
- **the decision to go to Venus's temple.** Polia resolves to complete the nurse's prescription (p. 417): go to Venus's
  priestess and "reveal the occulted burning." This is the act that leads directly into Book I — the temple of Venus where
  the priestess unites the lovers (ch. XVII). Book II is about to rejoin Book I's dream at the seam.
- **love as a thistle "combing" the heart.** Her burning love "carminates" (cards/combs, like wool through thistles) her
  heart — the same tearing-pest imagery Poliphilo used of his love (the moth, the caterpillar, p. 393), now hers. The two
  lovers' torments finally match: she suffers as he did. The symmetry the whole double-narration builds toward.
- The coming to the priestess, Polia's confession, and the reunion complete on p. 427.
