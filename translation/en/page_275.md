## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The full epiphany of **Cupid** and the start of his speech: his blinding beauty (over-gilt hair,
frightful illuminating eyes, rose cheeks, gold-and-cyan-and-mallow peacock wings), his voice that "recompaginates every
dissolved thing" and wakes the dead, and his address to the kneeling lovers — declaring their **sacrifices and prayers
accepted** by Venus and their "ardent desires" about to be fulfilled. All secure.

- **Cupid the god, seen close.** After the multiform, half-glimpsed Cupid of the boat (p. 285) and the little spirit of
  the smoke (p. 227), here the god appears in full, unbearable to look at — the theophany the whole temple sequence
  earned. His wings' colours (rose, cyan, mallow, gold) and his "frightful illuminating eyes" give a precise, renderable
  divine figure for the world/novel.
- **the voice that wakes the dead.** Cupid's power is described as raising cadavers "from the eternal sepulchres and the
  initial matter," calming the sea, bridling fire: Love as the cosmic force of generation and resurrection — the exact
  counter to the Polyandrion's Hell of the love-dead (p. 250). He comes *out of* the graveyard-visit to raise the lovers
  from its dread. The placement is deliberate: Love answers Death at the tomb's edge.
- **the prayers accepted.** Cupid confirms that the Venus-temple rites (chs. XVII–XVIII) and the lovers' constancy have
  been received, and their desire will now be "efficaciously resolved" — the divine ratification that carries them to
  Cythera. His invitation into the boat completes on p. 276.
- **Psyche preferred** — a small psychological touch: Poliphilo imagines Cupid himself finding Polia more beautiful than
  Psyche. The book's habitual surpassing-topos, here turned on the god's own bride.
