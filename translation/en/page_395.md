wretched, not understanding of the malign fate and deadly lot, its exit and destruction imminent to my scanty life; and
to what disaster Fortune intends me, I know not, nor can know, nor foresee — with what calamity, with what troubles, with
what mourning and grief I am implicated, and to eternal weeping proscribed — if thou, my chief hope, succour me not, in
the present anguishes devolved and prolapsed. Whence, considering this effect of love, disproportionate to that cause, I
cannot, by any pact, come into the cognition of it. For this love appeared to me a sweet thing, but the effect that I
feel is supremely bitter. I understand not, then, what thing is this monstrous love — save that I come into conjecture
that thou, Polia, consentest to my tormentous anguishes; nor, however, in thy angelic face do I see any token of pity and
clemency. And for this only, the exasperated soul, by disdain, to flee I feel; nor more can I sustain it, because I lose
the frozen spirits, virtue and health. Alas, then, unhappy lover without peer, troubled — O, above whatever lover, most-
calamitous — I, before me, see the dark death prepared, threatening; of the aspect of which terrified, consternated and
oppressed — for thee, sole hope of my living (that so to be it persuaded me). O fallacious! O iniquitous! O perfidious!
— thou hast conducted me to this most-bitter point. Alas, Polia! Alas, my Polia — what should I more do? What other
refuge, nor succour, can I attempt? To what side can I turn me? Alas, Polia, aid me — for, to my very self, unhappy,
without thee I cannot lend help! For which thing I feel myself perish!" And, the miserable voice raised, with the
springing tears, wretched, the last words terminated, **prostrate on the ground, he died**.

Now, in this sole extreme power — that a man, all the other members and senses lost (most-piteous Nymphs), only the
talkative tongue valorous remains — he made long laments, much better than that which I now can replicate, with so much
bitterness of heart, piteously weeping — much more than the weeping of the wretched Ariadne, which the son of celestial

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The end of Poliphilo's plea and his **death-swoon**: he protests his innocence (he is guilty of
none of the great sacrileges — Ixion, Salmoneus, Arachne, the Danaids), cannot understand why Love is so cruel ("this love
appeared sweet, but the effect is supremely bitter"), and, calling Polia "fallacious, iniquitous, perfidious," with a last
"Polia, aid me," **collapses and dies** before her. Polia notes she cannot re-tell his laments as movingly as he made
them. All secure; the climax of her cruelty. The page breaks as she begins to feel something (p. 396).

- **"He died."** Poliphilo swoons to death at Polia's feet — the mortal end her cruelty drove him to (the argument, p.
  387: "she saw him swoon to death"). It is the moment the whole Polyandrion foreshadowed (love kills); Polia has made a
  love-corpse. What she feels next (p. 396) is the turn of her conversion.
- **"This love appeared sweet, but the effect is supremely bitter."** Poliphilo's last analysis — the sweet/bitter
  paradox (pp. 279, 393) resolved, for him, into pure bitterness. The disjunction between love's cause (sweet) and effect
  (deadly) is the mystery he dies unable to solve — the mystery Book I answered (Love's generative fire) and Book II will
  answer through Polia's conversion.
- **the innocence-catalogue** — Ixion, Salmoneus, Brennus, Echo, Syrinx, the Pierides, Arachne, the Danaids: the great
  punished transgressors, none of whose crimes Poliphilo shares. He dies protesting he did nothing to deserve Love's
  cruelty — which throws the guilt onto *Polia* (and, as Book II reveals, onto her family's Venus-hubris, p. 384).
- **more than Ariadne's weeping** — his laments exceed Ariadne's (abandoned on Naxos, won by Bacchus): the surpassing-
  topos, even in Polia's report of his death. Her inability to match his eloquence is itself a confession of what she
  destroyed.
