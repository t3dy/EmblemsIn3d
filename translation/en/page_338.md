## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The close of Polia's portrait (cheeks like autumn apples, the Sun-and-
mystery-god comparisons), then the pivot: **Psyche crowns Cupid**, and two named nymphs take charge
of Polia (**Imeria**) and Poliphilo (**Erototimoris**); then the file of allegorically-named nymphs
begins presenting Cupid's insignia — first **Toxodora** with the bow, and **Ennia** with a sapphire
flower-scattering amphora. All secure; the names are meaningful Greek and are glossed.

- **The nymphs are named allegories.** From here the procession is a sequence of personifications,
  each Greek name a concept: **Toxodora** "bow-giver," **Imeria** (Himeros, "longing/desire"),
  **Erototimoris** "love-honouring," **Ennia** (perhaps *ennoia*, "thought"), and (pp. 339–340)
  Velode, Omonia "Concord," Diapraxe "Accomplishment," Typhlote "Blindness," Aschemosyne
  "Shamelessness," Teleste "Initiator." The triumph is a masque of the aspects of Love.
- **Psyche crowns Cupid** — the soul-bride sets the votive crown on Love's head, "finer than Hiero's"
  (Hiero II of Syracuse dedicated a famous gold votive crown — the one of Archimedes' displacement
  problem). The myth of Cupid and Psyche presides over the whole triumph.
- **Imeria takes Polia, Erototimoris takes Poliphilo** — the two lovers are formally received into
  the procession as its honoured captives, each led by a nymph. This is the moment they join Love's
  triumph as *doryktetoi* (p. 330).
- **"apples of Decius"** — an uncertain reading; likely a place/variety name for a red autumn apple.
  The comparison is to Polia's flushed cheeks.
