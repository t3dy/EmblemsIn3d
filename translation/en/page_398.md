# Page 398 — Chapter XXVII

*(Chapter argument:)*

> POLIA SOMEWHAT EPILOGUES HER IMMANITY (INHUMANITY); AND (TELLS) THAT, FLEEING, SHE WAS CAUGHT UP BY A WHIRLWIND, AND,
> WITHOUT NOTICING, CARRIED INTO A FOREST — WHERE SHE SAW MADE A BUTCHERY OF TWO DAMSELS; TERRIFIED BY THIS, IN THAT
> MANNER SHE RETURNED TO HER PLACE. THEN, SLEEPING, THERE APPEARED TO HER TO BE RAVISHED BY TWO EXECUTIONERS. TERRIFIED
> THEREBY, MOVING FROM SLEEP, SHE ROUSED THE NURSE AND HERSELF; WHO GAVE HER USEFUL COUNSEL UPON THIS OCCASION.

Deservedly, Polia, being come to this pass, could not moderate herself, nor contain herself, that somewhat piteously she
should not sigh. And, more times speaking, the tears drawn into the amorous eyes, and the rosy cheeks somewhat flowing,
she commoved, provoking likewise the circumstanding Nymphs to compassion of the dolorous lover Poliphilo — that thus
sadly, by vehement love and excessive grief, he should have perished — (and) they drew, from the deep of the tender heart,
amorous sighs. And, upon me the placid and humid eyes benignly converting, almost as guilty they damned Polia. But, for
so much, being more avid to understand the end of such an unjust occasion, and, a little pause made, they solicited that
she should follow her gracious speaking. And there Polia, mannerly, having taken the most-subtle sudary (kerchief) that
from the candid shoulders hung, the moist eyes wiped, and the most-purple cheeks dried; and, the warm sighs interdicted,
and the sweet voice affirmed, with matronal gestures, in this manner following, said:

"Most-blessed Nymphs, hear a great savagery — that I know not what soul, mild and pious, would not now, against me,
injuriously, alter itself. The divine retribution, then, where was it hidden? — that, by my malevolent obstinacy and hard
pertinacity, my beloved Poliphilo died unworthily. O celestial vengeance, why wast thou then tardied to delay? — that,
rightly, against my iniquitous and perfid soul, in that point, thou oughtest not to have lulled thyself. But, indeed, it
stood not, then, much time, that I manifestly saw prepared the enkindled ires of the offended Goddess, and of her arrow-
shooting son — unless I expiated my rude iniquity, and, candescing (kindling) the most-frigid and rigid heart, that most-
holy Numen had not devoutly placated, and out of my pertinacious purpose, of the false suasions and vain thoughts, and my
mind of fallacious and subtle opinion, opportunely —

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The opening of **chapter XXVII**: its argument (Polia caught up by a whirlwind into a forest,
where she sees two damsels tortured; then her dream of being seized by two executioners; her nurse's counsel); the frame
(the nymphs weep for Poliphilo and "almost damn Polia," who wipes her eyes and resumes); and Polia's confession that
**divine vengeance** was already preparing against her cruelty. All secure.

- **The nymphs judge Polia.** A striking narrative touch: Polia's own audience — the nymphs — weep for the dead Poliphilo
  and "almost damn Polia" as she tells it. Her confession is heard as an accusation; the storytelling frame becomes a
  tribunal. Book II is, structurally, Polia on trial before Love's court, telling her own crime.
- **the turn toward punishment.** Polia announces the pivot: "the enkindled ires of the offended Goddess (Venus) and her
  arrow-shooting son (Cupid)" were being prepared against her unless she "expiated her rude iniquity" and let her frozen
  heart be kindled and Love placated. The chapters ahead are her *punishment and conversion* — the mirror of Poliphilo's
  ordeal, and what makes the eventual union (Book I) possible. The frozen heart must be thawed.
- **"the divine retribution, where was it hidden?"** Polia, in hindsight, wishes vengeance had struck her *sooner* — she
  condemns her past self more harshly than any judge. The depth of her repentance is the measure of her conversion.
- The whirlwind and the vision of tortured damsels follow (pp. 399–400).
