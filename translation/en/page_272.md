# Page 272 — Chapter XIX

From here, with most-joyful solace, departing — with curious desire most-eager to behold new things — hastening to
a semi-integral tribune, I beheld some exquisite relics of mosaic painting, wormwork-made. In this place I found no
sepulchre; but in the glasswork painting, still, **Proserpina, with Cyane and with the Sirens, gathering flowers near
the burning mountain of Etna**, was perfectly discerned. Where **Pluto, unsealing the fire-rooted crater of the
flame-breathing mountain, voluptuously ravished her to his love**; and Cyane, for not being able to succour her,
piteously weeping. There I found huge stones of the putrescent wall gaping, and grassy through the cracks with aster
and pellitory; which was also entangled and destroyed, as by a wedge fixed of a big root of an aged wild-fig, whose
roots, springing everywhere, creeping, had destroyed the tessellations, and, the coition of the joints deserted,
rendered great looseness of the walls.

Whence I only beheld part of a little river, (that) seemed a vestige of human form transmuted into it (Cyane), of
incredible art feigned, and marvellously expressed — such the like as once, in the shrine of Minerva on the Capitol,
in the tablet was seen the rape of the said (Proserpina), painted by Nicomachus. Where, with the mind applied to such
pleasant regard being — lo, at my back I hear the fall of some tessellations; and, finding myself solitary, in a
deserted and silent place, suddenly somewhat frightened, and, turning back, looking, I saw a gecko, or wall-lizard,
that had been the cause of such ruin.

Therefore no little displeasure invaded me, for not being able to see the whole work integrally, it being in the
greater part demolished. Considering, then, the violent manner in which Proserpina was suddenly seized, a sudden and
sad thought, in the loving heart, fiercely struck me, saying: "Alas, wretched, impudent and unhappy me! O importunate
investigation, and unbridled curiosity of past things, and of broken stones inquiring — to what am I devolved? If, by
my ill misfortune, my most-beautiful Polia were seized from me, and, by neglect of so great a present thing — above
all the treasures of the world most-pleasing — were driven off from me!" And, in a moment, a more bitter blow
transfixed the sad heart, presenting already in the confused mind the piteous and lamentable case: that the fugitive
of the burning fatherland (Aeneas) incautiously lost his beloved Creusa. And much more excessively it disturbed me,
invading, such a terror, remembering that, on the sandy and solitary shores, drawn away from my presence, my most-
loving Polia sat alone.

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The chapter's turning-point: a mosaic of the **Rape of Proserpina** (Pluto seizing her by
Etna, Cyane weeping, dissolving into her river) — surpassing Nicomachus's famous painting — is half-destroyed by a
wild-fig root, and, as Poliphilo studies it, **a lizard dislodges some tesserae behind him**, startling him. The
image of Proserpina's abduction seizes his mind: he panics that his Polia, left alone on the shore, may likewise have
been snatched (like Aeneas's lost Creusa). All secure.

- **The Rape of Proserpina — the fear that ends the visit.** The Polyandrion's last and most consequential image: the
  abduction of Proserpina by Pluto (to whom the whole tomb is dedicated, p. 236) makes Poliphilo suddenly terrified
  that Polia, out of his sight, has been carried off. The chapter's antiquarian idyll snaps into dread. This is the
  narrative hinge (the argument, p. 241, foretold it): the epitaphs' cumulative pathos culminates in personal panic.
- **the lizard.** A small, precise touch: a gecko knocking tesserae loose in the silent ruin is what startles him into
  the fear — Colonna grounds the supernatural dread in a tiny natural cause (cf. the owls, p. 247). The world/novel
  can use it as the jump-scare beat that turns the polyandrion visit.
- **Aeneas and Creusa** — the exemplum: the man who, fleeing burning Troy, lost his wife behind him. Poliphilo casts
  himself as Aeneas, Polia as Creusa — his antiquarian "curiosity of past things" (leaving her to read tombs) now
  reads as the negligence that loses the beloved. His self-reproach turns the whole graveyard-therapy against itself.
- **Cyane dissolving into her river** — the water-nymph who tried to stop the abduction and melted into her spring:
  the metamorphosis-motif (cf. Cythera's aviary, p. 310) even in the Hell-mosaic. His flight back to Polia is on p. 273.
