# Page 295 — Chapter XXI

I beheld also another little grove, in which, with even placing, was a most
pleasing oak-wood of tender leaves. And here I saw the broad-leaved, and the
common oak, and the robur, and the *hemeris* producing the medicinal *canchry*,
and the *haliphloios* with its salt bark, with plenty of Italian oaks, and Turkey
oaks, and cork-oaks, and beeches, and holm-oak — or smilax, or holly — here leafy
with leaves that do not fall, tended by the Querquetulan nymphs.

In another fence there followed, in equal order, another most gratifying little
wood of fragrant wild cypresses, savin, romidascalo, or junipers, led topiary-wise
into manifold figured devices, with minute and pricking leaves — the willing
consort of the divine mother, preservable — and loftiest cedars of manifold use,
weeping cedar-oil, of foliage like the cypress, of which at Ephesus stood the
statue of Diana, in the most noble temples most highly prized for its eternal
hardness; the aged Carie, and refusing the gnawing moth; fruitful to its great
Crete, beautiful in Africa, and fragrant in Assyria; with a lovely interspersing
of *vrate*, or savin, of perennial greenery, harmful to Lucina, shaped, and
likewise variable.

Afterwards I saw also a tall and leafy pine-wood of pine-nuts: here the Tarentine,
or wild pine, and the urban, and the bitter stone-pine, or Apine, and the
pinaster, and the Zapina, and the weeping Resina, artfully distributed.

Again, in another cloister, there stood a most copious box-grove, planted in round
and square marble tubs, among fragrant and flowering little pot-herbs — such as the
like would not be found on the Macedonian mountain Cytorus — most dense, with a
lovely tapering to a point, diminishing in just measure and degree, and thinning
with a graceful gradation, not without commerce of many other most admirable
devices most beautifully reduced. But one marvellous work exceeded all these. For
of this kind of little tree I saw all the labours of tall Hercules industriously
and with antique shaping composed [in topiary], not without manifold representation
of innumerable other feignings of diverse animals, always green, with leaves that
do not fall, regularly placed, and with a proportioned and congruent interval
distributed through the grassy and flowery meadow.

Likewise there was another neatly planted with manifold trees. And here the hard
cornel, with its blood-red fruits, and some of white fruit; and there was the most
bitter yew, pleasing and most apt to the lethal instruments of Cupid. Afterwards,
mixed together, I saw the elm, lindens, and the finest *phylire*, chaste-tree,
hornbeams, and ashes, and the flowering spear of Romulus, and many medlars, and
rough service-trees.

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** A dense arboreal catalogue — oaks, conifers, box, and a
mixed grove — secure in substance. The nomenclature is heavily Plinian and several
names are Colonna's Latin-Greek hybrids; a botanist could tighten `romidascalo`,
`phylire`, and a few others.

- **These are further compartments of the twenty-fold *bosco*** — the oak-wood,
  the cypress-and-cedar wood (led into topiary), the pine-wood, the box-grove, and
  a mixed grove. The catalogue is systematic, one genus per compartment, and this
  is precisely the "twenty types of tree plantation, one for each compartment" of
  Segre's plan (GARDENS.md §5).
- **the box-grove topiary of the Labours of Hercules** — the single "marvellous
  work" that exceeds the rest: box clipped into all twelve labours of Hercules,
  plus figures of animals, "always green." This is the most elaborate topiary in
  the book, and the reason the world's Cythera puts clipped topiary at the *prati*
  centrepieces. Colonna is describing garden sculpture in living box a century
  before it became a European craze.
- **the yew "apt to the lethal instruments of Cupid"** — yew for bow-wood; the
  grove supplies the material for Cupid's arrows, planted on his own island. The
  cornel too was a bow- and spear-wood (the "spear of Romulus," the cornel-shaft
  that took root on the Palatine, is here among the trees).
- **Querquetulan nymphs** — the oak-grove nymphs of old Roman religion; **Lucina** —
  Juno of childbirth, whom savin (an abortifacient) offends: Colonna's botany
  carries its pharmacology.
