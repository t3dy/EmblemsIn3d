## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The nurse's counsel proper begins: "he who takes counsel cannot perish"; she warns Polia she may
have **offended the gods** by her cruelty, and recites the punished — Ajax Oileus (lightning), Ulysses's men, the
**Propoetides** (turned to stone for scorning Venus), **Arachne** (to a spider), **Psyche** (her labours for
disobedience), and "many girls cruel to their vowed lovers" — then turns to **Cupid's power over even the gods**
(Jupiter). All secure; a catalogue of divine vengeance.

- **The nurse's argument: fear, then love.** She works first by *fear* — the gods punish the cruel and the scornful of
  Venus (the Propoetides, the cruel girls) — completing what the visions began (chs. XXVII). Her list is a compendium of
  the *punished-for-refusing-love* myths, aimed straight at Polia's case. The rhetoric is deliberate: terror softens the
  ground for the positive argument (Love conquers all, even gods — pp. 411).
- **the Propoetides — Polia's mirror.** The Propoetides (who denied Venus's divinity and were turned to stone — Ovid,
  *Met.* X) are the exact type of Polia's family (who compared themselves to Venus, p. 384) and of Polia herself (the
  stone-hearted, p. 396). The nurse names the precedent for the petrification Polia risks (Anaxarete, p. 396). Her
  cruelty is a scorn of Venus, punishable by stone.
- **Cupid wounds the gods.** The pivot to the positive case: if Cupid overpowers Jupiter himself (and Mars, Apollo —
  p. 411), how can Polia, a mortal girl, resist? Resistance is not virtue but folly. This is *Amor vincit omnia* (p. 284)
  turned into a nurse's practical argument. Love is inevitable; better to yield gracefully.
- **Hippolytus** — revived by Diana/Aesculapius: even the chaste hero (Polia's model) is bound up with the gods'
  power over life and death. The nurse subtly undermines Polia's Diana-devotion by showing Diana too serves the larger
  order.
