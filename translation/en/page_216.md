# Page 216 — Chapter XVII

And there, with the utmost devotion, and with ancient rite, the sage seeress first accepted a Tutulus, and
bound the hair; then set over it the superb Mitre; and in the middle of the precious Mitre, tightened above
the most-tenuous little veil, covering the holy head.

The other Tutulus, with the other little veil, she gave to the Nymph. And she, without delay, having ornated
the blonde head with the Tutulus, set over it the veil. Both the veils were bound in the crisping (of the
hair), and conjoined, with a stupendous jewel — of most-shining and full-of-colour Sapphire, the one
consigned to the Nymph; but that of the Priestess, of *Ananchitides* (a divination-stone).

Vested, then, devoutly, over the orifice of the mysterial Cistern, without delay she made me approach there;
whence, having accepted a golden little key, with religious observance she unsealed the bolted Well. Where the
sacrificer, consigning to that Virgin who had carried the Mitre the candid candle, took, venerating, the
Ritual book; and, having opened it, put herself before the high Prophetess. Who began, low, in the Etruscan
tongue, somewhat to read. Then, scrupulously, she took the sanctified Brine, and, with many sacerdotal
signings, with the right hand into the thundering Cistern she poured it. Then she made, from the ardent torch
(of which the Nymph was bearer), the pure candle be kindled.

This done, she made the ardent torch be turned, with the little flame downward, into the middle of the
orifice; and, interrogating the Nymph, said these words: "Daughter, what petition and desire is thine?" She
answered: "Holy Priestess, grace, for this one, I ask — that together we may come to the amorous realm of the
Divine Mother, and drink of this holy fount." And to me likewise she said: "And thou, my son, what askest
thou?" Humbly I answered: "I, most-sacred Lady, not only the efficacious grace of the supernal mother
supplicate, but above all — that she, whom, doubtful, I esteem to be my most-desired Polia, I beseech that by
her, more ambiguous, in so great an amorous torment I be not thus retained." The divine Priestess said to me:
"Apprehend, son, then, now, that kindled torch, from her pure hands, and, holding it thus, with me three times
sincerely, in this manner, thou shalt say:

> 'Just as the water shall extinguish this burnable torch, in the same manner, the fire of love re-kindle her
> stone-made and gelid heart.'"

With the holy rite, and with those proper words which the Hierophant said to me, saying — at whatever
terminated and finish, all the expert priestess-virgins, with venerable ministry, this responsory said: "**So
be it.**" At the last time, the ardent torch, into the frigid Cistern, she made me, with reverence, immerse.

## Notes

**Confidence: high.** The **rite of Venus at the well** — the priestess vests herself and Polia (mitre,
veils, sapphire and *ananchitides* jewels), unseals the cistern with a golden key, reads in Etruscan, casts
the brine, kindles the pure candle, and then puts the ritual questions: Polia asks grace to reach "the
amorous realm of the Divine Mother," Poliphilo asks that "she, whom I doubtfully judge to be my Polia," end
his torment. He must quench the torch in the well saying "as the water quenches this fire, so re-kindle her
frozen heart," the virgins answering "So be it." All secure; a fully-staged liturgy with dialogue.

- **The rite, and its meaning.** The ceremony is a marriage-initiation: the lovers are vested as a pair
  (matching veils), and the central act is Poliphilo quenching a torch in the cistern while praying that the
  *fire of love re-kindle Polia's cold heart*. Water quenches fire so that love may kindle it — the paradox
  of the rite. This is the temple-scene the visual-novel and the world can stage directly: the well, the
  torch, the vested lovers, the response "So be it."
- **the first clear naming of Polia as present.** Poliphilo says aloud he "doubtfully judges" the nymph to
  be Polia — the recognition the whole book has been withholding is now at its threshold (resolved on p.
  217–218). The rite is the frame in which the beloved is finally identified.
- **Etruscan and the *ananchitides*** — the priestess reads in "the Etruscan tongue" (the archaic sacred
  language) and wears a stone of *ananchitides* (Pliny's divination-crystal, used in hydromancy): Colonna's
  antiquarian sourcing makes the rite feel genuinely ancient and oracular.
- **"So be it" (Cusì fia)** — the virgins' thrice-repeated responsory: a liturgical refrain the world could
  use as the temple-rite's audible signature.
