# Page 362 — Chapter XXIII

— that divine sight breathed upon my eyes and passed into them, but both of us,
roused by an extreme sweetness and driven and harried by a new pleasure so long
desired, remained together in a devout fear, almost in ecstasy.

And finding myself so, I began with some reason to be afraid, misdoubting the
vision that the son of Aristaeus had in the vale of Gargaphie; and in a moment it
put both wonder and terror into me.

In the midst, the divine Venus stood naked in the clear and most limpid little
waters, up to her ample and divine flanks — waters which gave back the Cytherean
body neither thickened nor doubled nor broken nor foreshortened, but wholly and
simply, so that it was perfectly discerned in them just as it was. And round
about, at the lowest level, there rose a foaming that gave off a fragrance of
musk. With such divinity was that body transparent, with such particular clarity
did that majesty and venerable aspect present itself, as a precious and
coruscating carbuncle flashes in the rays of the sun — with features and a
marvellous composition neither seen nor ever imagined among human kind.

She had — oh, with what loveliness — her golden hair pleasantly and delicately
dressed, neatly curled and crisped above the milky whitening brow with wandering
and restless whorls, which were hindered from spreading out like vine-tendrils;
and from her rosy shoulders they were becomingly kept from falling free by the
most beautiful little waves. The face rosy and snowing. The eyes starry and
luminous, with an amorous and most holy regard. The honeyed cheeks purpled. The
small narrow mouth most purple, of coral — the dwelling and the little estate of
every fragrant bud. The breast more than snowy and treasured up, with two small
swelling paps that resisted every inclination. The body most ivory and smooth.
Divine semblances. Ambrosial — breathing, rather, a spirit of musk.

Her hair then, most beautiful, like the finest golden threads trailing, lay upon
the purest waters, not sinking, but scattered in a gyre and floating out very
long: in that show the rivals of long-haired Phoebus in the clear Olympus,
radiating illuminating rays. And above the twisted little locks, over part of the
loveliest brow, with a dense growth and an airy heaping, they shone out before
her and covered her as with a little shade, down to the small ears. From these
hung two ostentatious pearls, such as the divided Union did not hang for her in
the Pantheon at Rome; nor did the island of Taprobane ever produce their like for
conspicuous whiteness. And a circlet went about her, or rather a plaited band of
vermilion-whitening and pleasant roses, springlike interwoven, and it flashed
with little gems.

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** This is a blazon — a head-to-toe catalogue — and the
sense of every clause is clear; the difficulty is that Colonna coins a diminutive
or a compound in nearly every one (*melule gene*, *eburissimamente glabello*,
*torcularei crinuli*), and English has to choose between keeping the strangeness
and keeping the sentence readable. I have kept the strangeness where it carries
meaning and let it go where it was only ornament, which is a judgement another
translator could reasonably make differently. Nothing here is *doubtful*; a good
deal is *debatable*.

- **The son of Aristaeus** is Actaeon, who came upon Diana bathing in the vale of
  Gargaphie and was turned to a stag and torn apart by his own hounds. Poliphilo
  has just torn the veil from a naked goddess with his own hand, and he knows
  exactly what the precedent is. Colonna does not name him, and neither do we —
  the whole force of the allusion is that the reader supplies it.
- **`velitati`** — from *velites*, the skirmishers of a Roman legion: harried,
  skirmished at. Kept, because "driven" alone loses the image of being set upon
  from all sides.
- **the divided Union** — Pliny's story. Cleopatra dissolved one of a famous pair
  of pearls in vinegar; the other was cut in two and hung as earrings on the
  Venus in the Pantheon at Rome. Colonna ranks his goddess's pearls above the
  most famous real ones, as he ranks everything.
- **Taprobane** — Ceylon, the classical byword for pearls.
- **`non crasso, non gemino, non disfracto, non breve`** — a list of the optical
  distortions water *would* impose on a submerged body, each denied in turn. The
  water is so pure that refraction itself is suspended. It is a physicist's
  compliment, and I have kept its four negatives rather than smoothing them.
- **`suboliva uno spumamento`** — the foam is Venus's own attribute (she is
  *Aphrodite*, foam-born), and here it is scented. The detail is doctrinal, not
  decorative.
