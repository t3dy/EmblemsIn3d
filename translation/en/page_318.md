THE SECOND kitchen-garden cloister follows immediately after the just-recited work.
At the edge of which, in order, the other seven steps toward the centre began. Upon
the last was compacted a varied close of tree-colouring, most highly to be admired,
with towers, or watch-turrets, most excellently heaped up of orange-trees; and, flanking
the door, were planted in order two jambs — that is, between the wall of the tower and
the opening, or door-leaves, those rising out beyond the top of the tower mutually
joined themselves into one, lifted three feet above the tower's summit. Then the thick
foliage began, fashioning itself into a moderate cypress. And so, in circuit through
all, of two paces its height. The between-tower hedge varied in colour, and in
tree-kind: between two it was woven of juniper. Between two others, of mastic. Then of
arbutus, of privet, of *dendrolibanum* (rosemary-tree), of dog-thorn, of olive, of
laurel. Ever in one fashion in its fresh green foliage. Reiterating, after the last, the
first successively — most beautiful of topiary work, free of any tow-stuffed show, with
never-perishing fronds.

Where, between two towers, upon the level, in the middle a wondrous pinnacle stood out.
For, in the intervals of the wall-hedge, sprang up plants of box, with exquisite
artifice led into symmetrical crescent-horns, to the completing of the between-tower
space, with its hollow, or upturned gap, fashioned with singular diligence. In the
middle, between the two horns, rose a juniper, step by step declining in tiers, up to
the top attenuating, as if they had been turned whirling on the lathe, and the pricking
little fronds made even. The thickest set in the middle gap. Between the horn-work
ascended, upright, a stalk, mounting a foot and a half, where a box-sphere rounded
itself, most justly proportioned.

Within this close, between the boundaries of the ways, were kitchen-garden squares of
wondrous make, dissimilar in the plotting of the herb-work, alternating to the
completing of all this admirable circuit.

The first quadrangle, marked off from the other by the roads — by which the illegitimate
squares came out — was a knotwork of the square lineament, fashioned by little bundles,
with the greatest polish, three palms wide. The first band in the middle passed off
into a circle, and from the two angles the bands met again at the rounding, one above
the other. Which ring knotted within itself another band, divided from the first,

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The second garden-cloister, reached by a second flight of
seven steps: orange-tree towers with box crescent-and-sphere finials between them, the
between-tower hedges each of a different shrub (juniper, mastic, arbutus, privet,
rosemary, olive, laurel, repeating), and then the intricate box knot-parterres in the
kitchen-garden squares. All secure.

- **The orange-tree towers of the second ring.** Each tower is built up of clipped
  orange-trees, its two door-jambs trained to meet three feet above the top; between
  the towers, box crescents ("horns") flank a tiered lathe-turned juniper topped by a
  box sphere. This is the *topiary architecture* of Segre's plan made explicit — living
  towers and gates, ring within ring.
- **the between-tower hedges, a catalogue of shrubs** — juniper, mastic, arbutus,
  privet, rosemary-tree, dog-thorn, olive, laurel, "reiterating after the last the
  first": a rotating palette of evergreen hedge-species around the ring. Concrete
  planting for the world's terrace hedges.
- **the box knot-parterres begin** — the "illegitimate squares" (the trapezoidal beds
  of p. 298/311, forced by the radial roads) are filled with three-palm-wide box
  knotwork; the geometry runs on through pp. 319–320. This is the earliest detailed
  literary *knot garden*.
- **"free of any tow-stuffed show, with never-perishing fronds"** — Colonna insists the
  topiary is living plant, not the stuffed-cloth show-pieces of Roman triumphs: real
  evergreen sculpture.
