# Page 441 — Chapter XXXII

"And already the rubicund Sun, and beardless, at the extreme parts of Hesperia, the future serenity indicating — from the
sacred oracle, the delicate and ingenuous and promiscuous (mixed) women took leave and departure; and, to the solemn
offices and ceremonial observances (not as the Egyptians to Isis and Osiris, with wailing; nor as the barbarians, with
cymbal-, drum-, and pipe-strepitus; but as the Greeks, with choral dances, with melodious songs, and Latin consecrations of
little maidens, with devotion, and with the utmost alacrity performed) they made an end. Whence, from my unfed eyes and
vacillating senses, her choice and non-human image separated and disjoined, I found myself decocted and burnt of vehement
love, and to crackle like burnt Salt; and, with the eyes stupefied of the illustrious beauties, and of the brightness and
genial polish of her charming form — of there, I, again, with (eyes) frequently to salute her, and, within myself, saying
to her: 'Farewell, farewell, little robber and thievish (one) of my every good' — and secretly 'farewell' replicating,
frequently, with that little of heart which she, in her departure, permitted to remain in me (feeling myself to be ravished
and carried off, with her, my soul) — I made, I too, most-hardly, and sobbing, departure; her milky and whitening breast
made, of me, spoils, a high Trophy, and delicious repository.

"Not by other way (alas me), with the eyes desirously following her, than the beloved Protesilaus the ardent Laodamia,
pained, beheld his departing — and, more wretched, not discerning him, fell upon the shore, moribund, weeping, of mortal
grief, her Protesilaus frequently calling — so I, dolorous, with sweetest little tears most-richly resulting (like rain-
droplets), Polia called, invoked, re-called. 'O luckless Ariadne — foundest thou thyself thus, desolate of every hope, not
seeing thy perfid, deceiving Theseus?' — scattering his name, and, inane and vainly, calling, through the vast caverns and
hollow Rocks of deserted Dia (Naxos), calling him emptily; no other, to thy moist eyes objecting, appearing, than the gnawed
crags, the rigid mountains of Murex, the sylvan shrubs of Prinus, and the most-harsh shores, the curvings of the banks, of
the knotty waves and irruent, wave-sounding floods. As now, wretched me, relinquished by my re-found delight, by my unique
good, and most-efficacious remedy, in so much tear-weeping anguish and harsh torment — with a re-kindling of more ferocious
love, and thus laden of every grief? And, of this, I feel myself to agonise, because the relief of my anguishes,
singularly, beholding her, I felt. I persuade not myself, then, that thou, O unfortunate Io, in thy clear father Inachus,
thus afflicted sawest thyself, with the mutated form, and with the once-blond tresses made noxious and rigid horns, and the
human voice, thundering, to bellow, and the verdant meadows become an unwonted —"

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The festival ends and Polia departs; Poliphilo, "burnt of love, crackling like burnt salt,"
follows her with his eyes and bids her a secret "farewell, little robber of my good," feeling his soul "ravished away with
her." He laments his loss like **Laodamia over the departing Protesilaus, Ariadne on Naxos, and Io transformed**. All
secure; his grief at parting.

- **Parting as bereavement.** Poliphilo's grief at Polia merely *leaving the festival* is as extreme as a widow's
  (Laodamia), an abandoned lover's (Ariadne), a transformed victim's (Io) — his soul "carried off with her," his heart
  left behind as her "trophy and repository." The lover's separation-agony (cf. his day-is-a-year, p. 386) at full pitch.
  The parallels are all women mourning lost love, casting Poliphilo in the bereaved-beloved role.
- **"little robber of my every good."** His tender-reproachful farewell — Polia the "thievish one" who steals his heart
  and soul as she goes. The Petrarchan *dolce nemica* (sweet enemy, cf. p. 392) in a gentler key: she robs him, and he
  loves her for it.
- **the Greek vs. barbarian rites.** A learned aside distinguishing the Greek manner of worship (choral, melodious,
  devout) from the Egyptian (wailing) and barbarian (clashing cymbals): Colonna's antiquarian taste, and a note that
  Diana's temple keeps the *decorous* Greek rite. Even in grief his mind classifies.
- **Io, the transformed.** He invokes Io (turned to a heifer, her blond hair to horns, her voice to lowing): the
  metamorphosis-theme again, here as the image of loss and disfigurement. His grief-catalogue completes on p. 442, with
  Perillus's bull. Poliphilo's love-torment matches Polia's (chs. XXVI) — the two sufferings mirror each other.
