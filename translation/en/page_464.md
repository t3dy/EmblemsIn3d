# Page 464 — Chapter XXXVII (the dream ends)

"— me, above all the precious treasures and richest gem-wealth of the world, excessively most-esteemed. I pray thee — not
re-counting that which now, in the open and perspicuous, infallibly thou knowest, and which thou hast been able expressly,
undefective and ratified, to comprehend, in the divine presence placed of so many Nymphs — all thine, individual (undivided),
with every corollary, strictly I vow myself; with the tightest and juridical surrender, donatively I dedicate, and inseparably
I promise thee to bear thy precious love — ever, an intimately native and eternal inhabitant, in my tenacious and ardent
heart. And thine, most-firmly, I am; nor of another's was I ever, (even) had I lived more years than the Terebinth of Hebron.
Thou art that solid column and prop of my life, and true and most-immovable support, and my chief Philoctetes; in which I see,
perspicuously, all my refreshing, salutary hope, stabilised, and commodulated of diamantine snares and indissoluble chains,
from which I cannot divert, nor obliquate, my eyes, but, indefatigable, gazing."

And, wound (about me), embracing — the milky and immaculate arms about my throat — sweetly, biting, with the coral little
mouth kissing me, she strained (me). And I, prompt, the turgid little tongue, jesting, most-sweetly juicy, kissing her, to the
extreme demise; and I, unrestrained, into extreme sweetness fallen, with a most-honeyed little bite kissing her — more
provoked, she twiningly strained me. And, in the amorous little embraces straining me, I beheld a rosy and venerable blush, in
her snowy cheeks native, diffused, with a rosy Punic tincture, with the placid and ivory brightness of the stretched skin
shining, to supreme grace and adornment. And, provoked by extreme sweetness, in the most-lucent little eyes, little tears
emulating perspicuous crystal, and most-circular pearls (more beautiful than those of Euryalus, and than those of the
distilling Aurora over the matutinal rosy roses), sighing —

that celestial, deified image, like a fumy wand of musk-and-amber suffumigation seeking the ether, most-fragrant (with no
exiguous delightment of the celestial spirits, so unwonted a sweet-smelling little fume redolent), through the air resolving
itself — with the delightsome sleep, quickish, from my eyes, and with swift flight, took herself away, she saying:

> **"Poliphilo, my dear — loving me — Farewell."**

*(THE END of the Second Book, and of the whole dream. Poliphilo's waking follows in the epilogue, chapter XXXVIII.)*

## Notes

**Confidence: high** (the closing is plain and secure). The **end of the dream**: at the height of the lovers' embrace,
Poliphilo vows himself Polia's forever ("thine most-firmly I am, my column and prop"), and, kissing, "fallen into extreme
sweetness" — **Polia dissolves into fragrant musk-and-amber smoke, seeking the sky, and vanishes with the sleep**, her last
words *"Poliphilo, my dear, loving me — Farewell."* This ends the narrative of the whole *Hypnerotomachia*. All secure.

- **The dream vanishes at love's summit.** The book's central melancholy, fully realised: at the very instant of fullest,
  mutual, consummating love, Polia turns to perfumed smoke and is gone, and Poliphilo wakes. The *Strife of Love in a Dream*
  ends where it is most complete — the beloved possessed only in sleep, dissolving the moment she is wholly his. Love
  attained and lost in the same breath. It is the most famous ending in the Renaissance dream-vision tradition, and the
  emotional key to the whole work: the vision is a dream, and dreams end.
- **"Poliphilo, my dear — loving me — Farewell."** Polia's last words — tender, and heartbreaking — are the book's final
  human utterance: she loves him, and she leaves. The union is real and the loss is real: the dream gave him everything and
  keeps nothing. (This is the seam into the epilogue, ch. XXXVIII, already translated — Poliphilo's waking lament.)
- **the smoke of musk and amber.** Polia rises "like a wand of musk-and-amber suffumigation, fragrant, seeking the ether" —
  the same incense-imagery as the temple-rites (the aromatics of chs. XVIII, the spice-grove of Cythera, p. 324): the beloved
  becomes the perfume of the whole dream, dispersing. She was the fragrance of Cythera, and she vanishes as fragrance. A
  buildable, poignant image for the world/novel's ending (the beloved dissolving into scented light).
- **the whole book now in English.** With this page, the entire narrative of the Hypnerotomachia — from the Dallington seam
  (p. 193) through both books to the dream's end — is translated. Only the epilogue (XXXVIII, done), Polia's epitaph (done),
  and the printer's errata leaf (p. 467) remain of the whole volume. Dallington's four-century gap is closed.
