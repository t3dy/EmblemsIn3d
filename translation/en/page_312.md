The boundary of the recounted space ended in flowering banks of a most limpid
river, more so than Argyrondes in Aetolia, and more than Thessalian Peneus. The
banks of which were of precious greenest Spartan stone, like the Tiberian Augustan,
planked platewise, and the whole river hedged about between these marble limits.
Which banks were not entangled, nor occupied by *siler*, nor by willow-beds, nor by
osiers, nor by little reed — but the most purged and silvery waters, let in, hedged
them. The surface of which appeared, with celebrated and manifold flowers, most
delightful and beautiful. Which springing and flowing river, by passages and pipes
underground, in diverse and constituted places gushed out in order; and then, by
aqueducts of finest stone swiftly running, watered this solacing and happy site
everywhere evenly with a pleasant murmur.

The waters then pouring headlong into the neighbouring sea. And in this manner the
most clear river, exhausting its increment through the distributed devouring
outlets, did not overabound, but persisted at a perennial evening-out and content.
Which was in breadth twelve paces. The springing veins of which exceeded any
celebrated fountain, and even Cabille of Mesopotamia, more excellent. Nor did the
Virgin Castalia make such a one of hers. As these rendered the waters sweet,
fragrant, musky, and pure, sixteen palms deep — for of so most sweet a source the
fountain of Hercules in Gades did not so abundantly flow. Which were purified,
limpid and subtle in such a manner that they did not occupy the medium between the
sense and the object, nor make it disproportionate; but everything in the open
bottom throughout, as it subsided, was seen perfect. And with mirror-emulation they
rendered whole the things presented. The bottom was flattened of golden sand, full
of fine pebble-stones of manifold most shining colouring.

The greening, leafy and moist banks of which were most adorned with flowering
narcissi, and the emetic bulb, or water-loving sea-onion. Nor were lacking the
hyacinth and the lilies of the valley, and corn *xiphion*, and Illyrian. Here were
copious the marsh-marigold, and the horsetail, or mare's-tail, and the lion's. Infinite
violets — Tusculan, marine, Callatian, autumnal — and the balm-mint, or *cimiadon*, or
*trachiotis*, and other most noble riverside shoots. With innumerable little river-
birds. Here the halcyon of cerulean feathers, and other sudden little water-fowl.
Here the gluttonous and swimming swans, pleasing in the auspices, with the last song
of the meandering waters.

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The river and its banks — its marble revetment, its
underground feed and sea-outlets, its crystalline clarity, and its water-plants and
birds — are all secure. The optical praise of the water (below) is a precise little
piece of medieval sense-theory.

- **The ring-river, in full.** Twelve paces wide, sixteen palms deep, marble-banked,
  fed by hidden pipes and drained to the sea so it never floods — a perfectly
  regulated circular canal. This is the river of Segre's plan (GARDENS.md §5) that
  separates the middle *prati* from the inner terraces, and which the world roofs
  with the citrus pergola (the pergola itself is on p. 313).
- **the water "did not occupy the medium between the sense and the object"** —
  Colonna's optics: perception works through a transparent *medium*, and this water
  is so pure it adds nothing of its own, so the bottom is seen "perfect," undistorted.
  It is the same claim made of Venus's crystalline fountain-water (ch. XXIII) and of
  the island's crystal shores (p. 293) — clarity as the mark of the divine.
- **the bank-plants and halcyon** — narcissus (the transformed youth again),
  sea-onion, hyacinth (another metamorphosis), violets; and the halcyon (Alcyone,
  turned to a kingfisher) and swans "pleasing in the auspices." The transformation-
  motif runs even through the waterside flora and fauna.
- **Castalia, Cabille, Gades** — the Castalian spring at Delphi, a Mesopotamian
  fountain, and Hercules's spring at Cadiz: the famous waters this river surpasses.
