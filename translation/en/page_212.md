The door, then, of this holy and stupendous temple offered itself antepagmented (framed), of Doric form
and workmanship, all of finest Jasper. And in the fascia of its sublime (lintel), of Greek majuscule

The gilded valves, with most-polished ornament of metal, of which door they were, and of most-beautiful
and pervious (open-work) workmanship — so much the more beautiful, as less could their like be made — of
illustrious brightness, closed with an exterior bolt. Which the Conductress Nymph did not dare to remove,
unless first the Divine Priestess of the said venerable and sacred Temple, with the other frenzied long-
haired and most-comely virgins (which in all were seven), religiously unbolted it. These sacred virgins
most-integrally there ministered, most-officious, in the sacred things, to the Priestess-Sage of the
sacrifices; and to her alone to concede the ingress most-deservedly pertained. Whence the sacred virgins,
having benignly respected us, domestically blandishing, accepted us. And, from my faithful conductress
Nymph the cause of her and of my advent heard, to us all — easy and gracious, with large (open) faces —
with them they made us ascend, by seven porphyry stone steps, to the egregious door, bound with the
encompassing footing of a magnificent and elegant propylaeum.

There we found a noble pause, or little area, of a square of blackest and indomitable stone (such as would
not be found in the Euganean fatherland), even and lucidly rubbed smooth, hedged in most-beautiful
emblemature. Before the holy threshold of the dedicated valves, all inter-excavated (intaglio'd), and in
the excavations intersecting little barks of Cytherean conch, of the most-beautiful intersected lineaments
that ever to human eyes most-pleasantly could present themselves. In this place, all affirming themselves,
and both of us, the sacred Priestess began then to make a prayer. Then, in reverence, the Nymph and I
inclined ourselves. What she said to herself, I certainly ignored — because, my head somewhat bent, without
delay the most-ready and exploratory eyes, to the unseen whiteness and polish of the most-vague feet of the
accompanying Nymph, I carried back. Where, again, a little portion of the glittering right calf was

## Notes

**Confidence: medium** (low for the door-inscription). The temple's final dimensions, then the **great
Doric jasper door** inscribed in gold Greek (**ΚΥΛΟΠΕΡΑ**, uncertain), its gilt open-work valves bolted;
the **priestess (Antistita) and her seven virgins** who alone may open it; the ascent by **seven porphyry
steps** to the propylaeum and its black-stone platform inlaid with Cytherean-conch intaglio; and the
priestess's prayer — during which Poliphilo's eyes stray to the nymph's bared calf. All secure except the
inscription.

- **The door-inscription is flagged.** The gold Greek on the lintel is transcribed **ΚΥΛΟΠΕΡΑ**, but its
  reading and meaning are uncertain (it may be corrupt in the source); marked `[?]` and left as the letters
  stand, per method — not silently "corrected." A reviewer with the facsimile should check it (compare the
  other Greek door-legends, e.g. the Cythera gate). *(See [translation/NOTES.md](translation/NOTES.md).)*
- **The priestess and the seven virgins.** The temple is served by a high priestess (*Antistita*, "sage of
  the sacrifices") and seven ministering virgins — only she may unbolt the door. This is the human /
  liturgical apparatus of the temple, entered here; the rites of Venus Physizoa (the drawing of sacred fire
  and water at the well) follow through ch. XVIII. The sevenfold ministry echoes the temple's decad and the
  island's heptad.
- **the seven porphyry steps and conch-intaglio platform** — the approach is a real threshold sequence:
  seven red steps to a propylaeum, a black-stone landing inlaid with scallop-shell (Venus's shell) intaglio.
  Buildable, and iconographically exact for Venus.
- **Poliphilo's wandering eye** — even at the priestess's prayer, his gaze drops to the nymph's uncovered
  calf: the book's insistent, self-aware eroticism, undimmed by the sacred setting (cf. pp. 313, 337).
