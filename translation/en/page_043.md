# Page 43 — Chapter IV

For any part that does not agree with its chief is base. The order and the norm being removed,
however, what convenient, or pleasing, or worthy thing could be afforded? The cause, then, of such
unfitting error is born of negative ignorance, and has its origin in want of letters.

Nevertheless, although the perfection of that most worthy art does not stray from rightness, yet the
shrewd and industrious Architect, to make the object gratify the gaze, may lawfully polish his work
with addition and subtraction. Above all preserving the whole solid entire, and reconciled with the
universal. Which solid I call the whole body of the fabric, that being the principal intent and
invention and forethought and Symmetry of the Architect; which, well examined and carried through
without the accessories, shows (if I do not deceive myself) the excellence of his genius — because the
adorning afterwards is an easy thing.

Granted that its distribution also matters, and that one should not set the cornice at the feet but at
the head; and so the ovolo, and the dentil, and the rest, must be destined to their fitting place. The
ordering, then, and the chief invention are shared among the few; the ornaments offer themselves to be
worked by the many, and the common or unlearned sort. And therefore the handworking craftsmen are the
architect's ministers.

Which architect must in no manner succumb to accursed and perfidious avarice. And beyond his learning
let him be good, not talkative, kindly, well-wishing, gentle, patient, pleasant, copious, a curious and
universal searcher-out, and slow. Slow, I say, so as not to be hasty afterwards in mending. Of this
let it be enough.

Reducing finally the last three figures shown into one, and adding the second from the sixteen squares
contained in it, this figure will be produced. From which, then, removing the rhombus and the
diagonals, and leaving the three perpendiculars and the three straight lines, except the middle one —
which, cut off, ends inclusively between the perpendiculars — and by this rule two perfect squares are
found, the one above and the other below, each containing in itself four squares.

In the lower square, making the diagonal, which set upright in a perpendicular towards the straight
line A.B. will readily be found, by what it falls short of reaching, to be exactly the thickness of the
arch and of the Antae. So then the line A.B. will be the due place of the extended, or straight, Beam.
The middle point of the truncated line E.F. will be the point from which to bend the arch-beam into a
half-circle. Which must have as much addition at its turned-back horns as is the half-diameter of its
thickness. Otherwise, such a falling-short being there, I do not call it perfect. But this was most
beautifully and exquisitely and diligently observed by the best and most skilled of the ancients in
their arches, to give the arch elegance and fitting resistance, and to avoid the encroachment of the
projections of the Abaci.

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The architect's ethics, and the rule for setting out the arch. Dense but
well construed. The arch geometry is right and usable: the mid-point of the truncated line E.F. is
the centre for bending the *arcotrabe* into a half-circle, and the springing is stilted by the
half-diameter of its own thickness, which the translation correctly gives as an addition *agli
inversi corni* rather than as a flaw. *Abachi* as abaci, and the reason given for the stilting (to
keep the arch clear of their projection), are both right. Medium for the sheer weight of technical
vocabulary rendered by standard equivalents, and for one clause of the moralising — *la virtute in
quello consiste et beatitudine canta il poeta* — which the English leaves as obscure as the Italian,
without naming the poet.
