# Page 2 — Front matter

*(Leonardo Crasso's dedicatory letter to Guidobaldo da Montefeltro, Duke of Urbino. Latin. It
runs over onto page 3.)*

LEONARDO CRASSO OF VERONA SENDS MUCH GREETING TO GUIDO, MOST ILLUSTRIOUS DUKE OF URBINO.

Since I have always honoured and regarded you, most unconquered Duke, for the singular virtues
and the fame of your name — and most of all since my brother served under your auspices at the
siege of [?Bibbiena] — whatever was then bestowed by you upon him (and he often recalls it was
much, reporting your kindness and humanity toward him), all of that we have reckoned to belong
to all the Crassi; and what one received, we all count as received by us from you, nor do we
now concede to him that he is more yours than we all are. But my brothers await an occasion to
expose for your sake not only all they have, but their lives as well.

I myself, who so far as my strength allows think often — and shall go on thinking, until I have
done it — by what means I may [?open myself to] you, now come into some hope of my wish. For since
I know that with you one can do no more by the goods of fortune than (as the saying goes) by
water with the sea, and that letters and virtues alone have power with you, I have tried
letters as a ford, for a way of approach to you.

There came lately into my hands a certain new and admirable work of Poliphilo (for that is the
name given to the book), which — that it should not lie hidden any longer in darkness, but
should profit mortals in good time — I have seen to the printing and publishing of at my own
expense. But lest that book, bereft of its parent, should seem to be like a ward without
guardian or any patronage, I have chosen you as its present patron, in whose name it might go
forth [?a little more boldly]: so that as I now use it as the servant and messenger of my love and
regard toward you, so you may often use it as a companion to your studies and your manifold
learning.

**For there is in it not only such knowledge but such abundance that, when you have seen this
book, you will seem to have seen not so much all the books of the ancients as the hidden things
of nature herself.**

There is one thing in it to marvel at: that although it speaks in our own tongue, one has no
less need of Greek and Latin to understand it than of Tuscan and the vernacular. For that most
wise man reckoned that, if he spoke so, there was one road and one method by which nobody could
plead his own negligence as excuse for learning nothing. Yet he tempered himself so that none
but the most learned could penetrate into the sanctuary of his doctrine, while one who came to
it unlearned should not despair even so.

Add to this, that if any matters were by their own nature difficult, they are declared and
brought out in sweet speech with a certain pleasantness — as though a garden of every kind of
flower were unlocked — and they lie open and are set before the eyes in figures and images.
These are not things set out for the crowd and to be chanted at the crossroads, but such as,
drawn from the storehouse of philosophy and drunk from the fountains of the Muses, polished
with a certain novelty of speaking, may deserve the favour of every wit.

Take up then, most humane prince, our Poliphilo, with the brow you are wont to show the
learned; and take him up so that, since he is the little gift of a grateful mind, you may read
him the more willingly for being reminded of your Leonardo Crasso. And if you do this (as I
hope), then he will fear no one's censure, having undergone yours; and he will be read the more
often by others, who will be thought to have been read by you; and I shall hope that in some
part I have attained what I had wished for.

---

## Notes

**Confidence: high** for the sense; **medium** for a few clauses of the Latin, flagged below.

- **Who is writing, and why it matters.** Leonardo Crasso of Verona paid for this book. Aldus
  Manutius printed it in December 1499, but the money, the privilege on page 1 and the risk were
  Crasso's, and he seems never to have recovered them. His letter is therefore the nearest thing
  the *Hypnerotomachia* has to a publisher's preface, and the only contemporary statement of what
  the book was thought to be by someone who had read it and staked a fortune on it.
- **"There came lately into my hands…"** Crasso claims not to know the author, or affects not
  to. The acrostic formed by the great initials of the thirty-eight chapters —
  POLIAM FRATER FRANCISCVS COLVMNA PERAMAVIT — is either a secret he is keeping or one he does
  not have. Compare the epigrams of Andrea Marone on page 8, which make a game of the same
  refusal.
- **The famous sentence** is the one set in bold: *non magis omnes veterum libros, quam naturae
  ipsius occultas res vidisse videaris*. It is quoted constantly in the scholarship, usually in
  a loose form ("more secrets of nature than in all the books of the ancients"). Read literally,
  the claim is stranger and better: reading it will feel less like reading every ancient book
  than like *seeing nature's hidden things* directly.
- **On the book's language.** Crasso's defence of the notorious style — the Latinate,
  Greek-studded, half-invented Italian — is that it is *deliberately graded*: nobody can claim
  to have learned nothing, nobody but the most learned reaches the sanctuary, and the unlearned
  need not despair. That is a description of a book designed to be read at several depths, which
  is precisely the design this project's commentary lenses answer to.
- **And on the woodcuts.** *Figuris et imaginibus oculis subiectae* — "set before the eyes in
  figures and images". The plates are named here as a way of making hard matter easy. Set this
  beside John Dixon Hunt's argument that the three artificial gardens are *not* illustrated on
  purpose (see `GARDENS.md` §3): the book's own publisher thinks pictures explain, and the book
  withholds them exactly where explanation would spoil the point.
- **Uncertain readings.** *Quo nam pacto me tibi aperiam* — "by what means I may open myself to
  you", i.e. make himself known; possibly "commend myself". *Audaculus* is a rare diminutive of
  *audax*, "a little bolder", rendered here as "a little more boldly". The Bibbiena siege is
  presumably the Florentine action of 1498, in which Guidobaldo commanded.
