# Page 368 — Chapter XXIII

— steps, out of the recesses beneath the first pergola, valiantly making for the
sacred fountain, with a majestic and vehement face, and breasted and stout-
hearted with formidable ferocity, brawny and tall with broad shoulders, with the
lights of his eyes keen and terrible, but of a venerable dignity. Sumptuously and
proudly and divinely adorned, with a most cunningly wrought silver-gleaming
shield such as Brontes and his companions did not make for the exiled Trojan; his
head covered with a shining helmet wreathed with olive flowers, with a
prominent crest and a noble golden apex above the cone; clad in a golden
cuirass, such as the divine Julius did not bring from Britain nor dedicate to his
mother in her temple; nor did the excellent craftsman Didymaon ever make such a
one; with a pendent baldric, or cross-belt, girt about him, with a most sumptuous
golden scimitar knotted to it; with all the most beautiful military accoutrements,
most robustly holding a scourge in his hand, and attended by his growling
Lycaon.

Come now at last to the delightful and delicious fountain, he showed himself
eager to lay down his armour and, disarmed, to go in to the beloved goddess. And
there he and she embraced one another intricately, not with human blandishments
and endearments but with divine gestures and affection, in knotting embraces.
Whereupon the nymphs, perceiving this, with humble and reverent speech obtained
leave, and I too, and my lively Polia doing the like, in what manner we then
could, having spoken our thanks to the immortal graces, departed. There remained,
then, only the divine Mother with her son, and with those continually standing
about the fountain, and the armed one newly come, cast down to divine and
solacing delights — all the attendants withdrawn.

## Notes

**Confidence: high.** This is straightforward descriptive prose — the arming of a
divine warrior — and the classical comparisons are all secure. It is Mars,
though the text withholds the name and identifies him only by his splendour and
his coming to Venus; the reader supplies it, as with Actaeon and the rest.

- **The soldier is Mars.** The book's earlier triumph-relief already showed Mars
  before Jupiter with his wound; here the god of war comes disarmed to the
  goddess of love, and the two embrace "not with human blandishments but with
  divine" — the *Mars and Venus* of a hundred paintings, and the moment that
  ends the lovers' audience. That Colonna never names him is the same discretion
  he uses throughout.
- **`argyraspida`** — silver-shielded; the *argyraspides* were an elite corps of
  Alexander's army with silvered shields.
- **the shield Brontes did not make "for the exiled Trojan"** — Brontes is one of
  the Cyclopes who forged Aeneas's arms at Vulcan's forge; Mars's shield outdoes
  even that. **Didymaon** — a legendary armourer named in the *Aeneid*.
- **"the divine Julius did not bring from Britain"** — Caesar was said to have
  dedicated a breastplate of British pearls to Venus Genetrix in her temple;
  Mars's cuirass surpasses it. Colonna's habitual one-upmanship, and it keeps
  Venus's Julian association (cf. the Caesar obelisk in the polyandrion) alive.
- **Lycaon** — here Mars's hound (the name of the wolf-king of Arcadia, apt for a
  war-god's dog).
- The dividing marks close chapter XXIII; XXIV opens on the next page.
