# Page 7 — Front matter

*(The rest of the Italian terza rima, begun on page 6.)*

> — four triumphs of the supreme high-thunderer.
> Then it tells you further, in no rustic manner,
>    the various affections, the amorous work,
>    and the wounds that Love deals to earthly men.
> Here with Vertumnus joyful Pomona
>    triumphs; and the sacrifice of Priapus,
>    with the little ass, and the monstrous member.
> There is a great temple, which from its first beginning
>    is wholly perfect, with wonderful art,
>    with manifold rites to such an office.
> Another temple, gnawed in many parts,
>    wholly spoiled by antiquity,
>    as you will be able to see in these pages.
> And other things that will be welcome to you:
>    Tartarean seats, infinite epitaphs,
>    and the boat of the son of Venus.
> The honours that are all imparted to him
>    with the highest reverence by the gods —
>    the seas, and rivers, and the other streams united.
> Meadows, gardens and Cytherean orchards,
>    in the middle a fair theatre laid out there,
>    where he triumphs with his Cupids.
> The fountain of the Paphian, conquered by this,
>    with her venerable and beautiful form,
>    and the tomb of Adonis, driven from life —
> who was so loved in the world by her
>    that now the anniversary is celebrated for him
>    by the Naiads and by wretched little Venus.
> This is contained in the first book
>    of the excellent dreams of Poliphilo,
>    in a style graceful, transparent and various.
> Down in the second it tells the parents
>    of Polia, and her stock, and her form, and where she was born,
>    and the first founders of Treviso.
> Then in this wholly enamoured work
>    there is a worthy book, and full of much ornament,
>    which whoever does not read will have an ungrateful mind.
> Diverse things are in this treatise
>    which it grieves me to report; but do you accept
>    the work that the Cornucopia has sent us,
>    amending it if it should prove incorrect.
>
>                         *The end.*

## Notes

**Confidence: high.**

- **"Wretched little Venus."** *Venus mischinella* — a diminutive of pity, and the single warmest
  word in the whole front matter. The goddess is not grand here; she is a woman keeping the
  anniversary of a dead lover, year after year. Chapter XXIV, the last station of Book I, is
  built on that.
- **"With the little ass, and the monstrous member."** *Cum l'asinello, et mentula monstrosa* —
  the poem names plainly what the Latin elegy on page 4 covered with "rites to the Lampsacan
  god". The vernacular version is more explicit than the learned one, which is the opposite of
  what one expects and worth noting: the Latin decorum is protecting the Latin reader, not the
  other. The plate (#71) is the book's most explicit image, and whether it belongs in a walkable
  world is a standing question in `NEXTSTEPS.md`.
- **"Amending it if it should prove incorrect."** The poem ends by asking the reader to fix the
  book's errors. The 1499's own errata leaf is the last page of the volume (page 467), so this is
  not merely a modesty formula — the printer knew there were mistakes and said so twice.
- **"The Cornucopia has sent us."** Probably the horn of plenty as a figure for the book's
  abundance, matching *quod cornu copia larga dedit* in the Latin elegy on page 4. It may also
  glance at Niccolò Perotti's *Cornucopiae* (1489), the vast Latin lexicon Aldus had reprinted —
  in which case it is a joke about needing a dictionary to read this.
- **"Driven from life."** *De vita spinto*, of Adonis, killed by the boar. The fountain and
  sepulchre are chapter XXIV; see `ADONIS.md`.
