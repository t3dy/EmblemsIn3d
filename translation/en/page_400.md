# Page 400 — Chapter XXVII

"— a moment I was, through the air, deported and set down. And there, then, with the beating heart, beyond belief
frightened of so sudden a case, and so unhoped — I began to feel that which yet I wished: 'Woe! woe!' most-strongly to
exclaim, with feminine ululations, and feeble voice, and fearful laments, as much as they availed — such as the Noble
Ravennate heard and saw (in Boccaccio's tale of Nastagio).

"Where, without delay, I saw, disorderedly coming, two doleful and luckless girls — here and there, and often stumbling
(the utmost provocation of pity) — to an ignited (fiery) vehicle constrained, and, with chains candent (white-hot) of
strong steel, to the yoke ensnared; which, hard-straining the tender and whitest and downy flesh, scorched (it). And
dishevelled, nude, with the arms bound at the back, miserably they wept, the mandibles stridulous, and, upon the fiery
chains, the melting tears frizzled. Incessantly stimulated by a flame-bearing, and without measure furious and implacable,
boy — who, winged, above the ardent vehicle sat, with his aspect formidable (more indignant and horrible was not the
terrible Gorgonean head to Phineus and his companions) — with a beast-like rage and fury, and with a nervy (sinewy) and
incendious scourge, ferociously he beat (them), without pity stimulating the ensnared girls; and with a greater vengeance
than Zethus and Amphion against Dirce the stepmother."

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Polia's **vision of Cupid's punishment of cruel women**: set down in a pathless wood, she sees two
**dishevelled, naked girls chained to a fiery chariot** with white-hot steel, "hard-straining… scorching" their flesh,
driven and scourged by a **furious winged boy (Cupid)** — a scene she likens to the hound-hunt of the damned woman in
Boccaccio's "Nastagio degli Onesti." All secure; the punishment-vision that will convert her.

- **The infernal hunt of the cruel — Polia's warning.** Two women who (like Polia) were cruel to their lovers are dragged,
  chained and scourged by Cupid in a fiery chariot: an eternal punishment for coldness in love — the exact fate Polia has
  earned (the ice-damned of the Polyandrion's Hell, p. 250; Anaxarete, p. 396). The vision *shows her her own future* if
  she does not repent. It is the hinge of her conversion (as the Rape-of-Proserpina fresco was Poliphilo's terror, p. 272).
- **the Boccaccio echo.** Colonna explicitly invokes "the Noble Ravennate" — **Nastagio degli Onesti** (*Decameron* V.8),
  where a spurned lover sees the ghost of a cruel woman hunted and torn by hounds each week, and uses the vision to win his
  own disdainful lady. Polia's vision is the same *exemplum contra crudelitatem*, and Colonna names his source. A rare
  explicit literary citation, tying the Hypnerotomachia to the Italian novella tradition.
- **the machinery of the vision** — fiery chariot, white-hot chains, sinewy scourge, the winged tormentor: buildable,
  vivid infernal imagery (the counterpart to Book I's Cupid-triumph, here Cupid as torturer). Nothing in the 3D world uses
  Book II, but this is among its most stageable scenes.
- **Zethus and Amphion against Dirce** — the twins who tied cruel Dirce to a bull: the punishment-of-the-cruel myth again.
  Polia sees her sisters-in-cruelty suffering, and reads the lesson. The vision continues (pp. 401+), driving her home in
  terror.
