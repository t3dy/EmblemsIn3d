# Page 258 — Chapter XIX

> **HEUS VIATOR** — *(the epitaph of Leontia and Lollius; Poliphilo's Latin rendering of the Greek)*
>
> "Ho, traveller! Interpose thy hands a little (in prayer), being adjured; and, reading, much-groaning, give kisses
> to the metal, adding: 'Ah, cruel monster of Fortune! They ought to have lived.'
>
> "Leontia, a girl, the chief beloved of Lollius, a freeborn youth — when the intemperance of love pressed (her),
> afflicted with paternal torments, she fled away. Lollius follows; but, amid embracing, taken by pirates, they sell
> them to a certain trafficker. Both captive, they board a ship. When, by night, Lollius suspected Leontia to be
> carried off from him, seizing a sword, he butchers all the sailors. The ship, the sea's savagery arisen, dashed on
> the rocks near land, sinks. They climb a rock. By the impulse of hunger, seizing Leontia, I set her on my shoulders.
> 'Favour, be near, O father Neptune,' saying, 'us and our fortune I commit to thee.' Then, with a dolphin-like effort,
> with my arms I cut the little waves. But Leontia, amid the swimming, speaks: 'Am I a trouble to thee, my life?'
> 'Lighter than a water-skater, Leontia, little heart.' And, oft asking, 'Hast thou strength, my hope, my little
> soul?' 'I say (yes).' Those (words) thou rousest. Soon, embracing (my) neck, she sweetly kisses her bearer, solaces,
> exhorts, gives spirit to the diver. I exult. To the shore at last we come, safe. Unhoped-for, a roaring lion attacks.
> We embrace each other. The lion spares the dying. Terrified by the chance, a little boat cast up on the shore
> together with a rowing-oar, we fugitives board. Each in turn, singing, we row. A day and a night — and a third —
> wandering. Only the sky itself, on every side, lies open. By lethal hunger we are tormented; and, wasting with long
> fasting, we rush into embraces. Leontia saying, 'I pray thee, dost thou perish of hunger?' 'It is enough to be with
> thee, Lollius — I am fed.' But she, sighing: 'My Lollius, dost thou fail?' 'Not at all,' I say, 'in love, but in
> body.' With only vibrating and mutual tongues we fed ourselves sweetly; and, more tightly, with gaping mouths, sweet
> kisses cast, ivy-like we embraced. Both, without turning (breath), we die. By the surges — not (even) raging —
> hither, by the breeze, we are carried; and, wretched, in the estuary-air, bound in those very embraces, among the
> Plutonic shades here we are laid. And whom the piratical rapacity did not retain, nor the leonine maw devour, and
> the sea's immensity refused to take — the narrowness of this little urn here holds them both. This unhappiness I
> wished thee to know. Farewell."

---

## Notes

**Confidence: high** (the epitaph is a continuous, secure Latin narrative). The **epitaph of Leontia and Lollius** —
the longest and most novelistic inscription in the Polyandrion: a complete shipwreck-romance. The lovers flee her
cruel father, are captured and sold by pirates, escape (Lollius killing the crew), are shipwrecked, swim to shore
(Lollius bearing Leontia, praying to Neptune, their tender exchange mid-swim), are spared by a lion, drift three days
in an open boat singing, and finally **die of hunger in each other's arms**, kissing — now both held in "the narrowness
of this little urn." All secure; a masterpiece of the sepulchral-narrative genre.

- **The great romance-epitaph.** This is the Polyandrion's emotional centre — not a curse (Laodia, p. 252) nor a
  refusal (Nevia, p. 256), but *mutual, faithful love destroyed by fortune*: two who loved truly, endured pirates,
  shipwreck and a lion, and died together starving, still kissing. "They ought to have lived." It is the tragic ideal
  against which the whole Polyandrion is measured. Prime material to voice in the world/novel — a complete story in one
  inscription, and deeply moving.
- **the tenderness mid-catastrophe** — the dialogue while swimming ("Am I a trouble to thee?" / "Lighter than a
  water-skater, little heart") and while starving ("It is enough to be with thee — I am fed") is the passage's genius:
  intimacy inside disaster. Colonna's "beautiful and appropriate" writing at its height.
- **"the narrowness of this little urn here holds them both."** What pirates, lion and sea could not take, one small
  urn contains: the epitaph's closing turn, the vastness of their ordeal against the smallness of the grave. A line
  worth surfacing whole.
- **the address to the traveller** — like Nevia's (p. 256), it commands the passer-by to *pray, kiss the stone, and
  grieve*: the epitaph reaches out of the tomb to move the reader, and succeeds (Poliphilo wept, p. 257).
