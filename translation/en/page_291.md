stalks and tendril-lacings, or incisions, and fringings, most elegantly expressed

beautifully, descending inlaid from the pointed summit of the flat, following the
shaping of the gunwales — that is, the rail, or lip, where the tholes were fixed —
a marvellous frieze, a palm's breadth wide, all of gold and gemmed, of incredible
cost, egregiously girding it with a marvellous and friendly distribution and
placing of the gems; and all the wooden composite so diligently, and with
exceptional polish, with the planks joined plated fashion without the caulker's
art, and without any wedging, appearing an even-matched joinery, as of one whole
solid. And over the coating of the fragrant and blackest pitch, gleaming and
mirror-like, and with a gold trituration, throughout most admirably designed in a
Syrian lineament, with all the other circumstances above fittingly described. Thus
it was.

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** This is one of the book's technical set-pieces — the
anatomy of Cupid's hexareme — and it is the hardest kind of Colonna to render: a
specialist vocabulary (some of it his own coinage) applied to a boat that is half
ship and half goldsmith's work. The sense of every clause is recoverable, but a
naval historian could refine several terms.

- **The "dolphin" stern-ornament** — the *aplustre*, the curled stern-post of an
  ancient galley, here shaped as a dolphin's curve and set with gems. The boat is
  built like a real hexareme (six banks of oars, three thwarts a side) but dressed
  as jewellery: gold-plated keel and ribs, a gemmed frieze a palm wide along the
  rails, the hull lacquered with mirror-black pitch inlaid with gold "Syrian"
  arabesque.
- **"without the caulker's art, and without any wedging"** — the planks fit so
  perfectly that the boat seems carved from one solid, needing neither caulking nor
  wedges. The paragone of craft: art surpassing the necessity that usually shows
  in a real hull.
- **`exeres`** — a hexareme, a galley with six files of oarsmen; kept as "ship of
  six banks" on p. 290. **tholes** (*scalmi*) are the pins the oars pivot on.
- Terms rendered by their standard Vitruvian/nautical equivalents where one exists
  (*transtri* → thwarts, *carina* → keel, *coste* → ribs); a reviewer with a
  glossary of Quattrocento shipbuilding could tighten `oruli`, `trapheco`, and the
  fractional slopes.
