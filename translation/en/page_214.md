# Page 214 — Chapter XVII

In a tablet of Magnet, to the right of the ingress, was carved, of exquisite antiquarian Latin letters,
that celebrated Virgilian saying:

> **TRAHIT SUA QUEMQUE VOLUPTAS** — "Each is drawn by his own pleasure." *(Virgil, Eclogue II.65)*

On the left, the tablet I saw, of most-ancient Greek majuscules, elegantly inscribed:

> **ΠΑΝ ΔΕΙ ΠΟΙΕΙΝ ΚΑΤΑ ΤΗΝ ΑΥΤΟΥ ΦΥΣΙΝ** — in Latin, "Each ought to do according to his own nature."

Raising then the eyes, curiously stimulated, to the magnificence of so great a Temple, and to the vastness
of the noteworthy and sky-reaching cupola, with the other most-exact parts — of ambition, and of outstanding
artifice, of divine device, and of superb workmanship, and wondrous lineaments, of stupendous ostentation,
seasoned most-marvellously. And much more marvellous I judged the incredible beauty of the divine Nymph,
which enticed my inspecting eyes, and held all my soul — insomuch that from the right disquisition of
whatever considerable part of it (the temple) I might move, and she alone, drawing, coarcted (me) to
contemplate her with stupor and marvel. Give pardon, then, reader, if I have not perscribed every fitting
particular.

And so, then, the sacred Priestess entering the Temple, with the ingenuous and outstanding Nymph, and I
pertinacious following her, and with all the other sacred Damsels — with their most-rich hair falling, most-
ornate, over the milky necks, dressed in most-choice purple, and above, worn back, the most-tenuous cotton
(robes) shorter, or curt, than the first garment — to the fateful orifice of the mysterious cistern, devout
and festive, they conducted us.

Into which, as was said, no other water entered save that which, through the perforated Pilasters, from the
water-channels and compluvia of the fastigiate Temple, freely, without harm to the structure, poured itself
in. The high priestess there made a nod to the virgins, and they went into an Adytum sacristy; we three
alone remained.

## Notes

**Confidence: high** (the two inscriptions are secure and identified); medium for the surrounding prose. The
**two door-mottoes** — Latin **TRAHIT SUA QUEMQUE VOLUPTAS** ("each is drawn by his own pleasure," Virgil) on
the right, and the Greek **"act according to one's own nature"** on the left; then the entry into the temple,
the apology to the reader (the nymph's beauty distracts him from describing it), and the approach to the
cistern, the virgins withdrawing so "we three alone remained." All secure.

- **The two mottoes — the temple's thesis.** Flanking the door: *Trahit sua quemque voluptas* (Virgil, Ecl.
  II) and its Greek partner, *each acts according to his own nature*. Together they state the creed of Venus
  Physizoa's temple — desire is natural law; each being follows its own pleasure and nature. This is the
  philosophical inscription-pair of the whole temple (as AMOR VINCIT OMNIA is the boat's, p. 284, and QUIS
  EVADET/NEMO the triumph's). Prime material to surface in the world/novel at the temple threshold.
- **the magnet carries the mottoes** — the tablets are themselves *of lodestone* (the magnet of the door,
  p. 213): the stone that draws iron carries the text "each is drawn by his pleasure." Colonna makes the
  material pun with the meaning — attraction, literal and erotic, is one thing.
- **the apology to the reader** — Poliphilo confesses he cannot describe the temple fully because Polia/the
  nymph's beauty keeps pulling his attention: the inexpressibility topos turned personal, and a wry admission
  that the love-story overrides the architecture.
- **"we three alone remained"** — the priestess, the nymph and Poliphilo, at the well: the rite of Venus (the
  drawing of sacred water/fire) is about to be performed for the initiates alone (as Cythera's revelation was
  for the few, p. 352). The implements arrive on p. 215.
