# Page 359 — Chapter XXIII

Which fountain, at the very middle of this more-than-human building, was
divinely constructed and set out in this manner.

Of that blackest stone which alone, and entire, made the whole paving — that is,
the floor of the area — there rose at its centre a little wall a foot high,
brought to an excellent polish and furnished with every fitting ornament,
heptagonal on the outside and round within. It had its running cymatium and
socle, its small plinths and its wave-mouldings, wrought and set in due order
beneath the bases; and over the middle point of each angle there was built up a
column swelling in the shaft — that is, bellied — seven of them in all, turned
with the utmost exquisiteness. Two of these stood answering one another,
equally, opposite the entrance, in full view of where we knelt face down.

One of these lathe-turned columns, on the right hand, shone blue with the finest
sapphire; and on the left one glowed spring-green with an emerald of a colour so
surpassing, and more brilliant, than the stones set for eyes in the lion on the
tomb of Hermias the prince. Nor was such a one given by Ptolemy to Lucullus; nor
was that of so great price which the King of Babylon presented to the King of
Egypt; nor were those of the obelisk in the Temple of Jove of such estimation;
nor was the statue in the temple of Hercules at Tyre so great a marvel, as this
one showed itself to be.

Next to this followed a column of turquoise stone, most beautifully coloured a
lovely cerulean, endowed graciously with its virtue; and though it was an opaque
stone, it was nonetheless most brilliant, and shone back like a mirror.

Touching the sapphire column there stood another precious one, opaque also, of a
most delightful colour like melilot, and with a lustre like the translucent
flower of the buttercup. Adjoining this was one of jasper of a glassy colour, and
the other of topaz flashing with a golden colour. The seventh, alone and
singularly, was hexagonal, of the most limpid Indian beryl with an oily sheen,
giving back whatever stood before it reversed.

And this one answered to the midpoint between the first two — because every
figure of an odd number of angles has one angle facing the middle of the interval
between two others. So then: the circle drawn about its half-diameter, an
equilateral triangle set up within it, and then a line from the centre, and in
the middle of that line —

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The gem-lore and the column order are secure — Colonna
names each stone plainly and the Plinian comparisons are identifiable. Two
weaker areas: the mouldings of the kerb (*cimasula, socco, arulette,
undiculatione*) are a technical vocabulary I have rendered by their standard
Vitruvian equivalents without being certain Colonna means exactly those members;
and the geometrical construction in the last paragraph is compressed to the
point of obscurity, so I have translated it literally rather than smoothing it
into a proof. **A reviewer should look hardest at the last paragraph.**

- **The seven columns and their stones**, in the order Colonna gives them:
  sapphire (right of the entrance), emerald (left), turquoise, a melilot-coloured
  opaque stone, jasper, topaz, and — alone, and hexagonal rather than round —
  Indian beryl. Colonna does not say the seven are the seven planets.
  **A sixteenth-century reader did.** The annotator called Hand B, working on the
  British Library copy, labelled the heptagonal-fountain woodcut with the
  alchemical sign of a different metal at each of its seven angles, one per
  planet (`hp.db`, `folio_descriptions` y7r, "Fons Heptagonis: The Seven
  Metals"). The planetary reading is therefore not a modern imposition but a
  documented contemporary one, and the Dream Garden follows it on that authority.
- **`enthesiata, overo ventriculata`** — *entasis*, the swelling of a column
  shaft that Vitruvius prescribes so a column does not appear pinched. Colonna
  glosses his own Greek with the plainer Italian, so the translation keeps both:
  "swelling in the shaft — that is, bellied."
- **`petra caeca`** — literally "blind stone," the period term for an opaque
  gem as against a transparent one. Rendered "opaque," with the contrast to the
  transparent stones kept visible.
- **`vatrachio`** — βατράχιον, the buttercup, whose petals have exactly the
  waxy translucency Colonna wants for the stone's lustre.
- **`in contrario gli obiecti reddendo`** — beryl's double refraction, which
  returns an image reversed. He is describing an optical property, not a
  decorative one.
- **The lion on the tomb of Hermias** — Pliny reports a lion monument whose eyes
  were emeralds. Colonna's chain of comparisons here is all Plinian gem-lore; we
  leave the allusions unglossed in the text, as he does.
