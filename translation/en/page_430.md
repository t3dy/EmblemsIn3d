# Page 430 — Chapter XXX

"— Mother and Lady (Venus), that she command her veiled son (Cupid), that he, licitly, re-assume his amorous arms, and
that, into that stony heart, that pricking and penetrable dart, indignant, he draw — which, in me, without any pity, thus,
lancing, he fixed; and, by such equity and mode, will (satisfaction) be, to my little sadnesses, repayingly satisfied; and
modified will be all my importunate and urgent sighs, and so many languors — which, however onerous and molesting, patient
and willing I would avail to support, if Polia likewise should feel, somewhat, what thing is intense loving, and how sweet
and delectable is the being of two hearts coming into one. Whence, most-clement Priestess, if thus thou make coequal this
displeasing dissimilitude, most-blessed I hold myself. For so much, let no admiration, sublime Lady, seize thee, that I
assume ardently a venerable daring, and, of such a cause, reluctant, to speak.

"Wherefore thou oughtest to know that too much more love than befits invades and rams me, and, stimulating, exacerbates me;
and, to this (postponed every other thing), constrains and presses me. Nor ever do I hope of so many my torments to evade,
nor quietment, nor term to consequute — save whenever I think (thou, pious mediatrix) to have placated, and, placating,
settled, and, settling, delineated (soothed) the displeasing heart, and the truculence, of her, of so sweet and divine an
aspect, feigning (deceptive) — which, with so much comely elegance, an excellent and salubrious help, by the ministry of
those enticing eyes, through the middle of my redundant heart, promises me no mediocre hope, with every voluptuousness of
mitigating my incredible griefs, and of lining somewhat my petulant and ardent fires — if, at present, I can re-unite the
will and her mind, unequal from mine, and more separated from mine (O me!) than Ossa from Olympus. For I so extremely love
her, that never have I found myself mine, but ever all hers. A just thing it is that, as I am all hers, and a humble
servant, she, uniformly, be all mine, venerable lady, and totally possessress. Then thou, excellent sacrificer — being
sole and chief, in whose sentence consists and depends the power to commune (us) under this amorous Yoke, and, with the
utmost expertise, to master and discipline those who, wholly, with sincere and pure heart, are addicted to this holy
service, of the most-holy and mysterious flames perpetually to serve — that now, if I find not myself deceived, I believe,
and (hope) that this ingenuous and comely, and of singular virtue preclear light, and of beauty celestial splendour, with
me (in this place convened) consents to such services to be received and re-numbered (enrolled)."

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Poliphilo's petition in full: he asks the priestess to pray Venus to **command Cupid to strike
Polia's stony heart** with the dart, so she love him and the two become "one" — to "make coequal this displeasing
dissimilitude"; he professes total love ("never have I found myself mine, but ever all hers"), and hopes Polia now
consents to be "enrolled" in Venus's service. All secure; the plea for the union completed.

- **"Two hearts coming into one."** Poliphilo's whole prayer is for *equality* of love — that Polia be struck as he was
  struck, so their "dissimilitude" (he burning, she cold) be made "coequal." This is the union the temple exists to
  effect (and Book I's rite performed): not his conquest of her, but the equalising of two into one. His "never found
  myself mine, but all hers" is the self-surrender that love, made mutual, will requite ("she be all mine").
- **the dramatic irony.** Poliphilo, not knowing Polia has *already* converted (chs. XXIX — her side, which he did not
  witness), prays for what has already happened: Cupid has already struck her stony heart (p. 419). The double narration
  gives the reader what the character lacks — we know Polia is already his. His plea is answered before he finishes it,
  which is why Polia "interrupts the response" (argument, p. 428) — she can no longer contain the love he is praying for
  her to feel. A neat structural payoff of the two-books design.
- **"Ossa from Olympus."** Their wills were once as far apart as the piled mountains of the Giants — a learned image for
  the "dissimilitude" now being closed. Colonna's erudition serves the emotional reconciliation.
- **the priestess's office, restated** — she alone can "commune them under the amorous yoke" and enrol them in "the
  service of the mysterious flames": the temple-rite of Book I (the joining at the well). Poliphilo's petition sets up the
  scene Book I dramatised. Polia's interruption (her overflowing love) follows on p. 431, closing the loop.
