# Page 24 — Chapter III

Each face of the squared base of the mass, below the beginning of the stepping of this admirable
Pyramid set above the aforesaid building, was in length six stadia. Multiplied by four in
circumference, the said equilateral footing took up, all told, four and twenty stadia. Then in
height, raising the lines from each angle by measure — as far as the lowest line of the plinth
extends — all four running together and meeting at the middle of the summit constituted the perfect
Pyramidal figure. The middle perpendicular, above the centre where the diagonals of the Plinth cross,
was one part less in six than the ascending lines.

Which immense and terrible Pyramid, rising by steps adamantine with wonderful and exquisite Symmetry,
contained one thousand four hundred and ten steps, or stairs, wrought in relief — apart from ten
steps needed to finish the tapering. In place of these was set and substituted a stupendous Cube,
solid and firm and monstrous in its thickness, offering itself beyond belief as a thing carried up
and appointed to that summit; of that same Parian stone of which the steps were made.

The said square was set beneath as the base and support of the obelisk that is to be spoken of. This
most huge stone — such as was never the block quarried by Titis — had a falling-away in its
circumference of six parts, two in the descent and one in the pointed level; and it remained on the
topmost plane four paces broad by diameter. In the levelling of which there rose four Harpy's feet of
metal, with their pads and clawed talons cast, fixed into the great stone towards the angles, along
the diagonal lines, and firmly leaded in; of proportioned thickness, and of the height of two paces.
Which, knotting themselves most beautifully together, encircled and bound the lowest Socle of a great
Obelisk, cast in wonderful foliage, and fruits, and flowers of fitting greatness. Upon which the
Obelisk pressed, most firmly set above them. Its breadth two paces, and seven times as much in
height, artificially sharpening itself, of Theban fire-flecked stone. On the faces of which were
Egyptian Hieroglyphs excellently carved, smooth, and brightly polished like a mirror.

On the topmost pinnacle of it, set above with the utmost diligence and art, rested a settled base of
brass. In which there was besides a turning engine, or broad-brimmed cap, fixed upon a stable pivot
or pole. Which held up an image of a Nymph, an elegant work of the material already named — enough to
turn to stupefaction anyone who considered it most carefully and with fixed gaze. Of such and so
