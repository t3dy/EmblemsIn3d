## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** **Polia's conversion — the flame kindles.** Alone after the nurse's counsel, thinking of the
Poliphilo she killed, she feels **Love finally penetrate her "hard and torpid heart"** — the first sweet flame nesting and
spreading — and she resolves to submit to Love; but a catalogue of the metamorphosis-victims of illicit love (Phyllis,
Dido, Stheneboea, Scylla, Echo, Byblis, Dryope, Myrrha, Nyctimene, Menthe) rises to terrify her. All secure; the turning-
point of Book II.

- **The frozen heart thaws — at last.** This is the moment the whole book works toward: the "hard and torpid heart" (the
  porphyry, p. 389; the wax-on-wet-rock, p. 399) finally admits Love's "first sweet little flame," which "runs and dilates
  through the cordial parts to the intimate base." It is the exact fulfilment, from within, of the Venus-rite's prayer to
  "re-kindle her stone-made, gelid heart" (p. 216). Book I's rite and Book II's conversion are the same event, told from
  the two sides. The great convergence.
- **love enters through remorse.** Crucially, the flame is kindled by the memory of *the man she killed* — "ignorant by
  what celestial care led," Poliphilo comes to mind, and Love finds "a little entry" there. Her conversion is love born of
  guilt: she loves him because she wronged him. The remorse she could not feel (p. 404) becomes, now, love.
- **the fear of metamorphosis.** Against the new flame, her learned memory throws up the whole gallery of women destroyed
  or transformed by illicit love — the metamorphosis-victims (Dido's suicide, Byblis and Dryope and Myrrha turned to
  trees/springs, Nyctimene to an owl, Menthe to mint). She fears love's dangers even as she yields. The transformation-
  theme (the aviary, p. 310; her own family, p. 384) returns as her private terror. Her resolve is hard-won.
- Her decision to go revive Poliphilo (and her Anaxarete-fear) completes on p. 419.
