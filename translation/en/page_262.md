# Page 262 — Chapter XIX

> **DIIS MANIBUS.**
> **MORS, VITAE CONTRARIA ET VELOCISSIMA, CUNCTA CALCAT, SUPPEDITAT, RAPIT, CONSUMIT, DISSOLVIT. MELLIFLUE DUOS,**
> **MUTUO SE STRICTIM ET ARDENTER AMANTES, HIC EXTINCTOS, CONIUNXIT.**
>
> "To the shades. Death — contrary to life, and most swift — treads on all things, tramples, seizes, consumes, dissolves.
> Mellifluously (sweetly) it has here joined two, who mutually, tightly and ardently loved each other, (now) extinguished."

Gladdened, with incredible solace, by so much variety of antiquarian and magnificent works, still, without doubt, my soul
increased (in wishing), insatiably, more lustrating, to investigate other novelties. Whence, even if before, tearful, the
Greek epitaph of the two miserable lovers dead of starvation (Leontia and Lollius) had excited me — much more, again, there
presented itself to me a much-admired but pitiable monument, of two other most-unhappy lovers, standing in a great stone,
with such lineament expressed: a square, by the diagonal raised (set lozenge-wise), contained in itself two little
pilasters, with a little cornice and a half-hemicycle above; between which, from the angles of the little arch, hung a
tablet, on which, reading, I saw such a pitiable epigram.

---

## Notes

**Confidence: high** (the hieroglyph-epitaph is secure). The **Egyptian hieroglyph** on the ivory chest decoded — a
meditation on Death (*"contrary to life and most swift, it treads on all… (yet) sweetly has here joined two who loved each
other ardently, now extinguished"*); then Poliphilo turns to yet another tomb, of "two other most-unhappy lovers" (its
epitaph on p. 263). All secure.

- **"Death… sweetly joined two lovers."** The chest's hieroglyph turns death's terror into a consolation: the same power
  that "treads on all, consumes, dissolves" also *unites* the two lovers forever in one grave. It is the Polyandrion's
  gentlest doctrine — death as the joiner of the faithful (cf. Cornelia Annia, p. 261; Leontia & Lollius's shared urn,
  p. 258). A resonant inscription for the world's crypt of the two preserved bodies.
- **the emotional accumulation.** Poliphilo notes the tombs are affecting him in sequence — the Leontia epitaph made him
  weep, and now "much more" this one. The chapter is a rising curve of pathos, building toward the Rape-of-Proserpina
  relief that finally frightens him back to Polia (pp. 280+). The world/novel can pace its polyandrion as this same
  escalation.
- **the lozenge-set tomb** — a square set diagonally with pilasters and an arched tablet: another buildable tomb-form (cf.
  the temple-lidded sepulchre, p. 260). Colonna varies the monument-types tomb by tomb, a catalogue of funerary
  architecture.
- **death "mellifluously" (melliflue)** — the startling adverb: death acting *sweetly*. Colonna's oxymoron holds the
  Polyandrion's whole paradox — the tomb of love's victims is also, in its own dark way, love's fulfilment (the lovers
  united at last).
