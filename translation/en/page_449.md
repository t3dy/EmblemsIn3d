"— complacenting thee (in) that which, loving, unpremeditatedly befalls, most of the times, (to) whoever intensely loves.
At present, wishing to persuade (that), in those (cases), opportune and useful is perseverance — thou shalt understand

were touchstone-stone, or of human material congested; all the while the ever-vigilant Love assiduously stimulating, and
alone smearing and anointing the insolent appetite of blandishing hope. Such a tenor I wrote her:

*(Poliphilo's third letter to Polia:)*

"Sooner would I consume my tongue, ingenuous and most-noble adolescent-girl, than ever it could somewhat, upon the candid
papyrus, express how toilsome, how grave, how acerb is my bitter pain — which, day and night, in the languishing heart,
re-doubled, increases without intermission — seeing thee thus deaf and displeasing. And only because I recognise, too,
thee not to be content, and satiated, still, of my grave torments (no less, in the least — nay, excessively greater — than
those which, not much time (ago), twice I have sweetly written thee). But, since the fallacious, the forced, the savage,
and dolorific Love, with the impious fortune and my adversative star, necessarily constrain me, to thee, voluntary, to
handmaid and serve — O Nymph, above the human grasp, of wondrous beauty, and, of conspicuous and elegant filament, most-
admired; but, above all and whatever other, I dare say, excessively pitiless and cruel, like a sylvan and untamed beast,
more than the most-immense and famished Lion of Androcles, rigid and displeasing. Which things belie the human seed, and
that thy meek and divine simulacrum, of thy comely aspect pre-shining, and of that celestial and rare make, denigrate —
of humanity naked, and rebelling to the amorous little fires of Cytherea, and to the divine Empire of the shrewd Nature a
spurner. For, a juridical cause, and odious experience, alluring me, transport (me), (that I ought) to speak of so much
appreciable time vainly consumed... amorously afflicting thee, and day and night to that colloricated (heated), detained,
occupied, and useless, having lost (it) — inflamed and dried up, loving thee, sole elected to destroy my life — by that
which I manifestly see: that, the more I love thee, so much the more it seems that I, hardening thee, lapidise (turn thee
to stone). Ah, Polia — can it be that, in thee, would not be found one atom of piteous spirit, that a little gracious
audition, with internuncio (messenger) little epistles, I could find in thee — nor with pre-strepent sighs, nor with
affluent and frequent little tears, from my moist eyes vaporable, solicitous, irroring, which every hour weep —"

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Poliphilo sends the **third letter**, Polia unmoved "as Mount Olympus by the winds"; the letter
reproaches her cruelty (worse than the lion of Androcles, "of humanity naked, a spurner of Cytherea and of Nature"), and
makes the terrible observation: **"the more I love thee, the more I lapidise thee"** — his love hardens her to stone. All
secure; the third, most desperate plea.

- **"The more I love thee, the more I turn thee to stone."** Poliphilo's darkest insight — his love, instead of softening
  Polia, *hardens* her ("lapidise") — captures the tragedy of chs. XXVI from his side. His warmth met her stone and, far
  from melting it, seemed to harden it further. (The reader knows only the visions and Cupid's arrow, not love's pleas,
  finally melt her.) The stone-imagery, hers throughout, is here diagnosed by him.
- **the Agon persevered.** Twice-failed, Poliphilo writes a *third* time — "not leaving the begun Agon," testing "whether
  her heart were touchstone-stone or human." His perseverance is heroic and hopeless: he keeps writing into a heart of
  stone. The chapter's theme (perseverance, p. 436) at its most poignant — constancy that cannot succeed by its own
  efforts.
- **"a spurner of Nature."** He charges Polia with rebelling against "the divine Empire of shrewd Nature" (Venus
  Physizoa, generative nature) — the same charge the nurse made (p. 412): to refuse love is to offend Nature. His letter
  argues the book's creed, but arguments cannot move her. The letter continues on p. 450.
