my Polia singing along, dispensed celestial melodies to the open ears.

By which the black and multiform and lucubratory Cerberus would have been lulled; nor would he have kept
watch, with unmoving eyes, the metallic valves of Taenarum. And then the raging Tisiphone, with the
monster-bearing sisters, to the wretched souls would have shown themselves most-placid and benign. Nor
ever were Parthenope, with her sisters Leucosia and Ligeia — daughters of Achelous and of Calliope —
singing at the Capri isles near Pelorus, heard with so much harmony, of voice, mode, Lyre, and pierceable
tibia; whence the soul, incendiarily inflamed, removed from its place by the happy songs, and sounds,
effigies, beauties, retinue, and majesty, I could not redeem, nor make it my own. But, most-tightly
bound, it commended its estate into the delicate arms, and in the whitening bosom of Polia, a perpetual
and surrendered hostage, obliged it. Which then, cogitating, by delectable paths and voluptuous conduits,
arrived at the arcane delights. And thence, with all my excited virtues drawn into myself, I could resume
nothing else but a solacing imaginative, and glorious.

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The great surpassing-conceit of the song: the whole pantheon (Phoebus, Diana,
Selene, Proserpina, Bacchus, Ceres, Jove's eagle, Cerberus, the Furies) would have abandoned their
offices to listen, and even the **Sirens** (Parthenope, Leucosia, Ligeia) never sang so; and Poliphilo's
soul, ravished from its place, surrenders as a "perpetual hostage" in Polia's bosom. All secure; a
sustained mythological catalogue on the power of the music.

- **The music that would halt the gods.** Colonna runs the surpassing-topos to its limit: the Sun would
  forget the dawn, Diana would spare Actaeon, Ceres keep the corn green, Jove's eagle drop Ganymede — all
  to hear the nymphs and Polia sing. Music suspends the whole order of nature and myth. The chapter's
  argument (music's power over the soul) is made total.
- **surpassing the Sirens** — the ultimate comparison for dangerous, soul-drawing song (Parthenope and her
  sisters, the Sirens of the Campanian coast): the nymphs exceed even them. Yet here the song does not
  destroy but *binds* Poliphilo to Polia — Sirens' power turned to love's fidelity.
- **the soul as "perpetual hostage" in Polia's bosom** — the fidelity-thread again (cf. pp. 314, 337): the
  music removes Poliphilo's soul from himself, and it lodges, surrendered, in Polia. His rapture is
  channelled straight back to her.
- **"celestial flesh… like crystalline camphor tinged with crimson"** — the singers' throats are
  transparent, the voice visible passing through: Colonna's synaesthetic image of divine song made almost
  physical light.
