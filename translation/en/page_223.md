## Notes

**Confidence: high** (the Graces-invocation is secure and identified); medium for the altar-detail above it.
The altar's gem-festoons finished (nine gemmed threads, a platter carved with putti, monsters, flowers), and
then the **priestess's invocation of the three Graces** — Aglaia, Thalia, Euphrosyne — as handmaids of Venus,
called from the Acidalian spring and Apollo's throne to carry Polia's prayer to the goddess. All secure.

- **The invocation of the three Graces.** A luminous, quotable prayer: the Charites — **Aglaia** ("splendour"),
  **Thalia** ("good cheer"), **Euphrosyne** ("mirth") — daughters of Jove and Eurynome, Venus's own handmaids,
  are called from the **Acidalian spring** (Venus's bathing-pool at Orchomenus) to bear Polia's sacrifice to
  the goddess "with maternal affect." A ready piece of liturgy for the world's temple-rite and the visual-
  novel — the three Graces named and invoked. Its epithets (joyful/verdant/delectable) are gently exact.
- **the classical sourcing** — the Graces' parentage (Jove and Eurynome), their spring (Acidalia at
  Orchomenus in Boeotia), and their service to Venus are all from Hesiod and the mythographers: Colonna's
  invocation is scholarly as well as beautiful. The Graces as *intercessors* (carrying the prayer up to Venus)
  is his own devotional framing.
- **the whole company kneels but the priestess** — the *Antistita* alone stands (as celebrant); all others,
  including Polia, kneel on the mirror-floor. The liturgical choreography is exact.
- The prayer completes on p. 224, with the responsory "So be it," closing chapter XVII.
