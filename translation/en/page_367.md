## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The healing-and-renewal sequence is secure; the two
transformation-similes (Aeson, Hippolytus) are identified. The alchemical verb
`fermentare` recurs and is kept literal. The page breaks as Mars arrives.

- **Byblis** — who wept for love of her brother until she dissolved into a
  spring (Ovid). The image continues the run of transformation-myths, and here
  it is the *dissolving* into water that Poliphilo's swoon echoes.
- **`fermentare gli nostri accensi amori`** — "to ferment our kindled loves."
  Venus's act of binding the lovers is named with the alchemical operation for
  the fourth time in this chapter (cf. p. 364, `fermentosa natura`). Colonna is
  insistent, and so is the translation.
- **Aeson** — Jason's aged father, boiled young again by Medea's herbs. **The
  twice-living Hippolytus** (*virbio*, Virbius) — killed, then raised by
  Asclepius at Diana's plea. Both are resurrection-by-herb, matching Poliphilo's
  own revival by the goddess's sea-dew. *glycyside* is the peony, a real
  medicinal in Pliny.
- **The dew that transmutes, not destroys** — Colonna sets Venus's sprinkling
  explicitly against Diana's on Actaeon (again — cf. p. 362): the same act of a
  goddess casting water on a mortal who has seen her, but here it heals and
  clothes rather than turning to a stag. The chapter answers its own earlier
  dread.
- **"things unlawful to publish, to vulgar men not to be told"** — the standard
  formula of the mystery religions (and of alchemical secrecy), marking Venus's
  final instruction as an initiation.
