And, having integrally narrated the befallen cases of so much perturbative horror, and the apparitions, nocturnal and
diurnal, seen; and the used immanities; and to have been more dire and savaging than a Tigress, and more deaf to his
lamentations of his grave griefs and amorous pains than a stopped Asp (which, by enchantment, moves not); more displeasing
than Dictynna (Diana) to Minos; making little of his prayers and miserable weepings; with hostile hatred and rage toward my
Poliphilo; and to have been of mercy un-prompt, of pity naked, of humanity austere and alien, and of compassion unmoved.
Whence, almost of these such committed rebellions terrifying myself, acerbly reprehending myself, and repenting, tediously,
in my very self — it seemed vain to think of the excluded miseries. But, contaminated and compelled by an unmeasured
cordial agitation, and infected of perfused ardour, I re-began, much more now, to languish for love of my Poliphilo — who,
as soon as he was aware of my access, the most-avid eyes opposite converting, suddenly the mordant gaze, perpetual, ran
(such as a swift arrow, from a drawn bow directed) — into my prepared and freely-disposed heart it fixed itself, so that,
of amorous sweetness, through all me, I felt (myself) crackle and up-boil.

Then, most-placid Nymphs, to that reverend presence inclining myself, I besought pardon of the preterite, and, of the
present Agon, confirmation — offering myself, of the venerable Lady Mother (Venus), a veracious and intrepid worshipper; and
never to wish to rebel, nor to be failing, nor dissentaneous, to whatever empire of her potent son; nor to whatever coveted
desire of my amorous lord Poliphilo, to refuse — but benign, and pious, and obsequent, and graciously yielding, nor ever
disjoined; and, with the utmost observance, to his amorous vows prompt, and all most-devoted; and to live with him, with
more peace and sincere concord than the Geryones lived together. Scarce made the irrevocable espousals, than the Sacred
Priestess called Poliphilo into her presence.

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The close of **chapter XXIX** and the seam into Book I: Polia comes to Venus's temple (finding
Poliphilo already there, praying to be restored), **confesses her whole cruelty** to the priestess ("more dire than a
Tigress, more deaf than a stopped Asp… of pity naked"), repents, is struck by Poliphilo's loving gaze, and **vows herself
Venus's worshipper and Poliphilo's, never to refuse him** — whereupon the priestess **calls Poliphilo forward**. All
secure; Book II rejoins Book I.

- **The seam: Book II meets Book I.** This scene — Polia at Venus's temple, confessing to the priestess, vowing herself to
  love, the priestess calling Poliphilo forward — is the *same* temple-rite Book I gave from Poliphilo's side (ch. XVII,
  the priestess who unites the lovers, pp. 216–218). Book II has told the whole history behind that moment: Polia's
  lineage, cruelty, punishment, and conversion, ending exactly where Book I's dream showed the union. The two books now
  interlock. (In the world's terms, this is the origin of the union the Venus-temple station depicts.)
- **the confession — full remorse at last.** Polia names all her cruelty without excuse ("of pity naked, of compassion
  unmoved… hostile hatred toward my Poliphilo") and repents — the complete confession her whole narration built toward
  (from "swinish ferocity," p. 382, to here). Her conversion is now moral as well as emotional: she owns the crime and
  renounces it.
- **the vow of obedient love.** She vows to be "Venus's veracious worshipper… never to refuse Poliphilo… graciously
  yielding" — the exact reversal of her refusal (ch. XXVI). Her betrothal-vow completes the union Book I sealed. The proud,
  cruel maiden is now the devoted lover.
- **Poliphilo's gaze as arrow.** His loving look strikes her heart "like a swift arrow from a drawn bow" — she now receives
  the wound she once dealt. The lovers are at last mutually pierced. Chapter XXIX ends on the threshold of their formal
  reunion before the priestess — where Book I's temple-scene takes over.
