# Page 264 — Chapter XIX

Having read the compassionate case of the unfortunate lovers of this most-outstanding epigram — from here, again
supremely content, departing, not many paces making — there occurred to me a noble tablet of tetragonal marble, with
somewhat of fastigium (pediment), with two little columns, one to a side; of design immune and unencumbered. And, to
the compass of all the quadrangle, was, as if uprooted (in relief), a foliate crown, or jewel, diligently conducted;
in which I read such an inscription. Which stone lay cast down on the earth, but with the workmanship supine (face up).
Which elegant varieties not a little pleasure, in my soul, joyously accumulated.

More ardently invaded (I) found myself to the investigation of the most-worthy antiquarian works. There presented
itself to me a disrupted **tribune**, with the right wall reserved, in which I saw, with excessive voluptuousness, a
**porphyry sepulchre**, of most-worthy device, and of most-excellent workmanship, and of marvellous cost, and of
incredible artifice of sculpture. Which stood — at the extreme sides raised by two quadrangular little columns (a
third of them worked out, with regulated flutings, upon a footing with the base collocated) — and, perpendicularly
subjacent, was a little altar, with three elegant mourning Nymphs, toward the middle turned, weeping together. By the
like, on the other side, semi-detached from the solid, not without the corollary and requisite lineaments. Above both
the capitals the epistyle, ornate, extended itself. Then, with volutes of most-comely fronds and flowers —

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** After the Lopidia epitaph, a marble tablet with a foliate-crown relief, then the introduction
of a magnificent **porphyry sepulchre** in a surviving tribune — flanked by fluted columns and little altars carved
with **three mourning nymphs** weeping toward the centre, its entablature scrolled with foliage. All secure; this is
the tomb of Artemisia (identified on p. 265).

- **The grandest tomb yet.** Colonna signals this sepulchre as the chapter's architectural climax ("most-worthy
  device… incredible artifice"): a fully-articulated porphyry monument with flanking columns, mourning-nymph altars,
  and a figured entablature. It proves to be the tomb of **Artemisia** and **Mausolus** (p. 265) — the archetypal
  monument of conjugal love, the original "Mausoleum." A prime buildable centerpiece for the world's polyandrion.
- **the mourning nymphs** — three weeping figures on each flanking altar, "turned toward the middle": the tomb stages
  its own grief, sculpture mourning the dead. A ready motif for the world's polyandrion tombs (as on the Venus-temple
  well, p. 206).
- **Poliphilo's antiquarian delight deepens** — "more ardently invaded" by the desire to investigate: the graveyard-
  therapy (p. 242) has fully turned his erotic ardour into scholarly ardour. The tombs now *are* his pleasure.
- The tomb's inscriptions and figures (Artemisia drinking Mausolus's ashes) are read on p. 265.
