## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The climactic **homage of the marine gods to Cupid**: Nereus and Chloris, Neptune (trident, seal-
drawn), the conch-blowing Tritons, the Nereids on dolphins, Oceanus and Tethys with their daughters, grieving Aesacus,
plaintive Halcyone, Proteus, Glaucus and Scylla, and a throng of sea-monsters, birds and singing swans — all pay veneration
to the god as the boat crosses. Poliphilo, "more triumphant than any Roman, happier than Polycrates," is overwhelmed. All
secure; a full marine triumph.

- **The whole sea worships Love.** The marine pantheon — Neptune, the Nereids, Tritons, Oceanus, Proteus, Glaucus — comes
  to honour Cupid in procession, confirming Love's "monarchy" (p. 279) over even the deep. It is a sea-triumph answering the
  land-triumph of Cythera (ch. XXII): Love reigns on both. A spectacular, buildable escort for the world's crossing (the
  sea-gods rising around the boat). The chapter's argument (p. 241) named exactly this: "great veneration made to Cupid by
  the marine gods."
- **the transformation-figures, again.** Aesacus (turned to a diving bird, mourning), Halcyone (turned to a kingfisher,
  mourning Ceyx), Scylla, Glaucus: the sea-procession is thick with metamorphosis-myths (as the aviary was, p. 310), several
  of them *grieving* lovers — a faint echo of the Polyandrion just left, even amid the triumph.
- **"happier than Polycrates."** The tyrant of Samos, proverbially the most fortunate of men (until his fall): Poliphilo at
  his peak of joy. The surpassing-topos, on happiness — and a faint irony, since Polycrates' luck ended badly (the crossing's
  joy is real but the book's undertow of mortality persists).
- **the refreshing odour** — the page ends on a scent breathing from ahead (Cythera's perfumed air, p. 324): the island
  announces itself before it is seen. The last page of chapter XIX (281–282) completes the approach into chapter XX.
