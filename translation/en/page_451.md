By this way, then, I gave heed, sedulous, to reduce and humanate, and her sweetly to blandish, and to mitigate — soliciting
the asperity received from the initiated arduous and perilous work. But neither she, nor the perfid Love, consented to my
suasive words, with high and sworn espousals, openly showing her the nod and semblance of my — of every sweetness sweetest —
flames; (that) she, in return, with requisite reciprocation, love me. And with every industrious art, and shrewd cogitation,
I forced myself to kindle her of that veracious, nude, simple, and excellent affect, and amorous fire, in which, without any
remedy, continually — like a *Pyraulus* (fire-fly/flame-moth) — I nourished myself, wretched. And, beyond this, with her,
infinite times, with the mind I feigned to have a charming colloquy; and, ratiocinating (with her), daringly — intermixed,
frequently, the tormentable wailings — I said: 'O my little Nymph, of inhuman and ferine heart — of nature a soft girl (yet)
more than solid Steel, and more than murex-stone hardest, more tenacious than a retaining Grapnel, more obstinate than a
hinged Beam; more mordant than a rapt Talon, and much more than the cruel and foul Harpies rapacious of my heart: how canst
thou persevere in so much hardness and impiety? — more impious than Mithridates, more savage than Alcmaeon, more ungrateful
of so much affection than Paris toward Oenone.' — To my prayers, remove, then, these iniquitous abjections from thy Nymphal
heart, and this vulgar mark; and assent thyself, propitiated, to my suppliant petitions; concede, my Lady, that I consequute
the desired quiet; permit to penetrate thy auditory, my tormentable little sighs; consent to my ardent loves.

And, many, and to these similar, complaints and instances performed — in the least I could not move such agitation from my
continual grief, which, in me pressed, was so much in all the viscera occupant, and had so high germinated in the heart its
most-bitter roots, that, by other art, nor way, nor mode — save by its chief hope (Polia herself) — ever, to extirpate, I
know not, nor can, nor avail. And less profitable, still, were the groaning voices, about her superb palace vainly dispersed
— (she) more deaf than Icarus to the paternal admonitions, and more displeasing than Caunus to the desperate Byblis;
abominatrix of sweet love, superseding to the false and habituated opinions, in the tender and virgin age wont to harden. And
an ardous thing it is to leave that which, some time, in the soul is impressed: enerve, not easily can one. Thence, then, was
the exordium and origin, that I, simply ensnared and implicated, in these enveloping nets, and fallacious decoy, and in
these subtle, caducous, uncertain, fugacious and momentary snares and sharpnesses of love, enslaved — that, under this
molesting Tyranny and condition, and miserable servitude, subjugated and enrolled, I found one sole pleasure and alluring
delightment: to love her extremely; nor did I repugn to the flying (arrows) —"

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The **end of the third letter** ("by this way thy iniquitous will and my grief will cease.
Farewell"), and Poliphilo's continued **vain suit**: he pleads, imagines "charming colloquies" with her, catalogues her
hardness ("harder than steel, than murex-stone… more impious than Mithridates, more ungrateful than Paris to Oenone"),
haunts her palace — all fruitless, for love's roots are too deep in him to pull out except by "its chief hope, Polia
herself." All secure; the impasse of his unrequited love.

- **Love with no exit but the beloved.** Poliphilo's love is a "servitude" whose only "remedy" is Polia's own yielding —
  he cannot uproot it (its "roots germinated too high in the heart"), cannot flee it (the fish-trap, p. 447). His constancy
  is total and helpless: he can only keep loving and pleading, unto death. The perseverance the book praises is here shown
  as *inescapable devotion* — he perseveres because he cannot do otherwise.
- **the catalogue of her hardness.** "Harder than steel, than murex-stone; more impious than Mithridates; more ungrateful
  than Paris to Oenone" — Poliphilo's inventory of Polia's cruelty answers her own self-condemnation (she called herself
  worse than Anaxarete, Medea, p. 396). Both lovers, in their narrations, catalogue *her* cruelty — she confessing, he
  suffering it. The double account agrees on the crime.
- **the Pyraulus moth.** He "nourishes himself in love's fire like a Pyraulus" — the flame-moth that lives in fire (Pliny):
  the lover sustained by the very fire that consumes him, love as self-immolating sustenance. The sweet/bitter paradox
  (p. 279) in a single image.
- **love's one pleasure.** Even in this servitude he finds "one sole pleasure: to love her extremely." His love is its own
  reward, needing no return — the purest constancy. It is the love that will die for her and be revived by her. His death
  in the temple (the chapter's climax, from his side) follows on pp. 452+.
