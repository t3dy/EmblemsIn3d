commove myself; and at daybreak, in those all suffused, freezingly frozen I find myself; in the coruscating aurora, my

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The heart of **Poliphilo's plea**: his love-torment as an endless daily cycle (kindling at
evening, ash at cockcrow, freezing at dawn, dying at midday), a wound "more lethal than Philoctetes," gnawing "more than
the moth in wool"; only the imagined thought of Polia keeps his soul from departing. All secure; a bravura love-lament in
Polia's retelling, thick with natural and mythic similes.

- **The hours of love.** Poliphilo maps his suffering onto the whole day and night — a horological anatomy of love-
  torment (evening/twilight/midnight/cockcrow/dawn/morning/midday), each hour a different phase of burning or freezing.
  A striking, quotable structure (the lover as a clock of pain), and the fullest statement of the sweet/bitter paradox
  that governs the book (cf. p. 279).
- **the pests-similes.** His pain gnaws "more than the moth… the caterpillar… the woodworm… rust to steel": a catalogue
  of small relentless destroyers, exactly figuring love's slow corrosion. Colonna's natural-history erudition serving the
  emotion.
- **the plea's moral edge.** He charges Polia that she "showed herself guilty… without reason," and begs her at least to
  *imagine* feeling his pain if she cannot feel it: a demand for empathy from the empathy-less. Polia, narrating, records
  the charge against herself. His death-swoon follows (pp. 394–395).
