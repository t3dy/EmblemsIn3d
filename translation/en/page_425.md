## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Polia's **total transformation** ("from cold, fervid; from timid, magnanimous… the rigid breast a
little furnace of love; disdainful hatreds into inseparable loves"), her making of love-tokens (the hair-chaplet sent to
Poliphilo), and then a new vision: **Diana's ice-chariot** (drawn by stags, empty quiver, threatening vengeance) pursued by
**Venus's fire-chariot** (drawn by swans, roses, Cupid) bursting through her window. All secure; the conversion sealed and
the final divine confrontation begun.

- **The transformation, stated in full.** Colonna lists the complete reversal: cold→fervid, timid→magnanimous,
  bashful→incautious lover, hatred→inseparable love, the "rigid breast a little furnace of incensed love." This is the
  answer to every image of Polia's coldness (porphyry, marble, ice, wax-on-rock) — all melted into "a furnace." The
  frozen heart the whole book set out to thaw is now fire. Her conversion is complete.
- **Diana vs. Venus — the final vision.** The two goddesses appear in opposed chariots: **Diana in crystalline ice**
  (cold, empty quiver, vengeful) drawn by her stags in *leaden* chains; **Venus in fire** drawn by swans in *golden*
  cords, with Cupid. The whole book's conflict (chastity's cold vs. love's warmth; the leaden arrow vs. the golden,
  p. 411) is staged as a chariot-confrontation. Ice pursued by fire: Diana fleeing before Venus. The vision (its outcome
  on pp. 426–427) confirms which goddess claims Polia — and it is Venus.
- **the love-tokens sent.** Polia sends Poliphilo a chaplet woven of gold and *her own plucked hair* "in sign of perfect
  love" — the hair that was her pride, her family's hubris, and (torn) her grief, now given freely to the beloved. The
  motif resolves: beauty offered in love, not hoarded in cruelty.
- The confrontation of the goddesses and Diana's rebuke conclude on pp. 426–427.
