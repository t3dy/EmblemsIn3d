## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Arrival at the **round Temple of Venus Physizoa**, and the beginning of its
**geometric construction** — a circle inscribed in a square, height equal to diameter, the radius
divided 5 (+1), a second circle struck, and ten radial divisions carrying the peristyle and vaulting.
All secure; the geometry is a genuine architect's setting-out, of a piece with the Cythera plans.

- **The temple's generative geometry.** Colonna gives the setting-out as a real design problem: a
  domed rotunda whose height equals its diameter, laid inside a square, its radius stepped into
  sixths, a concentric ring struck for the ambulatory, and **ten radial divisions** organising piers,
  peristyle and vaults. This is the same "describe it so it can be built" method as the island-plans
  (pp. 311, 325) — the temple is a buildable rotunda, and the world could raise it to these rules.
- **Venus Physizoa — "life-giving Venus."** The temple is consecrated to Venus as the generative
  principle of nature (Greek *physizoos*, "life-producing"), not the erotic Venus of Cythera: the
  cosmic, fertile goddess. It stands on the shore at the end of the valley, between the pastoral
  Floralia (p. 196) and the sea.
- **Poliphilo's self-castigation** — the interior monologue that opens the page continues the
  fidelity-thread: he checks his own "inconsiderate desire," refusing to ask Polia for what he most
  wants, and rebukes his "shared, not wholly mine" heart. The pheasant-and-falcon simile (his beating
  heart) recurs from the pelican of p. 287.
- The architectural description of the temple (dome, columns, doors, ornament) fills the pages
  following, the way the theatre filled the end of ch. XXII.
