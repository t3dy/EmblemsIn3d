dissonant, but comparable — Lydian-wise sang. Not the plaints of raging Tragedy, nor in the cackling
Satire, nor in the deceitful Comedy, nor in the tearful Elegies; but with a most-adorned poem, and with
elegant little words, the supreme sweetnesses of the holy and fostering Erycina (Venus) they composedly
proffered, and the delectable fallacies of the assisting son (Cupid) they eloquently sang. And Polia,
affable and comely, of cultivated adornment, polished and elegant, congratulating — the thanksgivings of
the obtained graces (provoking to admiration) she sang, with such vehemence and sweet-sound as, of blind
Demodocus, at the hearing of the wise Ulysses, with wailing cithara, did not attain. Which most-pleasing
companion — feeling herself no less delighted, partaker — speaking jocosely, and blandishing, tempting
me: how did the instant things appear to me? Naming to me, of whatever rowing Nymph, the proper name; and
with sweet persuasion she affirmed to me that **perseverance alone wears the victor's diadem**. And in
this unbridled avidity wholly slipped down, and most-cast-forth, most-pleasantly we navigated, and
prosperous we arrived at the delicious island Cythera.

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The close of **chapter XX**: Poliphilo's meditation on the torment of deferral and
the "uncompacted appetite and power" of nature; then the boat skimming "like a water-skater" over the calm
sea while the nymphs sing in the **Iasian mode** and **Polia answers alone in the Lydian**, singing the
sweetnesses of Venus and Cupid; Polia's teasing question and her maxim that **"perseverance alone wears the
victor's diadem"**; and the arrival at **Cythera**. This ends chapter XX (7/7). All secure.

- **"Perseverance alone wears the victor's diadem."** The chapter's — and arguably the whole crossing's —
  moral, spoken by Polia in answer to Poliphilo's plea against delay (p. 288). The lover's reward is not
  seized but earned by constancy. A line worth surfacing in the world/novel as the motto of the Cythera
  approach; it is the affirmative counterpart to the triumph's QUIS EVADET / NEMO.
- **the modes: Iasian nymphs, Lydian Polia.** Colonna distinguishes the singers by Greek musical mode — the
  chorus in the Iasian (Ionian), Polia "alone… Lydian-wise." The Lydian was the mode of love-song and
  lament; Polia's solo is set apart from the chorus, as her voice is throughout. A concrete cue for the
  world's approach-music: a solo Lydian line over a choral Iasian ground.
- **surpassing Demodocus** — the blind bard of the *Odyssey* who made Odysseus weep; Polia's song exceeds
  his. The last of the chapter's music-surpassings (Orpheus p. 285, the Sirens p. 286, now Demodocus).
- **arrival at Cythera.** The boat "like a water-skater" reaches the island — the seam directly into chapter
  XXI (the island ekphrasis, already translated). Chapter XX is the crossing; XXI–XXIV the island. **All of
  the Cythera arc (XX–XXIV) is now complete.**
