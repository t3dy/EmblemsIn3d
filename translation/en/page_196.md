# Page 196 — Chapter XVII

*(Page 195 is a blank leaf — a full-page woodcut with no text — and is not counted.)*

Then, from here, both of us somewhat progressed, with inexcogitable solace, and proceeding further,
I beheld again, at the clear and fresh founts of the gracious and grassy meadows and shady groves,
the cascade-Napaeae dancing, and the pleasant Naiads playing, and the marine Nereids singing —
clad, some, in the skin of the sea-calf (not harmed by the wrath of supreme Jove), with great bark-
vessels of *estur* in hand stuffed with fruits and flowers, gathered from the gravelly shores, and
with diverse solaces idly making festival. Many were, in their hands, bearers of green Thyrses,
which at the summit were compacted of orange-flowers, with soft fronds, and of Egyptian roses,
yellow and Persian, and of Narcissus-flowers, with handfuls of crimson flowers, and odorous of
*amuletum*; with sylvan Pan the Arcadian, and the demigod sylvan hill-dwellers, and the Dryads, and
many others, and Zephyr with his beloved Chloris — to whom he had conceded the power over the
beautiful flowers — and many active and singing Shepherds, in their songs expert to contend, and
armed with their crooks, praised together, jubilating and applauding, with serious games and jests,
with most-ancient instruments of straw and of reed, and with trumpeting Tibiae of bark, of *scytala*
form, of a strange sound, the amorous and omnipotent Jove — festively glorifying — and the holy
country-life, and making most-devout the Floralian feasts.

For which thing, how much voluptuous pleasure I felt, I leave to judge to whoever in his cogitation
could feign it. Therefore, copiously stuffed with incredible joy, with my most-pleasing companion,
traversing, we continued our happy itinerary and amorous walk. Whence, some time, the eyes from
their sweet prison and binding — and almost proscription — somewhat removing, Lo! looking above the
tender and greenest tops of the wanton trees, I saw a lofty Pterygium (winged pinnacle), above,
appearing to me of a round fastigium (gable), esteeming it to be little distant from the whispering
shore, toward which she, facetious, was leading me. And in which the gliding and sloping little
rivers, that circled the valley, terminated; and from the feet of the most-ornate hills and little
mountains — part grassy, part wooded — ran, with most-swift water, in clearest little channels with
a grassy and sandy bed, one leaping down to a side. And then again, beyond the said Pinnacle, I saw a
superb and eminent cupola, appearing to me covered with livid lead. Which at the summit had a cyma in
octagonal form with columns, and above another cupola, and then eight quadrangular piers, covered by
a single top, in balustered figure, upon which stood forth a stylus with a most-round Triangle fixed,
especially splendent with golden brightness. Whence very pleasingly offered itself to me such a
showing, by which I was not —

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The turn, within **chapter XVII**, from the four triumphs to the temple: the water-nymphs (Napaeae, Naiads,
Nereids), Pan, the Dryads, and **Zephyr with Chloris** making a Floralian festival by the shore; then
Poliphilo first sees, above the trees, the **great lead-domed temple** with its octagonal lantern and
gold triangle-finial — the Temple of Venus Physizoa, which he reaches on p. 197. All secure.

- **The Temple of Venus.** Still within chapter XVII, after the four seasonal triumphs the narrative turns
  to the round temple of *Venus Physizoa* ("life-giving Venus"), a major architectural ekphrasis (its
  geometry begins on p. 197). This is the first great building of Book I's later half, and — like the
  Cythera theatre — a buildable structure described in the book's own dimensions.
- **the domed silhouette, from a distance.** Colonna gives the temple's profile as first glimpsed: a
  winged pinnacle, a round gable, a lead-covered cupola, an octagonal columned lantern, a second
  cupola, eight piers, and a gold triangle on a spire. A precise skyline for the world to raise.
- **Zephyr and Chloris** — the west wind and the flower-nymph he made Flora (Ovid, *Fasti* V); their
  presence makes the shore-festival a *Floralia*. The flower-and-fertility key of the whole book
  continues, now attending the approach to Venus's temple.
- **"the eyes from their sweet prison"** — Poliphilo's gaze, bound to Polia (the recurring image),
  lifts just long enough to see the temple: even sightseeing is a release from the captivity of love.
