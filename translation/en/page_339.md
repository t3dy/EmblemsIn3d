Salmacis. And in her left hand she held a sphere formed of golden plates, with its centre. And with

Newly, three other unpremeditating matrons presented themselves: **Teleste** (Initiatrix) the first,

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The core of the allegorical procession: **Phileda** gathers the flowers Ennia
scatters; **Velode** presents the quiver with the two arrows (**silver = love, leaden = aversion** —
Ovid's arrows of Cupid); **Omonia** and **Diapraxe** play with gold and crystal balls; **Typhlote**
(Blindness) offers the blindfold-veil; her wanton damsels **Asynecha** and **Ardelia** dance; and
**Aschemosyne** (Shamelessness) presents herself nude with a golden sphere. All secure; the names are
glossed from Greek and the attributes are standard.

- **The two arrows of Cupid.** Velode's quiver holds the Ovidian pair (*Metamorphoses* I): the golden/
  silver arrow that kindles love, and the leaden arrow, "cold and inauspicious," that repels it. Love
  wields both — desire and aversion are one god's work. A key emblem for the world's Cupid-figure.
- **the blindfold offered by Typhlote.** Here is the origin-moment of blind Cupid: Blindness herself
  hands him the veil to cover his eyes. Colonna stages the god's blindfolding as a rite of the triumph.
- **the personifications, read as a sequence** — Concord and Accomplishment play (the gold ball never
  meets the crystal: harmony kept by avoidance); then Blindness, Heedlessness, and Shamelessness attend
  — the darker aspects of erotic love, unflinchingly named. Colonna does not sanitise the triumph.
- **Aschemosyne's "Tribade insolence"… Hostius' mirrors** — a frank catalogue of shamelessness
  (Hostius Quadra, the notorious voyeur of Seneca who watched himself in magnifying mirrors). The
  passage is deliberately transgressive; rendered at its own level, plainly.
