re-acquire, with the greatest doloration she contristated herself. And, beyond this, to her ill, was a mortal accessory,
thinking of the other happy and content married-women, and, frequently coming to mind, imagining, of those that lay in
the sweetest embraces of their most-desired lovers, and of the amorous and delectable solaces, and of their consummated
appetites and desires — as she judged, the wanton nature stimulating, and the wicked and scelerate love. She, in herself
finally re-taken, and in ardent envy bursting, and, in the mind sedulously repeating the intolerable and tedious
superbity of the odious old man, and of the dolorous, unconsoled life having grown weary — she set to scratch and claw
the face, and the breast, with the little palms, savaging, to beat; every hope truncated, dissolved into inundating
tears. And the facile eyes, into weepings more bitter than Egeria's converted; no thing grateful, none appetible, every
thing untasteable, save the improbe death — and to desire the accelerated end that Iphis made. Whence, of here, was born
a rabid furor, of her very self a lovesick and most-cruel executioner concrete; she took, secretly, one black day (the
husband of this unaware), a cutting and knifed iron; and, like a conscious malefactress, impotent of her desires, and
every confidence broken, of herself a mortal enemy made — consenting to the furious conceit — she used a horrend and
frightful vengeance: and, crowned with funereal smilax and with fronds of *ostrya* (hornbeam), through the middle of the
sad heart, impiously (the infernal and mournful Furies invoked) — O unheard crime! — all transfixed the noxious iron.

"O wretched and afflicted me — if, in this my little age (which may the supernal (gods) preserve me from), such a
misfortune, as of thee, could befall, for some similar offence, I would die before the time, of grief, and, of sadness,
accelerating the supreme cloister of my life. O me — who would sufficiently avail, then, to this horrid, most-unhappy
and urgent tempest, and miserable demise of the recounted woman — and my calamity, in kind, in amplitude, in weight, and
asperity, ably, then, assimilating, to compare? What (encounters) of shades, of Lemures, of Manes, of Larvae; what
nocturnal apparitions; what of Demons frights, ever more noxious and horrible, could incur upon me — if these dolorous
eyes should see some sinister of thine, and damage? Then, Polia, my daughter, and my sweetest hope: attend, with the
soul erect and united, the inevitable ire of the Gods — either timely, or with tardiness, wont infallibly to make like
vengeance — such as, for her displeasingness, came to Castalia, from Apollo; and, by this same mode, the shapely Medusa —"

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The exemplum's end: the frustrated wife, grieving her **irrecoverable wasted youth** and envying
happy wives, claws herself and **kills herself with a knife** (crowned with funereal smilax, invoking the Furies); the
nurse says she would die of grief if the like befell Polia, and warns her to fear the gods' **inevitable vengeance** (as
struck Castalia, and Medusa). All secure; the cautionary tale complete.

- **The suicide — Book II's second love-death.** The woman kills herself not from love refused (Poliphilo) but from love
  *forced and unfulfilled* — the other extreme. She, like Polia, is "a conscious malefactress" (the exact phrase, pp. 397,
  399), self-slain. The two deaths frame Polia between the errors: too cold (Poliphilo's killer) and too late/too hot (this
  woman). The nurse's warning: choose measured love now, or die of one extreme or the other.
- **the grief for wasted time.** The deepest note: she grieves not the ugly husband but the "irrecoverable time, uselessly
  dispensed" — youth spent in refusal, unrecoverable at any price. This is the *carpe diem* argument's dark heart (love in
  season, for time cannot be re-bought — completed p. 416). Polia, still young, can still choose.
- **the nurse's love for Polia.** She says she would "die before her time" if such befell Polia — the maternal love (cf.
  p. 407) that grounds her counsel. Her warning is not cold doctrine but a mother's fear. The warmth that thaws Polia is,
  again, love received before love given.
- **Castalia and Medusa** — women punished for scorning suitors (Castalia fled Apollo into her spring; Medusa's cruelty
  turned her hair to snakes, p. 416): the myths of petrification/transformation for coldness, Polia's exact risk. The
  nurse's final application follows on p. 416.
