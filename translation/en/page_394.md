# Page 394 — Chapter XXVI

"— and a pleasant figment, already, dislocated, my soul would freely have departed — such as, at present, at that point
disposed, I feel it advancing. And by such a mode I prove the shattered heart somewhat to restore itself, and a little to
respire. Of a sudden, then, I see myself totally frustrated, and left inane and void of every subsidy and help. And so,
orbicularly, in the aforesaid agitations relapsing, pass and flee my days, this exasperated life dolorously living. O me,
often, with industrious and sagacious cogitation, I would wish to withdraw myself from so molesting a weight, and from
this urgent burden and pressing yoke, and from so sweet a pondering of thee, and from this deadly subjection to free
myself, attempting. Alas, then, more irate and more indignant, Cupid encapistrates (halters) me in evil errors, and,
against the attempted flight more vigilant, and more entangling me, enveloping, impedes me from fleeing. O most-beautiful
above all outstanding Nymphs — would it please the gods (that), henceforth, by thee, rather, odious death were given me,
than that, at present, in so exasperated a bitterness, thou shouldst not hear these my amorous and just petitions, with
affectionate entreaty, and prolix laments, produced by the subministering occasion, already, more than one (day),
within the cremated heart conceived and heaped up. Wherefore, Polia, most-worthy of veneration, beautiful thing and
eternal glory and preclear praise — for thy love, to die I persuade myself, and by the unfitting ferocity of Cupid —
whom I may juridically pardon, if, in this mania, reproaching him, I should curse his cruel and malefic power, which has
so strongly, to the tyrannic arbitrament of its urgent and fallacious laws, subjected and taken me, and, having captived
me in so strong a flame, retrograde-flying, in such a manner has despoiled and deserted me of every help, and of every
quiescence destituted. Then, in a moment, repenting, those imprecations and the curse I revoke, terrified, fearing, alas,
lest he, in me, more impiously provoke himself to anger, and then more fecund a pain to my heart, and doloration, ferment;
and lest he kindle the more my delight and ardent desire of thy conspicuous elegance and gracefulness; and, on the other
part, (fearing) thee intractable, and less pious, as at present I suspect. And when this, O me, I premeditate,
considering intrinsically the yesterday's impiety — without doubt, derived, it seems to me to be between the mouth (with
the attrition of sounding and foamy teeth) of the Calydonian Boar, and between horrid Python, and between the leonine
spear (jaw), which, my flesh lacerating, they devour. And it seems to me to hear, sadly, murmur the infernal souls, and
all the infernal furies, and the frightful **Proserpina**, serpent-tressed, and three-headed **Cerberus**, and internal
**Pluto**, and **Acheron**, the wretched Tartarean ferryman, at the tremendous *scaphidium* (skiff), to invite me to
navigate the Stygian waves of Lethe and Cocytus, to the trembling judgement of **Minos, Rhadamanthus, Aeacus, and Dis**.
But, beyond all these abominable things, one more pestiferous accessory, in the mind, offends me: that I fear to be, as
yesterday, today too, repudiated by thee. Alas — what —"

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The continuation of Poliphilo's lament: he lives "orbicularly" relapsing through the same
torments; he cannot flee Love (Cupid "halters" him and blocks escape); he half-curses Cupid, then fearfully revokes the
curse; and he sees himself already among the infernal powers (Proserpina, Cerberus, Pluto, the judges of the dead) — his
worst fear being not Hell but **Polia's repudiation**. All secure.

- **Love that forbids escape.** Poliphilo tries to "withdraw from the pressing yoke," but Cupid "halters him… impedes him
  from fleeing": the captivity-of-love (the recurring theme) as a prison he cannot break — the mirror of Polia's later
  captivity to Diana, and the very state the Venus-rite will release. Book II shows both lovers bound before both are freed.
- **the underworld foretasted.** His despair conjures the whole Hades (Proserpina, Cerberus, Pluto, the four judges) — the
  same infernal machinery as the Polyandrion's Hell-fresco (p. 249) — but he ranks *Polia's rejection* above all its
  horrors. The beloved's cruelty is worse than damnation: the Polyandrion's doctrine (love kills) enacted in real time.
- **the divided prayer** — cursing Cupid, then revoking the curse in terror: Poliphilo's oscillation captures the lover's
  helplessness, unable even to blame the god safely. His swoon-death is on p. 395.
- Polia records it all faithfully, building to the moment she watched him fall — the peak of the cruelty she confesses.
