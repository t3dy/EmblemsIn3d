## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Poliphilo's account of **falling in love at the temple**: at a festival in Diana's temple he sees
Polia among the virgins "like a Goddess among her Nymphs," and — "like wood once burned that re-kindles faster" — is
inflamed anew, his heart "a little furnace," resolving to "love her eternally"; he praises her sun-bright eyes and starry
face. All secure; his version of the coup de foudre.

- **His side of the first love.** Where Polia gave the first sight from her window (p. 386), Poliphilo gives it at the
  temple festival — his heart "ploughed by Cupid's arrows," his soul "vowed and offered" to her. The two accounts of the
  falling-in-love complement each other (as the book's frame promised, p. 381): her vanity-at-the-window and his
  worship-at-the-temple are the two faces of the one event.
- **wax and fire — the loving heart.** He is "more tender than wax to fire, disposed to receive the impressed images":
  Polia's beauty stamps itself on his soft heart. It is the exact inverse of Polia's *stony* heart (porphyry, marble):
  his heart is wax, hers stone. The two temperaments the whole book contrasts, here in his own image.
- **"a salutary refuge."** He calls Polia's presence "a salutary refuge" for his burning heart — the same word (*refuge*)
  Polia used of him (p. 433). The lovers each name the other their refuge: the symmetry of mutual love, retrospectively
  complete.
- **the beauty-ekphrasis.** His praise of Polia (sun-eyes, starry face, arched brows) resumes the beauty-inventory of
  Book I (the triumph, p. 337) — now motivated as the memory of first love. It continues on p. 439 (her teeth, breath,
  hair).
