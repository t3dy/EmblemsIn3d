## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Polia's conversion completes: after a last catalogue of love's victims (Smilax, Canens, Acis,
Helen) and a last recoil (her Diana-vow), the **flame surges irresistibly** ("as Nessus's venom through Hercules's body"),
Cupid strikes again, and she turns wholly to the dead Poliphilo — **resolving to go and revive him**, grieving "him, tenderly
loving me, I reputed an enemy," though still terrified of being Anaxarete. All secure; the turning-point achieved.

- **Love wins the inner battle.** Polia's mind swings between the new flame and her fears (the metamorphosis-victims, her
  Diana-vow) until Cupid's second stroke settles it: "with all my soul already suborned of amorous suggestions, to the
  swooned Poliphilo I returned." The conversion is dramatised as a genuine psychomachia — reason, fear, vow, and love
  contending — won at last by love. Colonna gives his heroine a fully interior conversion, not a mere plot-turn.
- **the resolve to revive him.** She resolves "to behold, dead, him whom I would not see alive" — the exact reversal of her
  crime (she watched him die, p. 395; now she seeks his body to restore it). The dead-raising miracle (the argument, p. 417)
  is set in motion by her converted love. Her grief — "him, tenderly loving me, I reputed a mortal foe" — is, finally, true
  remorse for the *man*, not just fear for herself (the completion begun at p. 408).
- **the Nessus simile.** Love spreads through her "as the Lernaean venom through Hercules's body" — love as a consuming
  poison, unstoppable once in the blood. The image (love as Nessus's shirt) is double-edged: her salvation comes as an
  affliction, sweet and burning at once (the book's sweet/bitter paradox, p. 279, now hers).
- **still Anaxarete.** Even converted, she fears she is Anaxarete (the stone-turned cruel maiden, pp. 396, 415) going to her
  victim. The dread of her own guilt persists into the reunion — which the miracle (his revival) will absolve. Chapter XXIX
  continues to the temple.
