"— irate and implacable, resumed his curved and dolorific bow, and, noxiously, through the middle of the superb breast,
wounded that sylvan and contumacious heart, with a pointed golden dart penetrating to the extreme line; and, the limit
being transgressed, and in her bosom, burning, nestling itself, the most-valid Love, with the blind fires, fervidly began
to re-kindle; and the wound so profound, perilous, atrocious, and various, that, drawn (healed) into a scar salubriously,
it could not (be). And, by the stimulating ardours amorously forced, and under the unwonted bite and bridle, and sedulous
pricking, impatiently vexed, she began, all languishing, to perish — desiring, then, the sweet petitions that the noble
adolescent vainly made — and, to his aspect, no more he appears (i.e. he was gone). And already Love, his convenient
violences licitly using, immodest, in her, beyond thinking, and urging, quickened. And, having made of her re-struck heart
a furnace-like fire — not so much the most-beautiful and elegant youth, but, by her ill misfortune, made venereous, of
whatever condition anyone had been (had she, of special grace, been able to have him), to her ardent and voluptuous desires
and itching concupiscences, without respect, she would have appreciated (him) most-opportune. I judge that, had an Egyptian,
or an Ethiopian, and an exploded (base) man offered himself, she would not have refused him to her demands — let alone (any)
of our patricians.

"Ultimately, the ingenuous Matron — excessively amorously languishing, and, in the acerbities of the reinforced flames,
exaggerated, and by the enticing ardours stimulated, and of itching appetites, and intemperate lasciviousness, incredibly
agitated, and by so much importunate libido irritated (as if in Didyma born) — and the intolerable pressure not sustaining,
in bed, most-sad, sick and infirm, she fell. Whence, like Antiochus, son of Seleucus, of his stepmother beyond measure
enamoured, and by an immortal languor invaded, (who) was detected, by the pulsing touch (of the pulse), by Erasistratus the
physician, to be languishing of love for the stepmother — by such a way, by the shrewd and expert physician clearly
understood that the woman lay languid, of measureless love maniacally suffering; consulting upon it, the stepfather and her
mother, that death might not supervene, found the opportune remedy: to marry her. So that it stood not long time, that there
was found for her a patrician man, of good condition of kindred, and rich — but old, and of precipitate age, and occiduous
senectitude, more than he (being shaven) showed; come almost to the doubtful age; and somewhat the cheeks were pendent, the
eyes ulcerated, the hands tremulous, the breath fetid, the head hiding (because there appeared the back of a scabby dog), and
the habit (of body), in the breast, all drooping; only, anxious, holding the soul to rapacious avarice most-devoted, and, to
the inexplicable Cupidity, supremely intent. And being come —"

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Cupid's revenge on the refusing girl: he strikes her at twenty-eight with the **golden arrow**, and
she burns with uncontrollable, indiscriminate lust (she would take even a base man) — falls sick of it (like Antiochus, whose
love-sickness Erasistratus diagnosed by his pulse); and her parents, to cure her, **marry her to a rich but repulsive old
man**. All secure; the exemplum's grim turn.

- **The two arrows enacted.** The girl who was cold (the leaden wound, or none) is now struck with the **golden arrow** and
  swings to the opposite extreme — indiscriminate lust. The nurse's exemplum dramatises Cupid's power to make cruelty into
  its opposite: the frozen become the incontinent. Both extremes (the Polyandrion's ice and fire, p. 250) are Love's
  punishment for refusing the *measured* love in due season. The lesson for Polia: love now, moderately, or be forced later,
  ruinously.
- **the Antiochus diagnosis.** The nurse cites the famous tale (Antiochus's secret love for his stepmother Stratonice,
  diagnosed by Erasistratus from his racing pulse — Plutarch, Appian): a learned medical exemplum for love-sickness, and the
  precedent for marrying the girl off "as a cure." Colonna's erudition serves the nurse's argument.
- **the repulsive old husband.** The grotesque bridegroom (pendent cheeks, ulcerated eyes, fetid breath, avaricious soul) is
  the antithesis of the "worthy peer" the girl refused: her punishment is to get, too late, the opposite of what she scorned.
  The grotesque portrait (continued p. 414) mirrors the executioners (p. 406) — Book II's ugliness is always the wage of
  refusing beauty's proper use.
- **married as a remedy** — the parents marry her to save her life, but to the worst possible match: the tale turns on the
  irony that late, forced love is a torment, not a cure. The disastrous wedding-night follows (p. 414).
