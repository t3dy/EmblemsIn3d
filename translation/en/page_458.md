# Page 458 — Chapter XXXV

And then, to the genetrix, too, thus he (Cupid) says: "Lady Mother of the warm loves, glorious nurse — this is the cause of
the ill and heart-grief, and of the pernicious exiling, and molesting Hyperoria (banishment), of this poor little and
wretched, exiled, and tormenting soul. But (in a) brief little instant, disconsolate soul, I shall make thee most-
efficaciously, of thy coveted (desire), satisfied and content, and to re-migrate unharmed where dislocated thou art. And I
wish to unite thee, and fittingly to couple thee, with thy cruel adversary (Polia); and to remove and break all the
obstacles repugning to my flying ingress." Sealed, then, the divine lips, of a sudden he re-assumed his candent, penetrable,
and barbed armatures, prependent from the most-holy flank, from the promptuary Quiver — manifestly seeing I, with the curved,
and, with rigour, corded bow — into the delicate breast of the shown image (Polia's), striking, he shot, of a golden arrow,
little-feathered of mordant thorns, and adorned of manifold colouring. Nor sooner, wounding, did that fulgurant arrow fix
itself (with a fermentous propagation of love), than the virgin girl — ductile, facile, mild, benign — gladly acclived
herself, bending; and, vanquished and prostrated, with Nymphal manner accusing herself (as those who, infirm and unarmed,
avail not to contrast) of the accustomed cruelty and ferine savagery.

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** In the throne-vision, **Cupid strikes Polia's image with the golden arrow** — "into the delicate
breast of the shown image, striking, he shot" — and she instantly **yields**, "ductile, mild, benign," self-accusing of her
cruelty; and Poliphilo beholds "three presences — two divine" (Venus, Cupid) and Polia. All secure; the vision's enactment
of Polia's conversion.

- **The conversion seen from heaven.** Poliphilo, at Venus's throne, *watches Cupid strike Polia* with the golden arrow —
  and she yields at once. This is the divine, seen-from-above view of the very conversion Polia narrated from within
  (p. 419: the flame kindling in her heart). The two accounts of her turning — her inward experience and his heavenly
  vision — converge: Cupid's arrow, which she felt, Poliphilo saw fired. The double narration gives the miracle its inner
  and its cosmic face.
- **the golden arrow, "feathered with mordant thorns."** The love-arrow (gold, the arrow of desire, p. 411) fletched with
  thorns and many colours — love that is sweet and stinging at once (the book's sweet/bitter paradox). It "propagates love
  with fermentation" through Polia's breast: her stone heart leavened into love. The exact instrument of her thaw, shown.
- **"the shown image."** Cupid shoots Polia's *effigy* (shown to Poliphilo, p. 457) — a magical correspondence: striking
  the image strikes the woman (she yields at that instant). The vision's magic mirrors the plot's timing (Cupid struck
  Polia, p. 419, as Poliphilo lay dying). Love-magic by image, the book's hieroglyphic-emblematic logic applied to the
  heart.
- **the three presences.** Venus, Cupid, and Polia (near-celestial): the triad Poliphilo's soul beholds. Polia is placed
  almost among the gods — the beloved deified (his idolatry, p. 440, confirmed at the throne). Her divine beauty is
  described on p. 459.
