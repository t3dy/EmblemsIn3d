"No otherwise glad and gracious to him, and pleasing rendering myself, than Atalanta to Hippomenes, and the pleasant

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Polia completes her declaration — Cupid's power penetrates all (no armour resists him), so she
dares not refuse; and, moved by Poliphilo's grief, she "shattered her heart through the middle" for love, welcoming him
("my joy, my hope, my Refuge, my most-ardent love") and promising his torments will vanish "like fog in the wind" — and
**gives him the kiss, the *arra*** (pledge) of her love. All secure; the seal of the reunion.

- **The kiss-pledge — the union sealed.** Polia gives Poliphilo "this amorous kiss, for a pledge (*arra*) of my inflamed
  heart" — the same legal love-pledge Book I sealed (the *arra*-kiss, p. 218). Book II now shows its true origin, from
  her side: the kiss that betroths the lovers, given freely by the once-cruel Polia. The two accounts of the betrothal
  kiss meet exactly.
- **love's total reversal, in her endearments.** "My joy, my gladness, my solace, my hope, my Refuge, my most-ardent
  love" — the litany of love-names lavished on the man she once dragged out by the feet (p. 397). The "return, alacritous
  and jocund" undoes the death she caused: she summons him back to life and joy. Her promise that his torments will
  "vanish like fog in the wind" answers his years of suffering (chs. XXVI).
- **Cupid's universal dominion, restated.** Polia's argument (no cuirass, no shield resists Love's arrow) echoes the
  nurse's (p. 411) and the boat-standard's *Amor vincit omnia* (p. 284): Love conquers all, so she yields. The book's
  motto, now in her own mouth as the reason for her surrender.
- The kiss, the priestess's response, and Poliphilo's turn to narrate follow (pp. 435–436).
