# Page 457 — Chapter XXXV

Having benignly heard my lamentable complaints, to herself, immediately, the glorious Goddess and sublime Lady called the
flying offspring (Cupid), demanding of him what had been the cause of so much injury. He, then, smiling and enticing, thus
took to say: "Amorous Mother — it will not be a protraction of time, that becomingly co-adapted shall be the present
litigation and discordant souls, with reciprocal vicissitude of equability." Nor had he sooner proffered, charmingly, these
little words, than the mellifluous (one) turned himself to me, saying:

"Behold diligently this noteworthy image. How many would there be, who — however great — most-content would repute
themselves, esteeming themselves blessed, beatific, and best, only to gaze on her, let alone to be loved by her — that such
a virgin Thalasio (a bride) had not, by lot, in the rape of the Sabines" (showing me that true and divine effigy of Polia).
"And attend, and with wondrous affect appreciate: these particular gifts, given by the Gods most-precious, ought not to be
spurned — because, however much we are accustomed to concede (them) to the earth-born, nonetheless, many would wish them, and
cannot consequute them. Which (gift), graciously most-precious, now I give thee; and the first-fruits of so glorious a
congeries of virtue and corporeal beauties, which I, gracious, offer thee."

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The vision's resolution: Venus calls **Cupid** and asks the cause of Poliphilo's injury; Cupid,
smiling, promises the lovers will soon be reconciled "with reciprocal equability," and then, turning to Poliphilo, **shows
him Polia's "true and divine effigy" and gives her to him** — "these gifts of the Gods ought not to be spurned… the first-
fruits of so glorious a beauty I offer thee." All secure; the divine grant of Polia.

- **Cupid reconciles the lovers, by Venus's command.** The god who wounded Poliphilo (and hardened Polia) now promises to
  make their love *equal* ("reciprocal vicissitude of equability") — the very equalising Poliphilo asked the priestess for
  (p. 430). At Venus's throne, the reconciliation is decreed: Cupid will strike Polia (which, in the plot's time, he
  already has, p. 419). The near-death vision is where the union is *authorised by heaven* — the divine ground of the
  earthly reunion (chs. XXIX–XXXI).
- **Polia given as a gift.** Cupid shows Poliphilo "the true and divine effigy of Polia" and *gives* her to him — "these
  gifts of the Gods… the first-fruits of so glorious a beauty I offer thee." Polia is a divine gift, not a conquest: the
  union is grace, granted by Love himself. This reframes the whole courtship — Poliphilo did not *win* Polia by his
  letters or pleas (which failed), but *received* her from the god. The theology of the book: love is given, not earned.
- **"Thalasio… the rape of the Sabines."** Cupid says no Sabine bride was as precious as Polia (Thalasius, the cry at the
  Roman wedding, from the Sabine women): the surpassing-topos, on brides. Polia exceeds the founding brides of Rome.
- **the vision's function.** Poliphilo's near-death experience *reveals* the divine decision behind the earthly plot: the
  gods have granted the union. When he revives (in Polia's arms, already converted), he knows the reunion is heaven's will.
  The two lovers' stories, and the two temples' rites, all rest on this throne-scene. Book II's theological centre.
