# Page 423 — Chapter XXIX

Whence, no sooner had he completely re-assumed and refreshed the pristine virtues — in my bosom and in my arms — and
somewhat re-purpled the cheeks, than the Pontiff (Priestess) of the Sacred Temple, with a tumultuary throng of the
obsequent little priestesses and ministers of the holy service (perhaps, my anguishings and tearful lamentations, and the
high and improbe sighs, in the thundering Temple, having heard), came there toward us; and, animadverting (arrived) of the
illicit operations, interdicted in that holy and unpolluted place, gravely offended, with her other ministers, of ire
extumescent — some with rods, and others with branches of young-oak, at us reproving, and gravely threatening, and
beating — dissociated our sweet embracing, perturbing it.

For which thing, then, immodestly I doubted lest to me should befall that which, to the terrific Medusa, the irascent
furor of Minerva befell — when she, in her clean Temple, Neptune amorously knew (lay with). And that which likewise
befell Hippomenes, and the avaricious and swift Atalanta — who, for illicit conjoining, converted themselves into Lions.
And, again, the fury of the Proetides, by Juno. And scarce, out of their hands — unless with laborious greatness we had
fled — and —

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The reunion is broken up: **Diana's priestess and nymphs**, hearing the commotion and finding the
lovers embracing in the temple, **drive them apart with rods and oak-branches**, "gravely offended" at the "illicit"
love in the chaste sanctuary; Polia fears being transformed (like Medusa, or Atalanta and Hippomenes turned to lions) for
her temple-desecration. All secure.

- **Diana expels the lovers.** The chastity-cult violently rejects the new love — beating and driving out the pair for
  their embrace in Diana's temple. It dramatises the book's central conflict (Diana vs. Venus) as physical expulsion:
  Polia, having chosen Venus's love, is cast out of Diana's house. The two goddesses cannot share her. This is the
  necessary break — she must leave Diana to belong to Venus (the "separation from the colds of Diana," p. 229, enacted).
- **the transformation-fear, once more.** Polia fears becoming a lion (Atalanta and Hippomenes, who profaned Cybele's
  temple with love) or a gorgon (Medusa, who lay with Neptune in Minerva's temple): the punishment for love in a chaste
  sanctuary. Her whole story has been shadowed by the fear of metamorphosis (her family, p. 384; the visions); here it
  peaks as she is caught in the very act the myths punish. The final vision (pp. 425+) will resolve whether Venus or Diana
  prevails.
- **the irony.** Polia is now punished by Diana for *loving* — the exact reverse of her earlier cruelty (punished, in
  the visions, for *not* loving). Whichever she chooses, a goddess is offended; only Venus's grace (the coming vision, and
  Book I's temple-rite) reconciles it. She is caught between the two, as the crossing was "between two lords" (p. 279).
- The beating, expulsion, and the lovers' parting complete on p. 424.
