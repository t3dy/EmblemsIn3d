## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Polia's lament peaks (she calls the Furies upon herself, names herself "bitch, perfid, barbarous"
for killing the man who loved her), and then — weeping over him like **Argia over the dead Polynices** — she lays her hand
on his cold breast and **feels a faint pulse re-boil**; embracing him closer, his "fugitive spirits" re-warm. All secure;
the revival begins.

- **The revival by love and tears.** Polia's weeping embrace does what the Furies and the visions could not undo — it
  *revives the dead*. Her hand on his cold breast feels the "deafened pulse re-boil"; her closer embrace re-warms his
  spirits. Love and remorse, poured out over the corpse, raise him. It is the miracle that answers her crime (she watched
  him die, p. 395; now she raises him), and the emotional counterpart to the rose-bush and communion of Book I (the dead
  love made living). The converted beloved is herself the reviving power.
- **Argia over Polynices.** She weeps like Argia (who braved death to mourn and bury her husband Polynices — Statius):
  faithful love over a corpse, the exemplum of devotion reaching the dead. Colonna's chosen parallel for the revival.
- **the reversal completed in the body.** Where she once made him "cold as marble" (p. 420) by her frigidity, her warmth
  now literally re-warms him. The stone/cold imagery that ran through her cruelty (porphyry, marble, ice) is undone by
  the heat of her converted love. The two temperatures of the whole book — Diana's cold, Venus's warmth — meet and resolve
  in her embrace.
