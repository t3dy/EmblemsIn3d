## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The **end of the first letter** (Poliphilo lays out the two fates — her mercy = his triumph, her
refusal = his ruin — and offers "the oblate and immolated soul, both alive and dead thine I am. Farewell"); then **Polia,
unmoved** ("to a marble-born statue I spoke, my words like wind-eggs"), and Poliphilo's resolve to write a second ("the
first blow does not split the tree"). All secure; the letter's close and its failure.

- **"Both alive and dead, thine I am."** The letter's sign-off — total self-surrender, prophetic of his literal death and
  revival (chs. XXVI, XXIX). He offers Polia "the immolated soul, the slaughtered heart" to dispose of at will; she will,
  in fact, slaughter them (his death) and then dispose them back to life (his revival). The letter foretells the plot.
- **the marble statue.** Polia receives the letter unmoved — "to a marble-born statue I spoke, my words like unfertile
  wind-eggs." Her stony heart (porphyry, p. 389) is proof against rhetoric: the letter cannot melt what only grace will.
  Colonna's point — persuasion fails where the heart is stone; the visions and Cupid's arrow (not the letters) convert her.
- **"the first blow does not split the tree."** Poliphilo's perseverance-maxim (cf. the "round ball needs an impulse,"
  p. 443): one letter failing, he writes again — the constancy the whole chapter praises. His love keeps writing into
  silence. The second letter follows.
- **the two fates offered.** He frames her choice starkly — mercy (his triumph) or refusal (his death and her vain
  repentance). She chooses refusal, and *does* vainly repent (chs. XXVII–XXIX): the letter's warning comes true. The
  double narration makes his prophecy the plot's spine.
