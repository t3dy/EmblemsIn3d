*(Chapter argument:)*

> POLIA RECOUNTS BY WHAT MODE THE SAGACIOUS NURSE, BY VARIOUS EXAMPLES AND PARADIGMS, ADMONISHED HER TO AVOID (THE
> GODS') IRE, AND TO EVADE THE THREATS OF THE GODS; AND HOW A WOMAN, DESPERATE FOR INTEMPERATE LOVE, KILLED HERSELF.
> COUNSELLING (POLIA), WITHOUT TARRYING, TO GO TO THE PRIESTESS OF THE HOLY TEMPLE OF THE LADY VENUS, (AS TO) WHAT SHE
> OUGHT, UPON THIS, TO DO — (WHO) WILL BENIGNLY LEND HER FIT AND EFFICACIOUS INSTRUCTION.

Not without supreme fatigue and expended labour, divine and outstanding Nymphs, can one draw a disposed and inclined
soul back from a purpose — most of all (a purpose) made difficult by obstinacy, and by time habituated, and the more
while, from it, one takes some delightful diversion and recompense. And to wish to adapt it to the contrary, and revert
it wholly alienated (from the former), by a fallacious estimation, most-most-difficult it shows itself. And, marvelling
at this, one is not (moved) in the least, if the sense, some time depraved, distorted, and corrupted, is wont to make
things of sweet matter appear ungrateful, unsweet, spurnable, and bitter. And less marvellous it offers itself, most-
outstanding Nymphs, if whiteness, to the sick, impure, and blear eyes, appearing blackish, offends; if things rutilant,
of a livid coating, and the (things) glittering of candour and of splendour shining, spotted, sprinkled with rust,
covered with caligo, suffused with cuttlefish-vomit, and infuscated with atrament (ink), are damned — without doubt, not
by defect of the object, but by the sensual disease. So, and not otherwise, I: since, to the colds of chaste Diana, my
soul and my mind being firmly habituated and professed, and religated and proscribed — grave, then, and much difficult
rendered itself the ingress of ardent Love, acceptable.

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The opening of **chapter XXVIII** (the nurse's counsel): its argument (the nurse admonishes Polia
by many examples to fear the gods' wrath, tells of a woman who killed herself for love, and counsels her to consult the
priestess of Venus's temple); and Polia's reflection that a soul long "habituated to the colds of Diana" finds Love's
ingress "grave and difficult" — her chastity a "sensual disease" that makes sweet things (love) seem bitter. All secure.

- **The turn of the whole book: Diana → Venus.** Polia diagnoses her own cruelty as a *disease of perception* — like
  sick eyes to which whiteness looks black, her Diana-habituated soul finds Love "bitter" and feels "nausea" at it. Her
  conversion (chs. XXVIII–XXIX) is the *curing* of that sense, the reversal from Diana to Venus that Book I's temple-rite
  completed (she who was "separated from the colds of Diana," p. 229). Here is that separation's beginning, from her side.
- **the nurse as the agent.** The "sagacious and versute Nurse," seeing Polia's "hard, massed" frozen heart, sets out to
  thaw it by counsel — the persuasion that fills this chapter (Cupid's power over gods, the fate of the cruel). She is
  the physician of Polia's diseased sense (as the argument says, she is "the true remedy," p. 408), and will send her to
  Venus's priestess for the cure proper.
- **the argument previews a suicide.** "A woman desperate for intemperate love killed herself" — the nurse will tell a
  cautionary tale (the other extreme, love's excess) to balance the warning against love's refusal. The Polyandrion's
  two damnations (excess and coldness, p. 250) structure the nurse's counsel too.
- The nurse's examples (Cupid's conquests, the punished cruel) fill pp. 410–411.
