# Page 3 — Front matter

*(The end of Crasso's letter; then Giovanni Battista Scita's poem to him; then the beginning of
the anonymous elegy to the reader, which runs over onto page 4. Latin.)*

…Farewell, and number your Crassi, with me, among your own.

---

**A poem of Giovanni Battista Scita to the most distinguished Leonardo Crasso, learned in the
liberal arts and in pontifical law.**

> This wonderful and new little book,
> to be set level with the books of our ancient forebears,
> in which whatever in the whole world life offers
> that is rare and noble is borne and borne again —
> it brings you, Crasso, as much thanks
> as it brought to Poliphilo its parent.
> Poliphilo gave it life; you too
> have given it life, and more: you drive off its death.
> For when it lay buried in neglect,
> already dreading Lethe close at hand,
> you give it to all the nations to be read;
> nor do you spare your money or your labour,
> but, better than its parent, you have lifted
> the cast-out child into your lap.
> They say Lyaeus was once twice-born.
> So has it now befallen this book: and Poliphilo
> gives it back a father, and Crasso a Jove.

---

**An anonymous elegy to the reader.**

> Candid reader, listen to Poliphilo
>    telling his dreams — dreams sent from the highest Pole.
> You will not waste your labour, nor will it irk you to have heard these things:
>    the work abounds in wonders, with matters so various.
> If, grave and stern, you despise the erotic, then learn, I beg,
>    the sequence of its matters, so well disposed.
> Do you refuse? Then at least the style, the new tongue and the new
>    grave speech: wisdom asks that you look at her.
> If you decline that too, then look at the geometry; learn the many
>    ancient things crowded with the marks of the Nile.
> Here are pyramids, baths, and enormous colossi,
>    and the ancient form of obelisks lies open.
> Here shines a diversity of bases and columns in their variety,
>    and their arches, friezes, architraves,
> and capitals and beams, and cornices with the quadrant —

*(the elegy continues on page 4)*

---

## Notes

**Confidence: high.** Verse is rendered line for line and plainly, without rhyme or metre:
forcing either would put words in the poet's mouth, which is the one thing this edition may not
do.

- **What Scita's poem is actually saying**, and it is the sharpest thing in the front matter:
  **the book was already dying.** It "lay buried in neglect, already dreading Lethe close at
  hand". Whoever wrote the *Hypnerotomachia* wrote it and then let it rot; Crasso found it and
  paid to print it. If that is true rather than flattery, the most celebrated illustrated book of
  the Renaissance survives by an accident of patronage.
- **"They say Lyaeus was once twice-born."** Lyaeus is Bacchus, born first of Semele and a second
  time from Jupiter's thigh after her death — so the book has two fathers: **Poliphilo the parent
  who begot it, and Crasso the Jove who carried it to term.** Semele's death by looking on Jupiter
  unveiled is itself one of the four triumphs Poliphilo will watch later in the book, which makes
  the compliment neater than it first appears.
- **The elegy is a table of contents in verse**, and a wonderfully cynical one. It works down a
  ladder of readers: if you will not read it for love, read it for the sequence; if not for the
  sequence, for the style; if not for the style, *look at the pictures* — the geometry, the
  hieroglyphs, the orders of columns. It is the earliest evidence of how this book was expected
  to be used, and it says: skim it, if you must, but do not put it down.
- **"The marks of the Nile"** — *niliacis notis*, the Egyptian hieroglyphs, which Poliphilo reads
  on the elephant's base, the Great Portal's piers and the bridge. See `ORNAMENT.md` and
  `hp.db.hieroglyph_vocabulary`.
- **"With the quadrant"** — *cum quadrante*, an architectural term for the fillet or quarter-round
  member of a cornice. The elegy is naming real mouldings, not gesturing at them; the list
  continues into page 4 with *symmetria*.
- **Giovanni Battista Scita** of Verona was a friend of Crasso's. His poem and the anonymous
  elegy were both printed here rather than composed for the author, whom nobody names.
