# Page 443 — Chapter XXXII

"— glorious death, miserably living, she acquire not.' Alas me — suddenly, in me the reason reciprocated (returning), all
these absurd curses, imprecated against my Polia, into me I re-torqued (turned back): 'Hey, Poliphilo — against thy good,
adverse thy soul, against thy heart, and adverse thy hope, art thou daringly, temerariously, to blaspheme? And that

amorous rage that of furor inflamed me, and that so demented me, praying the Gods, afterward, for her, all the contrary,
and all into benediction re-voking.

"Now, no more appreciating dying than thus living, I disposed myself to find a quite able and honest device, to give her
notice henceforth of my molesting and insupportable languors, and to confer to her my eternal conceit — thinking rightly
that there is no thing so hard, concreted in the human heart, that, with the little fire of love, softens not, is
conquered, and tamed; and (that) the round Ball, apt to roll, stable persists — but who gives it the impulse, knows the
office of its circled form. By such argument I cogitated to write to her, and to tempt (test) what might be the soul of so
noble and ingenuous a Nymph — a marvellous composite of every virtue and outstandingness, but, to me, a diuturnal contest,
and turbid sedition, assiduous anxieties, and continual grief, a familiar death without privation (deprivation), by the
privation of a thing so elegant, desirable, and beloved. And, of this, such an opinion persuaded me not, that in her other
should be found —"

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The end of **chapter XXXII**: Poliphilo, reason returning, **repents his curse** ("against thy own
good, thy soul, thy hope, dost thou blaspheme?") and revokes it into blessing; then, "no more appreciating death than
life," he resolves to **write to Polia** — trusting no heart is so hard that "love's little fire cannot soften it." All
secure; the decision to write the letter.

- **Curse revoked — love over spite.** Poliphilo turns his imprecation (p. 442) back on himself and "revokes all into
  benediction": his love masters his bitterness. He will not truly wish Polia harm — the constancy of his love holds even
  against her cruelty. This is the perseverance the chapter praises (p. 436), now moral: he blesses where he could curse.
- **"no heart so hard that love's fire cannot soften it."** Poliphilo's hopeful maxim — the very thing Book II proves,
  Polia's stony heart finally thawed (p. 419). His faith that the letter can move even her hard heart is vindicated by the
  whole plot. The "round ball that rolls once given the impulse" is his image: her heart needs only the right push
  (his letter, then Cupid's arrow) to yield.
- **the letter as the "device."** Unable to open his breast (the Socratic window, p. 440), Poliphilo chooses *writing* as
  the way to "give her notice of his languors" — the epistle that the argument named (p. 436). Chapter XXXIII is the letter
  itself, a set-piece courtly love-letter.
- **Herostratus** — who burned the temple of Diana at Ephesus for fame: Poliphilo fears his curse "invaded the sacrary of
  virtue (Polia)" like that arson. The learned self-reproach. Even his repentance is figured antiquarian.
