Lo, that she, the calm Polia, with outstanding gestures and modest movements, and with celestial semblance, and
festive face, and with paternal customs, with adorned beauty — worthy and illustrious of everlasting sight, and
venerable beholding, of wit, form, (and) modesty egregiously endowed — composedly, without delay and any tarrying,
rose from the pleasant seat under the frondy little canopy; and, with reverent leave and inclination, duly venerating,
and without any motion, with singular reverence, devout, she stood kneeling, with the face suffused with vermilion,
more than the ruddying Claudian apples.

For which thing I — of such cause and act ignorant and unaware, because the eyes, unceasingly to contemplate her
immense beauties retained and sedulously occupied, I knew not, revoking, to dislocate, nor to remove them, and thence
fittingly to decline them — I did the like (suddenly to her accosted): I genuflected on the ground.

And lo, that (I not adverting) opposite us, the divine **Cupid** — with naked and uncovered body, comely and pretty

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The reunion and the arrival of Cupid: Polia reassures Poliphilo (kisses, "soon I await the God
of love"; **"suffering is most often the cause of most-noble effects"**), and he recovers; then Polia suddenly
kneels, and **Cupid himself arrives by boat** — naked, unveiled-eyed — steering to the shore. This ends the long
chapter XIX and leads directly into the crossing (chapter XX). All secure.

- **"Suffering is the cause of most-noble effects."** Polia's consolation-maxim — the sister of "perseverance alone
  wears the victor's diadem" (p. 289): love's reward is earned through patient endurance. It resolves the whole
  temptation-and-terror arc of chapters XVIII–XIX (Poliphilo's restraint, p. 241; his panic, p. 272) into a single
  lesson. Prime, quotable material for the world/novel — the moral of the Polyandrion passage.
- **Cupid arrives — the chapter joins the crossing.** The god comes by boat to ferry the lovers to Cythera; his
  arrival here is the seam into **chapter XX** (already translated — the crossing, the *Amor vincit omnia* standard,
  the singing at the oars). The whole of Book I's later half now connects end to end: Temple of Venus → Polyandrion →
  Cupid's boat → Cythera. Poliphilo's "unveiled eyes" of Cupid recur (cf. p. 285).
- **the graveyard-therapy resolved.** Polia sent Poliphilo to the tombs to cool his desire (p. 242); it overshot into
  terror (p. 272); now, reunited and instructed, he recovers into "generous magnanimity." The Polyandrion has done its
  work — desire tempered, fidelity proven, patience learned — just as Cupid arrives to reward it.
- **The Polyandrion visit ends here.** The great sequence of tombs is done; the remaining pages of chapter XIX
  (275–282) cover the **boarding of Cupid's boat and the start of the crossing**, before chapter XX (p. 283) opens with
  the nymphs resting the oars to sing.
