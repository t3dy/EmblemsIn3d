> "To the shades. — *Traveller, bring your eyes closer here; then make words with me.* — 'I lived without love;
> loving, alas, I die.' — *Say, I pray, I beg: my [body] I gave to this Polyandrion, dead by the fire of love for a
> boy in the flower of his age, most beautiful.* — 'Why art thou mad? Yet thou wast living, in love.' — *Truly: when,
> a young woman, I had begun (to my) harm to love, the gift of love spurned, he refused; I wasted away, snatched by
> death, here I am.* — 'What can I do for thee?' — *Nevia the Roman, wretchedly dead of love for cruel Proculus,
> through the whole city and world proclaim. This is enough. Farewell.'*"

Leaving this, which with the utmost gladness I saw, I entered into a **tribune, broken and disrupted**. Where, again,
part of an egregious painting of most-lively mosaic I found. In which I saw a **Matron, prostrate upon a kindled pyre,
and raging upon her very self, killing herself**. And there, no other than feet, womanly shod, appeared — some part
with the calves, and little covered with drapes. All the rest was, by the unsated and voracious time, consumed, and by
antiquity, and by winds, and rains, and ardent Sun destroyed. In this same place the Ark (tomb) was comminuted, and the

## Notes

**Confidence: high** (the dialogue-epitaph is secure and famous). The chapter's finest inscription: the **dialogue-
epitaph of Nevia the Roman** — a spoken exchange between the passer-by and the dead woman, who "lived without love, and
loving, dies," having wasted away for love of the cruel, refusing **Proculus**, and asks only that her story be told
"through the whole city and world." Then a **mosaic of a matron self-immolating on a pyre**, and a broken tomb-vase.
All secure.

- **Nevia's dialogue-epitaph — the Polyandrion's masterpiece.** A miniature drama in stone: *Viator*, the passer-by,
  is drawn to "make words" with the dead; Nevia answers that she died loving where she was refused (Proculus "spurned
  the gift of love, refused"), and begs only remembrance — "proclaim it through the whole city and world." It is the
  opposite case to Laodia (the frigid, p. 252): Nevia *loved* and was refused, and died of it (one of the fire-damned,
  by the fresco's logic, p. 250). The two epitaphs frame the Polyandrion's two deaths. This dialogue is prime material
  to voice in the world/novel (the traveller and the tomb speaking) — the epitaph literally asks to be read aloud and
  spread.
- **"cruel Proculus."** Nevia curses her refuser by name — the Polyandrion's imprecation (p. 237, "thus perish whoever
  caused his lover's death") given a face. The tomb is a tribunal against the cold beloved.
- **the self-immolating matron.** A second Hell/death image (after the fire-ice fresco): a woman killing herself on a
  burning pyre — the fire-death of excessive love made literal. Buildable as a tribune-mosaic for the world's polyandrion.
- **the dialogue form** — Colonna casts the epitaph as call-and-response (traveller / dead), the classic *sepulchral
  dialogue* of Greek and Roman epigram, here fully dramatised. One of the passages the project's "beautiful and
  appropriate" quotation-standard is made for.
