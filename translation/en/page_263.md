# Page 263 — Chapter XIX

> **O LECTOR INFOELIX** — *(the epitaph of Lopidia and Chrysanthes)*
>
> "O unhappy reader, be present at this monument while it calls thee — and then, afterward, it asks that thou read
> into what human pleasure falls. Here is the ash of two lovers, who, long since, mutually burning insolently with
> itching love, by the wicked, unbridled impulse of pleasure, met in a deserted place among ruined rocks, where by
> chance also stood the broken walls of sacred buildings and rough ruins. There we both were ardently urged to pay
> Venus the desired gifts. Lying back, I, Lopidia, saw a snake, slipped down from on high, threatening. 'Ho! cease
> from what thou hast begun,' I say; 'my Chrysanthes, rise, flee — lo, a serpent about to devour us!' — as I watch it
> hurling itself from the wall. Soon he, terrified, looking up: 'O my Lopidia,' he says, 'I beg thee, go thy way, flee
> thou the road, let me attack the dying dragon.' Scarcely had I risen — alas, sad, wretched me! — when I saw my
> Chrysanthes, my life, entangled to destruction and tightly circled in the snake's coil, now anxiously breathing.
> And suddenly it wounds the throat of my Chrysanthes with biting teeth; then I behold my Chrysanthes being
> suffocated. 'Ah, ah, I perish, unhappy! I feel my Chrysanthes dying!' At once, furious, I rush at the serpent, and,
> a club seized, I hasten to strike; but the serpent twists its wrangling neck, nor by force could I drive off the
> entwined one. Striking at last, incautiously missing, I killed my Chrysanthes, most unhappy. Alas, I am undone!
> What have I done? What shall I do? Shall the serpent and I, so wretched, survive? By no means! But with Herculean
> daring — nay, with a ghostly, snarling fury — with that same club, the blow turned, upon the fallen corpse I strike
> the coiled beast, and kill it. What then, a girl, was I to do — lost and dead? My Chrysanthes and the beast,
> witnesses of my crime, thrown over my shoulders, I carry into the city, watering them again and again with sighs,
> heart-grief and tears. I climb a certain platform in the public forum, and, sighing, I make the matter known. In a
> crowd the citizens run together to the cruel and hateful spectacle; they pity the entangled chance, accuse Fortune,
> damn Venus. I attest my crime, I invoke the infernal powers: 'Therefore,' saying, 'receive me to pay the penalty
> together with my Chrysanthes; now let me transfer all the guilt upon myself.' Then, desperate, in the public sight
> of all, a sword seized, I transfix my breast, and gave my most-wretched self to be buried here, in an eternal tomb,
> with his corpse. Farewell."

---

## Notes

**Confidence: high** (the epitaph is a continuous, secure Latin narrative). The harrowing epitaph of **Lopidia and
Chrysanthes**: two lovers meeting secretly among the ruins to "pay Venus the desired gifts" are surprised by a
serpent; it strangles Chrysanthes, and Lopidia, striking at it with a club, **accidentally kills her own lover**;
she then kills the beast, carries both bodies to the forum, publicly confesses, and stabs herself to be buried with
him. All secure; a small, complete tragedy.

- **The accidental killing — the darkest of the epitaphs.** Where the others die of refusal, jealousy, or fortune,
  Lopidia dies of *guilt*: she killed the man she loved by her own hand, trying to save him. Her public confession
  ("I attest my crime… receive me to pay the penalty with my Chrysanthes"), her transfer of all guilt onto herself,
  and her suicide "in the public sight of all" make it the Polyandrion's most agonised story. A powerful, voiceable
  monologue for the world/novel.
- **the moral frame.** The epitaph opens by asking the reader to "read into what human pleasure falls" — a *memento*
  that illicit pleasure (they met to consummate love secretly in the ruins) brought the catastrophe. The citizens
  "damn Venus": the tomb warns against love's unbridled impulse (cf. the door-mottoes' balance, and the fire-ice
  Hell, p. 250). The Polyandrion moralises even its most sympathetic dead.
- **the serpent among the ruins** — the snake striking from the broken wall while the lovers embrace is a vivid,
  emblematic image (Eden's serpent in a Venus-garden): buildable as a tomb-relief or a novel-scene.
- **direct first-person voice** — like Nevia (p. 256) and Leontia (p. 258), Lopidia speaks in her own voice from the
  grave; the epitaphs are dramatic monologues, not just records. The world/novel can stage them as spoken.
