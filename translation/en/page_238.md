## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The close of Polia's account of the Polyandrion's rites — the cypress-branches hung and
burned at the next year's festival; the priest **Curio's** dismissal "*Ilicet*" releasing the mourners home; and
the lovers' arrival at the ruined temple on its sandy shore. All secure; the last of the antiquarian festival-
reconstruction.

- **"Ilicet" — the Roman dismissal.** The high priest closes the funeral rite with the formula *ilicet* ("you
  may go" — the standard Roman word ending a funeral), the counterpart to the *ite, missa est*. Colonna's cult-
  vocabulary is exact to the last word. The cypress (the funeral tree) branches hung and burned complete the
  cycle of mourning.
- **the whole Polyandrion, established.** Across pp. 236–238 Colonna has given the ruin its full identity: a
  collapsed seaside tomb-temple of Pluto, holding the graves of love's victims, with a yearly festival of black-
  sheep sacrifice, sea-cast ashes, rose-strewn tombs, funeral banquets and games, closed by the priest's
  "ilicet." This is the complete brief for the world's polyandrion station — its architecture (p. 236) and its
  meaning (pp. 237–238).
- **Polia as learned guide.** Polia narrates the whole antiquarian account ("magniloquent… eloquently
  expounding"): she is not only the beloved but the temple's expositor, teaching Poliphilo its history. The
  visual-novel can give her this docent-role at the polyandrion.
- The lovers now sit on the shore before the ruin (p. 239), and chapter XIX will read the **epitaphs** of the
  tombs within it.
