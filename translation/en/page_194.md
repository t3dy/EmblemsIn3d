# Page 194 — Chapter XVII

For this noble figment the outstanding artificer had shrewdly chosen the marble, which — beyond its
whiteness — was veined (in the requisite place) with black, to express the tenebrous, unlit and
nebulous air, with falling hail. Upon the flat of the said venerable Altar, rigidly rigorous, stood
forth the rude simulacrum of the garden-guardian (Priapus), with all his decent and appropriated
insignia. Which mysterious Altar a cupola'd little canopy covered, affirmed and sustained upon four
poles fixed in the ground. Which poles were diligently invested with fruited and flowered foliage,
and the top all covered with manifold flowers; and between each pole, at the rim of the opening, or
gap, of the canopy, hung, fixed, a burning lamp; and, in circuit, ornately, gold foils, by the
fresh and spring-bearing breezes inconstantly vexed, and sounding with metallic little rattles.

At which simulacrum, with the utmost religion and ancient rural and pastoral rite, certain little
flasks, or glass ampullae, with the foaming gore of the immolated Ass, and with warm milk, and
scintillating unmixed Wine, scattering, they broke; and with fruits, flowers, fronds, feast, and
jewels they libated. Now, behind this glorious Triumph, they conducted, with antique and sylvan
ceremony, the little old Janus ensnared, of ropes and tresses twisted of manifold flowers, singing
songs rurally — Talassian, Hymeneal, and Fescennine — and rustic instruments, with supreme joy and
glory, celebratedly exulting, and with solemn applause dancing, and female voices high-sounding.
For which thing, no less pleasure and delight, with stupor, did such solemn rites and celebrated
feasts here invade me, than the admiration of the preceding triumphs.

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The altar of Priapus under a flower-wreathed, lamp-hung canopy, and the
**rustic rite** performed at it — libations of ass's blood, warm milk and wine, and old **Janus led
bound in flower-ropes** amid Talassian, Hymeneal and Fescennine songs. All secure; the rite is a
learned reconstruction of Roman country religion (Priapus, the sacrifice of the ass, the marriage-
and-harvest songs).

- **The sacrifice of the ass to Priapus.** Ovid (*Fasti* I, VI) tells why the ass is sacrificed to
  Priapus (its braying once foiled his lust); Colonna stages exactly that rite, with the "foaming
  gore of the immolated ass," milk and wine. The passage is a miniature antiquarian essay on rural
  cult — the register that fills the second half of the book.
- **Janus led bound in flowers** — the two-faced god of beginnings, garlanded and led as in a
  triumph, to Fescennine (ribald wedding), Talassian (Roman marriage-cry) and Hymeneal songs: a
  fertility-and-marriage procession. It rhymes forward with Cupid's triumph (ch. XXII), where the
  lovers too are "led bound in flowers."
- **the canopy with wind-rattled gold foils and lamps** — the shrine is hung with gold leaf that
  clatters in the breeze and lamps at each corner: the same wind-animated, lamp-lit ornament as the
  later shrines. A buildable little baldachin.
- **the marble veined to depict the storm** (from p. 193) — restated: the sculptor's stone is chosen
  so its natural black veining renders Winter's clouds. Art collaborating with the material's own
  markings.
