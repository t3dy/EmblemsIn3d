Then the Monitress — all with the face turned to the pavement — that washing, tremulous, and with venerable
ministry, devoutly, upon the fire-holder poured. Whence, of a sudden, a smoke leapt to the convex heaven of
the cupola, little by little ascending — incontinently, when she had thus done, and to the ground prone
prostrated herself. Lo, a little instant — suddenly I felt move, and the grave earth quake under the round
knees, with an unthinkable din in the air; and in the Temple, with a horrendous roaring, thundering — not
otherwise than if, from the high heaven, into the measureless deep, a great mass were suddenly felt to fall —
and the strident hinges of the golden valves gave a rumble in the vaulted Temple, as, in a sinuous cavern, the
broken thunder should inwardly thunder.

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The rite's turning-point: the priestess and Polia wash the sacred blood from their
hands (the water caught in the golden *simpulum*), Polia wipes the blood-characters from the pavement with a
virgin sponge, and the priestess pours **the bloody washing onto the altar-fire** — whereupon smoke leaps up
and **the earth quakes**, the temple thundering "as if a great mass fell from heaven," the golden door-hinges
rumbling. All secure; the theophany's onset.

- **The earthquake as divine answer.** The moment the blood-washing hits the fire, the temple shakes with a
  cataclysmic din — the sign that Venus has heard (the prayer of pp. 229–231 is being answered). Colonna stages
  the miracle with full apparatus: smoke, tremor, thunder, shrieking hinges. A dramatic, stageable beat for
  the world's rite (and the visual-novel's temple climax).
- **the reserved blood consumed, then returned.** Every trace of the sacred blood — on hands and pavement — is
  gathered into the washing-water and poured on the fire: nothing of the sacrifice is wasted or profaned ("no
  other contact was licit"). The rite's scrupulous economy (cf. the cistern-immersions, p. 228) reaches its
  climax as the offering goes wholly into the flame.
- **the door-hinges thunder** — the same golden magnet-valves that opened silently (p. 213) now rumble in the
  quake: the whole building responds to the god's arrival. The temple, which moves, rings and glows on its own
  (pp. 208–213), now shakes.
- The smoke resolves into the miraculous rose-bush on p. 233 — the theophany itself.
