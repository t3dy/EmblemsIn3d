# Page 440 — Chapter XXXII

"— intruded all my good. This one, decently, I revere, and her, above all, I honorifice and worship — neither more nor less
than the Athenians their Pallas, and the Thebans the pleasant Bacchus, and the Indians Dionysus, the Romans Liber, and the
Arabians Adonis, the Ephesians Diana, and the Paphians the most-holy Venus, and, by the Tyrians, Hercules, and, by the
Aricians, the *fascelis* Diana. And this one, indefatigable, I follow — as the Heliotrope, Clymene, mutated into a flower,
revoluble gyres, following the aspect of her beloved Phoebus (Sun). And thus I, with amorous sect (devotion), hers wish to
be — ever with this same state of mind, nor, for terror, nor for delightment, ever wavering; but with affectionate
performance, to her vows most-humbly wish to succumb, like the timid partridge in the taloned claws of the rapacious Eagle.
Nor other image, nor simulacrum, nor shrine, in the intimate of my heart affixable — nor painted, nor carved — do I hold.
And, by this one, I hope to restore myself, and amorously to live glad — esteeming (it) a greater adornment than, to kings,
the Diadem, the Paludamentum to Emperors, to Pontiffs the Galerus, and the Lituus to Augurs. And, Polia dominating,
Poliphilo — this shall be my praise, glory, honorification, and sublimity — offering myself, in her amorous surrender, thus
vanquished, and thus prostrated: hoping, unanimous, to permeate to the triumphal realms, and to the delectable state, of
the Divine Cytherean (Venus)."

Standing, then, variously rapt, demented, and absorbed, in these feigned, so delicious cogitations and ponderings, and
fruition of such an image, from hour to hour, and from point to point, the adventitious and blind wounds, in the consenting
soul glowing, fecundated themselves; and (Cupid) received, and, of me, usurped wholly the jurisdiction, Tyranny, and licit
power. To all such affectionate mystery, this only supremely I desired: 'O me, could I open to her and discover my soul, and
indicate my intrinsic desires — with the Socratic affect, of fenestrating the breast, and of showing her the percussion of
the amorous wound, and the immoderate love that I bear her; and tell her of my pressing snare, and of the urgent flame, by
which, liquefied, the heart consumes itself; and show her the dissipation of the amorous life; and tell her, with piteous
and mild words, and groaning laments, the ultimate (extremity) that, in loving her, I sustain.' And thus, with the mind
erratic, cross, pathless, and vague, of intemperate ardour supremely I languished — and now sighing, and now glad; now
placid, quiet, and tranquil; some time indignant, without hope, hesitant, and discontent. That little Sacred and celebrated
day, with these commingled and inverse accessories, I consumed — briefest, for more than one atom of time esteemed, and
more than an instant moment."

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Poliphilo's rapture: he vows to **worship Polia** as the nations worship their gods, following her
"like the heliotrope the sun," her image "the only shrine in his heart," her love "a greater adornment than a king's
diadem"; and he longs to **open his breast** ("the Socratic affect") to show her his love-wound. All secure; the height of
his devotion, on the festival day.

- **Love as sole religion.** Poliphilo's devotion is cast as *worship* — Polia is his Pallas, his Venus, the "only shrine
  in my heart, no other image affixable." His love is monotheistic idolatry of the beloved. It answers Polia's own turn
  (she replaced Diana's image with his, p. 424): each lover makes the other the sole object of worship. The Diana-vs-Venus
  frame resolved in love's own cult.
- **the Socratic breast-window.** He wishes he could "fenestrate his breast" (open a window in his chest) to show Polia his
  wounded heart — the tale (from Momus) of the wish for a window in the breast to reveal the true heart. His love's
  frustration is that he cannot *show* it; the letter (pp. 443+) is his substitute — writing as the window to the heart.
- **the heliotrope.** He follows Polia "as the heliotrope (Clytie/Clymene) turns to the sun": the flower-of-faithful-
  following, and a transformation-image (Clytie turned to the heliotrope for love of the Sun) — the metamorphosis-theme
  turned, for once, to constancy rather than punishment. His love is the faithful turning the whole book commends.
- **the day "briefer than an atom."** Time flies in love's rapture — the festival day "consumed, briefer than an instant."
  The lover's altered time (cf. Poliphilo's day-that-is-a-year of longing, p. 386). His grief at its ending follows (p. 441).
