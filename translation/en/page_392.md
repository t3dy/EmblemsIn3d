"For which thing, so much had my furor amplified itself, that it permitted not the least (space) to feel the grave grief;
nay, more stingingly agitated and pricked by solicitous Love, it made me fiercer, more validly, to greater tolerance.
Nonetheless, I feigned manifold and various things in my soul, simulating prosperously (things) to come; many subsidies,
solaces and supplies, verisimilar, I composed with myself, and things of wondrous magnificence of love I largely promised
to my very self — and all I find fallacious hopes, and inane cogitations. Whence, thy choice and so enticing presence
divorced (from me), and from these sad eyes abstracted, I gave the primordium of a beginning of marrow-deep destroying
the basal underpropping of my life, and of beating (O me, most-bitterly!) the pulsing breast, sighing, thundering, with
frequent little sobs panting; and like an inane reed, or little reed-pipe, of its soul (which in thee alone lies and
lives), I found myself. And most of the time contristated, not knowing what, of this, I ought to do, I wept, tearing;
and, within myself groaning, thee, enemy of my every quiet good, I inculpated; thee, cause of all these errors and
troubles, I accused; thee, of my ardent loves the deserter, thee, sweet foe of my health, I calumniated; and, almost
out of my mind and maniac, compelled, against thee provoking the cupidine ire — as, to an atrocious and most-cruel one,
her most-holy torches (Love's) protervely spurning, and sole cause of my damages, I esteem (thee)."

Having patiently heard such a reasoning against me, up to this point, ignorant of like things — interrupting both his
molesting and displeasing and ungrateful speech, and my prayers (devotions) — without not only answering him, but even,
in the face not looking at him, indignant, blushing, suddenly I rose. And thence, having left him, made-little (of him),
as vain words, I fled, deriding him. But, the successor day come, judging that he would not persevere in the previous
day's invasion to molest me, no more, confident, I came into the said place to pray. Lo, that I — regarding him, with a
leaden and sad face, in the same manner aggressing to perturb me — thus likewise, sighing, timely he said:

"Alas, Polia most-beautiful — nay, conspicuous exemplar of whatever beauty — move thyself, mild, henceforth, and pious,
to so many my languishing pains, which, without intercalation, both day and night, and incessant, afflict me, and to
thee to come constrain me. And thy indecent, embittered heart, in so great hardness, one little bit soften; and,
repugning, insurd not thyself to my just desires, caused by the love that thy not-mortal beauties have diffused, all
through me, dolorous. And, again, extricate and loose the most-implicated bonds of thy tenacious mind. And reduce thee,

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The end of Poliphilo's first plea (his self-torment, his half-mad accusations of Polia as "sweet
foe of my health"), then **Polia's scornful flight** — she rises indignant, refuses even to look at him, and "flees,
deriding him"; and, the next day, when she returns to pray thinking him gone, he **accosts her again** with a second plea
(soften thy embittered heart, do not vilify thyself by atrocity). All secure; her cruelty at its height.

- **Polia's derision.** The cruelty she confessed (p. 382) shown in act: she hears his plea, then "without answering,
  without even looking at him… fled, deriding him," treating his words "as vain." Her self-condemnation in the telling is
  unsparing — she narrates her own scorn without softening it. This is the "swinish ferocity" she promised to reveal.
- **Poliphilo's persistence.** He returns the next day; his constancy (the fidelity-thread) becomes, from her side, an
  "invasion… to molest me" — yet his second plea is gentler ("do not vilify thyself by atrocity"): he pities *her* for
  her cruelty even as he suffers it. The lover's tenderness against the beloved's hardness.
- **"sweet foe of my health."** Poliphilo's oxymoron for Polia — the beloved as the enemy who destroys him — is the
  Petrarchan *dolce nemica*, here dramatised. His near-madness (accusing her while worshipping her) is the same divided
  state as his temptation (p. 240): love as torment.
- **the escalation.** His second, gentler plea (p. 392–393) precedes his death-swoon and her guilty flight (the chapter's
  climax, per the argument, p. 387). Polia's hardness is about to have its mortal consequence.
