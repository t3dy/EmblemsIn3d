## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Poliphilo's love-torment at its height: bereft of Polia's "light," his soul "burns in the furnaced
breast like Perillus in the bronze bull," and, "most-prompt to every supplice," he yet turns to bitter **imprecation** —
cursing Polia's cruelty and begging the gods that, if he dies, she too be denied when she begs for death. All secure; his
anguish and its dark turn.

- **The bronze bull of Perillus.** Poliphilo's burning soul is Perillus roasting in his own bronze bull (the torture-device
  he built for the tyrant Phalaris, first tried on himself): the maker destroyed by his own art — an image of the lover
  consumed by the love he "gave entry to." His self-inflicted torment, learnedly figured.
- **the willow that yields to the arrow.** He "gave gaping ingress to the golden arrow… bending like a willow-wand": his
  heart, unlike Polia's stone, *welcomed* love's wound (the wax/willow to her marble/porphyry). His susceptibility is the
  exact inverse of her hardness — the two temperaments the whole book contrasts.
- **the imprecation — and its dramatic irony.** Poliphilo curses Polia, praying that if he dies she be denied even the
  death she begs for. The reader knows this is *exactly what will happen* to her (the visions, her longing to die,
  chs. XXVII, and her later grief) — his curse is answered. But we also know it *converts* her, not destroys her. The
  double narration lets his bitter prayer land as both justice and, unwittingly, the means of her salvation. A dark
  structural payoff.
- **love's grief exceeds its joy.** His maxim — "humanity grieves the privation of pleasures more than it rejoices in
  them" — is the book's melancholy note: love's loss outweighs its having. It frames the whole courtship-torment. His
  resolve (to endure "every supplice") is the perseverance the chapter praises (p. 436), tested to its limit.
- The chapter completes (p. 443) with Poliphilo's decision to write the letter.
