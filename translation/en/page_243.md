## Notes

**Confidence: medium** (the hieroglyph-inventory is a plain description; the interpretations come on p. 244). The
tombs disposed in tribunes around the ruined round temple, then the **great red obelisk** behind it, whose base
carries **hieroglyph-panels** — the first (balance, dog, serpent, ark, sword, crown) and the second (eye, wheat,
scimitar, threshers, globe, rudder, olive-vase, ibises, coins) — which Poliphilo "reads" as Latin mottoes on
p. 244. All secure; the obelisk is the Polyandrion's central monument.

- **The obelisk of the Polyandrion.** A red-stone obelisk on a hieroglyph-carved base stands behind the round
  temple — the ruin's central monument, and the direct source for the world's polyandrion obelisk (its notes
  call it "the obelisk of Caesar"; the dedication is read on p. 244). Colonna gives the carvings picture by
  picture, then his humanist "translation" of them.
- **Colonna's hieroglyphics.** These panels are the Hypnerotomachia's famous *invented Egyptian hieroglyphs* —
  rebus-pictures Poliphilo decodes into Latin sentences (pp. 244+). They shaped the Renaissance idea of
  hieroglyphic writing (via Alberti, Valeriano) long before Champollion. The world's polyandrion animating one
  of them (the ant/elephant, p. 244) is faithful to their spirit: the stones *speak*.
- **the tombs in tribunes** — the graves of the love-dead are set in niches (*tribunes*) ringing the temple;
  their epitaphs are read through the chapter. The columns' stones (Numidian, Hymettian, Laconian) date and
  dignify the ruin.
- Poliphilo's readings of the two panels — the Julius Caesar dedication and the ant-and-elephant concord-motto —
  follow on p. 244.
