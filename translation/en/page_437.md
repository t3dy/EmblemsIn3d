# Page 437 — Chapter XXXII

"Beyond this, holy Lady — if Polia, egregious girl, here present (whose unvisited beauties the celestial spirits would
easily contaminate), without fatigue, expense, and bitterness of heart, and imperilling of the amiable life, had drawn (me)
to my ardent desire — by immortal Jove, likewise, similarly, I could, without those (torments), lightly leave her. But who
has no repugnance (opposition), a glorious palm acquires not, nor (he) who persists not at it. Because neither glory, nor
triumph, nor any good, can be consequuted without industrious fatigue; then fatigue is cause of good, and perseverance
births it, with its Companions. And for this, a more precious thing it is (and so it is held) which troublously one
acquires, than the (thing) fittingly attained. Lucius Siccius Dentatus, therefore, of his fortitude would not be dignified
of praise and memory, had the scars, drawn-over, of his wounds, at the back been seen — because, to the degenerate
soldiers, easy presents itself the wounding from behind; but, to the strong resistant, appertains only the wounding in
front (facing the foe).

"For which thing, Love — in my contaminated, infected, and most-defiled heart (of its morbid qualities) being, unbecomingly,
risen an invader — used a more burning displeasingness than the meridional Aethon of Phoebus uses to the fresh little
flowers and soft plants and herbs; which, immoderately, it burns more than the insatiable Vulcan (fire of) Etna. For which
cause, tightly being, of such an effect, haltered, infinite accidents, and various accessories, and manifold peril, with
evident danger, showed my pitiless, collapsed, and infirm fortune against me, wrongfully mocking. Of which pernicious and
deadly cases, at present, in some part, obeying thy vow (wish), I shall begin briefly to narrate.

"Notable Priestess, and most-outstanding Lady: after that my lethal languors are quieted and somewhat settled — hastening
to satisfy thy benign precepts, more with my pleasant words than with tearful sobs, I would touch that part where I am
allotted so most-preclear a love (being silent of that which already is said) — (which) more fervid and active, wholly,
constrained me. Now, me, in thy venerable and choice, and Polia's gracious, presence, happy reputing, I shall take a modest
daring; since, with a most-humane face, thou showest thyself not to be aggrieved of this audition.

"Phoebus being risen, to re-sup the fresh tears of the weeping Aurora, with the refreshed and golden rays — every star of
the orient put to flight — illuminated, with his Eoan (dawn-light), our hemisphere, disterminated from the horizon; and,
the laborific day made, the lazy quiet dispelled, and the ponderous earth —"

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Poliphilo continues his narration: hard-won love is the more precious (no palm without struggle;
Siccius Dentatus's honourable front-scars); Love invaded his heart "more burning than Etna," bringing "manifold peril";
and he begins the tale proper — at dawn, he came to Diana's temple (the festival where he first saw Polia, p. 438). All
secure; the frame of his courtship-account.

- **Perseverance, restated: love earned is love valued.** Poliphilo's thesis — "a thing troublously acquired is more
  precious than one fittingly attained"; "no palm without repugnance." His suffering *made* his love valuable; had Polia
  yielded easily, he "could lightly have left her." The book's moral (perseverance, p. 436) deepened: the trial is not
  incidental but constitutive of true love. The world/novel's whole quest-structure rests on this.
- **the front-scars of Siccius Dentatus.** The Roman soldier honoured because his wounds were all in *front* (facing the
  enemy, never fleeing): Poliphilo's emblem of honourable endurance. His love-wounds, borne facing the trial, are his
  honour. Love as valorous combat, once more (the Agon, p. 436).
- **the tale begins at dawn.** Colonna opens Poliphilo's narrative with the ornate dawn-formula (Phoebus drying Aurora's
  tears) — the epic gesture for "one morning." His story of falling in love (the temple festival) starts on p. 438. This
  is *his* version of the first sight Polia gave from her side (her window, p. 386); here it is the temple.
- **love as fire hotter than Etna.** Standard, but the register is his: Love "invaded" his heart, burning "more than
  Vulcan's Etna." His passion is stated at full pitch before the narrative shows its origin.
