## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Poliphilo's first letter continues to its plea: he asks Polia (whom he trusts "most-humane, noble,
magnanimous") to give "not-doubtful faith" that he bears "the greatest love that ever a lover bore a woman," asking only
"thy precious love"; he vows to love none but her while he lives, "wholly in thee alive, and in me wholly dead." All
secure; the letter's petition.

- **"Wholly in thee alive, in me wholly dead."** The letter's central paradox — his life is in Polia, not himself (the
  self-loss of love, cf. "never found myself mine but all hers," p. 430). His whole being is transferred to her "whitening
  bosom." This is the mutual self-gift stated in the letter's own voice — and, ironically, prophetic: he will *literally*
  die of this love (chs. XXVI) and live again in her (ch. XXIX).
- **the flattery-plea.** He praises Polia's virtues (humane, magnanimous, mild) precisely to *make* her so — arguing that
  her heavenly gifts would be "hallucinated" (wasted, ungrateful to God) if she refused love (the *carpe diem* / use-your-
  gifts argument, the nurse's too, p. 412). The letter is rhetoric aimed at softening the stone heart he hopes love can melt
  (p. 443). It fails (she is unmoved), which is the point: rhetoric alone cannot; only the visions and grace convert her.
- **asks "only thy love."** He asks nothing but her love — "the greatest and most-singular love that ever a lover bore." The
  letter is a pure suit, humble and total. Its failure (Polia unmoved) drives the second letter and the whole tragic
  courtship. Poliphilo's persistence in writing though unanswered is the perseverance the book commends.
- The letter concludes on p. 446 (still ch. XXXIII), and a second follows.
