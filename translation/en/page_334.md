Beyond this, some, with most-purple buskins on the little feet, with gold ankle-pieces, shod
themselves trimly. Which terminated upon the white pulp of the calves, where a genteel
re-woven band of a *policaria*'s breadth went round, wondrously adorned with precious stones,
with little laces of gold and silk. Some, barefoot, bore the vermilion silk sandals with
voluptuous vanity. Many were shod with soccoli of golden leather, closely chased with many
elegant figures. Very many wore crepidae of rosy leather, edged with gold. Some exquisitely
shod with polished, crescent-shaped footwear, or with a recurved opening, and of thonged soles,
with more new and marvellous ligatures, laces and thongs than could ever be told, of blue-grey
silk and of golden filament, with the vaguest and most-pleasing interlacings knotting round the
fleshy heel — more than one could devise in the mind — and from the narrow sole the little snare,
prettily entangled in a knot, of Armenian texture, came out, impeded, holding the great toe. And
thence toward the least (toe) it passed off, whiter than bone-lime. Then, climbing over the
little swelling insteps, most-beautifully they conjoined, with a little tongue let down from the
surrounding binding of the heel, over the top of the ivory foot, adorned with wormwork of gold
and of lucent gems.

Some, of silken satin cloth, with figures heaped by such artifice, with varied colouring
voluptuously shod, distinct from that of the superb and nymphish garb. Concluded comely with
gold buckles, with hooked clasp, and ensnared. Over the exquisite crepidae, the little swelling
insteps pressed by the elegant little gold-eyeleted straps, and the pearled instep-covers, and
with little knots of coloured silk and of gold with the ends of burnished silver fixed, were
decently interwoven, retained, with so beautiful an adornment as to blind and put out the eyes
of the unbridled bulls. Oh, what polish, beauty and brightness; what perspicacious ornament;
what unwonted works! With what keen diligence was seen the thoughtfully-found artifice of
giving to the beholders sweet and incentive pleasure, and a little death.

In the dressing, likewise, they were arrayed, beyond the superb and delightful invention, as
comely as the happy place condignly demanded, with artful fringes and slashes, or breaks, with
subtle intercutting of versicolour, and most-decorously with wondrous wormwork stitching. And
from the tight girdle downward, in front, hung in order the golden little tags of wormwork
labour. Some, in place of the little pears (tags), had chains of pearls of hazelnut thickness. At
the bottom, with a fastigiate length of alabaster figure; and likewise in place of the golden

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The footwear-passage, completing the costume: purple buskins with gold
ankle-bands, silk sandals, gilt-leather soccoli, rosy crepidae, and elaborately-thonged
crescent shoes; then the girdles, fringes, slashed hems and golden tags of the gowns. All
secure; the shoe-vocabulary (cothurnus, soccolo, crepida) is antique, and the lacing is
described thong by thong.

- **Footwear as fine art.** Colonna gives the same exhaustive attention to shoes as to hair and
  jewels: buskins to the calf, barefoot silk sandals, chased gilt-leather soccoli, gold-edged
  crepidae, and crescent shoes with silk-and-gold lacings knotted around the heel and toe. The
  procession is finished to the sole.
- **"sweet… pleasure, and a little death."** Again the interpretive turn (as with the hair-
  snares of p. 333): the ornament is "found artifice" designed to give beholders *dolce piacere
  e morire* — a foretaste of the erotic *petite mort*. Colonna keeps naming the triumph's beauty
  as a designed instrument of desire.
- **"the unbridled bulls"** — the male onlookers, whose eyes the shoes "blind and put out": the
  same disarming-by-beauty that took the gods' arms (pp. 326–327), now working on men.
- This closes the three-page costume ekphrasis; the text returns to the procession and the
  triumphal car in the pages following.
