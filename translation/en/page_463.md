## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The nymphs draw the lovers into a **joyous dance** at the fount, then depart, leaving **Polia and
Poliphilo alone**; and Poliphilo makes his **final love-avowal** ("thou art the unique pre-elected among mortals… conserve
my soul in thy eternal Love… my singular Goddess"), to which Polia begins her answering endearment ("my delight, my sole
refrigerium, dominator of my heart"). All secure; the lovers alone at last, on the threshold of the dream's end.

- **The lovers alone — the dream at its fullest.** All obstacles gone (the cruelty, the death, the visions, the priestess's
  blessing), Poliphilo and Polia are finally alone "in this most-pleasant place, all inflamed of love." The union is
  complete, mutual, blessed. Poliphilo's avowal and Polia's answer are the pure, reciprocated love the whole book (both
  books) worked toward — each calling the other "my Goddess," "my delight," "my eternal Love." The symmetry is total.
- **the names Philareta and Eupathia.** Poliphilo calls Polia "Philareta" (virtue-loving) and "Eupathia" (well/rightly-
  feeling) — new love-names crediting her converted heart: she who was cruel now *loves virtue* and *feels rightly*. The
  names mark her transformation as complete and celebrated. (They also continue the book's Greek-coinage naming.)
- **the imminent end.** Polia's answer breaks off mid-endearment — and the argument (p. 462) tells us how it ends: at the
  height of this mutual avowal, "embracing him, she disappeared, and the sleep." The dream dissolves at the instant of
  fullest love (p. 464). The *Hypnerotomachia*'s central melancholy: the beloved is possessed only in dream, and vanishes
  at waking. The most complete union is the most fragile.
- **love as reciprocal worship.** Both lovers now call the other "Goddess/dominator of my heart" — the mutual idolatry that
  Book I and II both moved toward. The union is two worshippers, each the other's deity. The dream's consummation, and its
  vanishing, complete on p. 464.
