## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The nurse turns from myth to a **cautionary exemplum** (its point aimed at Polia): first, praise of
Polia's Venus-worthy beauty ("worthy of Venus's warm services, not gelid unfruitful Diana"), then the tale of a beautiful,
high-born girl who **refused all suitors** — including a worthy peer — out of "ambitious levity," wasting her best years in
"cold, unpartnered beds," until at twenty-eight Cupid took his revenge. All secure; the beginning of the negative example.

- **The willow that loses its fruit.** The nurse's governing image for Polia: beauty unused for love is "like the fruit-
  losing willow" — a waste of nature's "outstanding office" (generation). To neglect love is to make oneself "an enemy of
  the gods." This is the *carpe diem* argument (Venus Physizoa's creed, p. 229) turned to persuasion: beauty exists to be
  used in love, and refusing it is both folly and impiety.
- **the exemplum's mirror.** The refusing girl is Polia's double — beautiful, high-born, cruel to a worthy suitor, wasting
  her youth. The nurse holds up Polia's own crime and shows its consequence (Cupid's revenge, pp. 413–414). The tale is a
  prophecy of Polia's fate if she does not repent, and a warning against *both* extremes (cold refusal, then — the twist —
  uncontrollable lust).
- **Ariadne's crown, the Hyades.** Polia's beauty outshines the constellations (Ariadne's crown, the Hyades on Taurus's
  brow): the surpassing-topos, now in the nurse's flattery. Praise softens Polia before the warning.
- Cupid's revenge (the golden arrow, the lust, the loveless marriage) unfolds on pp. 413–414.
