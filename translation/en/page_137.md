# Page 137 — Chapter X

Behold, without any waiting it was opened, and we let in; and there came to us a matron with a golden
sword, with fierce eyes and ready in her look, brandishing her raised and shining blade in her hand. In
the middle of which there hung suspended a little crown of gold and a branch of palm laid across it;
with arms of Hercules and made for labour, with a magnanimous bearing, with a slender belly, small
mouth, strong shoulders; in her face with a showing of not being frightened by any hard and difficult
undertaking, but of a fierce and giant-like spirit. And her name was Eucleia, and she was venerably
attended by six noble young women, obedient. The name of the first Merimnasia; of the second, Epitide;
of the next, Ergasilea. The fourth was called Anectea. And the fifth was named Statia. The last was
called Olistea.

The place and site seemed to me to be very laborious. Perceiving this, Logistica readily began to sing
in the Dorian mode and tone, having taken the lyre from Thelemia's hand, and playing sweetly, to say:
"O Poliphilo, let it not weary you to strive manfully in this place. For, the labour being lifted away
and removed, the good remains." So vehement was her song that I was already consenting to dwell with
these young women, however much the habit of labour appeared there — when suddenly Thelemia, trim and
coaxing, and with a sweet semblance, said to me: "It seems to me a reasonable thing that before you
settle here, my dearest little Poliphilo, you should by all means see the third door as well."
