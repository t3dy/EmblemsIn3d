# Page 5 — Front matter

*(The summary of the whole work, addressed to the reader. Italian — and, unlike everything
around it, plain Italian. One unbroken sentence-chain of about five hundred words.)*

Reader, if you wish briefly to understand what is contained in this work, know that Poliphilo
tells of having seen wondrous things in sleep; which work he calls, by a Greek word, *the strife
of love in a dream*. Wherein he feigns to have seen many antique things worthy of memory, and
all that he says he saw he describes point by point and by their proper terms, in an elegant
style: pyramids, obelisks, the greatest ruins of buildings; the difference of columns, their
measure, the capitals, bases, epistyles — that is, straight beams — bent beams, zophori, that is
friezes, cornices with their ornaments. A great horse. A very great elephant. A colossus. A
magnificent gate, with its measures and its ornaments. A terror. The five senses in five nymphs.
An excellent bath, fountains, the palace of the queen who is Free Will. A royal and most
excellent feast. The variety of jewels, or rather precious stones, and their nature. A game of
chess danced to three measures of music. Three gardens: one of glass, one of silk, one in a
labyrinth which is human life. A brick peristyle, in the middle of which the Trinity was set out
in hieroglyphic figures — that is, sacred Egyptian carvings. The three gates, at which he stayed
behind with Polia. How she was in habit and in bearing. Polia leads him to look upon four
wondrous triumphs of Jove, the loves of the gods, those of the poets, the various affection and
effect of love. The triumph of Vertumnus with Pomona. The sacrifice to Priapus in the antique
manner. A marvellous temple, described by its art, where sacrifices were made with wonderful
rites and religion. How Polia and he went to the shore to await Cupid, where there was a
destroyed temple; in which Polia persuades Poliphilo to go in and look upon the antique things.
And there he saw many epitaphs, and a hell painted in mosaic. How for terror he departed thence
and came to Polia. And as they stood there Cupid came with the little ship rowed by six nymphs,
into which both entered, and Love made sail with his wings. And there honour was done to Cupid
by the sea-gods and goddesses, and by the nymphs and monsters; and they came to the Cytherean
island, which Poliphilo describes fully, laid out in groves, meadows, gardens, rivers and
fountains; and the presents that were made to Cupid, and how he was received by the nymphs; and
how upon a triumphal car they went to a wondrous theatre, all described, in the middle of the
island. In the midst of which is the Venereal fountain of seven precious columns, and all that
was done there. And, Mars coming, they departed thence and went to the fountain where was the
sepulchre of Adonis. And here the nymphs tell of the anniversary that Venus kept in his memory.
Then the nymphs persuade Polia to tell her origin and her falling in love. And this is in the
first book. In the second, Polia tells her parentage, the building of Treviso, the difficulty of
her falling in love and its happy issue; and, the history being completed with infinite and most
worthy accessories and corollaries, at the song of the nightingale he awoke. Farewell.

## Notes

**Confidence: high.** The Italian is uncharacteristically plain — this page is the one place in
the book where somebody explains it in ordinary words.

- **This is the most useful page in the front matter**, and it is worth setting beside
  `COVERAGE.md`: it is a contemporary inventory of what the book contains, written by someone who
  had it in front of them. Everything it names has a place in this world or a line in the build
  queue.
- **"The palace of the queen who is Free Will."** *El palatio della regina che è el libero
  arbitrio* — the summary states flatly what the narrative only implies: **Queen Eleuterylida is
  Free Will**, her name from Greek *eleutheria*. Her two handmaids Logistica (Reason) and
  Thelemia (Desire) then read as the two counsels free will must choose between, which is exactly
  what happens at the three gates.
- **"One in a labyrinth which is human life."** The third garden is glossed here as an allegory
  and not a curiosity — the water-labyrinth of chapter IX, *where the boats go always forward and
  never back*. The Dream mode's narration already quotes that line.
- **"A terror."** *Uno spavento* — the dragon in the vaults beneath the pyramid, chapter V. The
  summary gives it two words. It is the only moment of physical danger in Book I, and the only
  station this project built as a playable crawl.
- **"Point by point and by their proper terms."** *Di puncto in puncto et per proprii vocabuli* —
  the summary's own account of why the book is hard: it insists on the technical word for
  everything. *Epistyli*, *zophori*, *coronice* are given here with glosses, in 1499, for readers
  who did not know them either.
- **The nightingale.** The whole dream ends *al canto dilla philomela* — at the song of the
  nightingale, Philomela. The last page of Book II keeps it.
