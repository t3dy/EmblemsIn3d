# Page 225 — Chapter XVIII

I cannot in the least let myself be persuaded that such rites, ceremonies, sacrifices were found out by Numa
Pompilius, nor at Caere of Tuscany, nor ever in Etruria, nor by the holy Jew; nor with so much religious
observance and order did the Memphitic Seers sacrifice and burn-offerings to Apis in Egypt, immersing the
golden patera in the Nile; nor yet with so most-religious a veneration, in the city of Rhamnus of Euboea, was
Rhamnusia (Nemesis) worshipped; nor was Jupiter Anxur worshipped with such superstition; nor those who, at
Feronia inspired, found such rites — walking without harm upon the kindled coals. Nor so entranced were the
Edonian Clodones, nor the Mimallones, aspired by the numen, as in the present they were entranced.
Deservedly I judge (beyond that which I have openly beheld) that, by the things prepared and superstitiously
disposed, (a marvel) ought to succeed. For, of the hair, the golden Polia Nymph — of such-made office worthy,
in the sacred things imbued and initiated — no sooner saw the nod of the sacristan monitress, than, from the
most-clean pavement, without voice, delay, or noise (no other one moving), most-promptly, religiously, she
rose. Whence, by the sacred monitress, to a wondrous hyacinthine little urn, set apart in the sacello, she
was conducted — such as, of such artifice, Mentor never knew to make.

And I, most-attentive, regarding her, in all acts scrutinously observed. And in aspect now I saw her such as
the most-lucid Phoebus, with the new day, colouring, paints the fresh aurora. And there, ceremoniously, with
prompt and untouched hands, an odorous liquor she drew out; and her milky and vermilioned face, breathing
purpling roses, with the delicate moistened hands, all shrewdly, she irrigated. Whence, thus devoutly
purified, with more sincerity than perhaps had the virgin Aemilia — before the step of the most-holy Altar,
where stood a wondrous golden candelabrum (which was of most-exact and much-admired expression, and elegantly
circum-ornated and knobbed with thick gems; in its summit stood forth, exigently, a round opening of a little
shell, or a platter, less than an arm's embrace) —

In this, then, was placed the sweetest sperm of the huge whales (ambergris); odoriferous musk; the crystalline
and fugitive Camphor; odorous Ladanum of great Crete; *Thymiama* and Mastic; both the Storaxes; the almond-
scented Benzoin; the ponderable Aloewood; *blatte-bisantie*, or Indian nails (onycha); and the happy germens of
Arabia. Which all precious things were, with distributed weight, most-excellently graded. To which the

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The opening of **chapter XVIII**: the surpassing of every ancient rite (Numa, the
Egyptian Apis-priests, Nemesis at Rhamnus, the fire-walkers of Feronia, the Bacchic Clodones); then **Polia
purifies herself** with rose-scented water and lays the **incense** (ambergris, musk, camphor, ladanum,
storax, benzoin, aloewood, onycha, Arabian spices) on a gemmed golden candelabrum before the altar. All secure;
the incense-list is a real perfumer's catalogue.

- **The rite as surpassing all rites.** Colonna opens by declaring this ceremony beyond Numa's Roman religion,
  the Egyptian Apis-cult, the fire-walking priests of Feronia, and the ecstatic Bacchantes — the surpassing-
  topos applied to liturgy itself (cf. p. 215, "more than the Etruscan discipline"). The temple of Venus
  outranks the whole ancient world's worship.
- **the incense of Venus.** The offering is a complete list of the great aromatics — ambergris, musk, camphor,
  ladanum, two storaxes, benzoin, aloewood, onycha, Arabian spice — "graded by weight": a precise perfume-
  formula burned to the goddess. Ready sensory detail for the world's temple-rite (cf. the perfumed pavements
  of Cythera, p. 355).
- **Polia as officiant.** Polia — "imbued and initiated in the sacred things" — performs the rite herself, at
  the priestess's nod: she is not merely the beloved but a trained temple-virgin (the "chaste college" of
  p. 217). Her competence is stressed ("more sincere than the virgin Aemilia," a Vestal). The visual-novel can
  stage her as celebrant.
- The kindling, the dove-sacrifice, and the sung hymn follow on p. 226.
