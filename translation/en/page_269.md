# Page 269 — Chapter XIX

> **ASPICE, VIATOR** — *(the epitaph of Sertullius and Rancilia)*
>
> "Behold, traveller, (the ashes) of Quintus Sertullius and of my sweet betrothed, the maiden daughter of Gaius
> Rancilia, together; and then afterward read what licentious Fate does. In our very flowering age, when the sharper
> force of love rushed upon us, mutually captured — at length, with father-in-law and mother-in-law consenting — in
> solemn wedding we are joined. But, O unhappy fate! On the first night, when, by the law of importunate pleasure to
> be discharged, we were compelled to extinguish the torch and render our vows to the shades of Venus — alas, in the
> very act, the marital house, collapsing, oppressed us both, most joyful, embraced, with the final sweetness. O
> deadly Sister (Fate)! Nor do I know what I had done (to deserve it). Consider: our longer hour was not then in the
> fates. Dear parents, bewail not our wretched and ghost-marked funerals with mourning nor tears, lest you make them
> more unhappy. But you — live your years longer than ours, best reader, and live your own. (Farewell.)"

---

## Notes

**Confidence: high** (the epitaph is secure). The wedding-night tragedy of **Sertullius and Rancilia**: a couple, wed
with their parents' blessing, are **crushed together in their embrace on their first night** when the marital house
collapses in the very act of love — "most joyful, embraced, with the final sweetness." (The next monument begins on
p. 270.) All secure.

- **Death in the act of love — the cruelest fortune.** Sertullius and Rancilia die not of love's excess or refusal but
  of sheer accident, at the instant of consummation: the house falls "in the very act." It is the Polyandrion's most
  arbitrary tragedy — pure "licentious Fate," love and death made simultaneous. The epitaph's turn from horror to
  gentle blessing ("live your years longer than ours, best reader, and live your own") is deeply moving. Voiceable
  material for the world/novel.
- **"extinguish the torch."** The wedding-night consummation is called "extinguishing the torch" — the same phrase as
  the temple-rite (Polia extinguishing Diana's torch, p. 216, 236): the quenched marriage-torch here becomes literal
  death. Colonna's imagery binds the two temples (Venus's fulfilment, the Polyandrion's ruin) once more.
- **the blessing of the reader** — the epitaph ends not with a curse (Nevia, p. 256) but with a *benediction*: "live
  your own years." The gentlest of the tombs' addresses to the passer-by; the dead wish the living long life. A tender
  counterweight to the chapter's darkness.
- **the coffered turtledove-niche** — a new tomb with a perspectival coffered vault and drinking doves (Venus's birds):
  Colonna's continued variety of buildable tomb-forms.
