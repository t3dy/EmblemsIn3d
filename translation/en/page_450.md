## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The third letter continues: Poliphilo's eyes, "the primary cause of my ruin," cannot stop
desiring Polia's; he pleads that, could she *see* him languishing, she would pity him; warns that her refusal earns only
"an infamous mark of cruelty and inexorable divine vengeance"; and asks "what treasure so precious as two united lovers?
what worse than to be loved and not love?" — threatening that, if she persists, he must die. All secure; the plea's dark
turn toward his death.

- **"To be loved, and not love."** The letter's central charge — the worst thing is "to be loved and not love." Polia's
  sin, named: she receives love and gives none. It is the imbalance Poliphilo asked the priestess to "equalise" (p. 430),
  and the "inequality" that is love's tragedy. The whole book's drama is the closing of this gap (her conversion).
- **the warning of vengeance — prophetic again.** He warns that her cruelty earns "inexorable divine vengeance… which lets
  not the criminal flee-er escape." This *exactly* foretells her punishment-visions (the fleeing damsels, the executioners,
  chs. XXVII) — Poliphilo's letter predicts, without knowing, the very torments she will suffer. The double narration
  makes his warning the plot's outline.
- **"two uniform lovers — the world's most precious treasure."** The letter's positive vision: mutual love is the
  supreme good, "no treasure so precious as two uniform lovers." This is the union the temple makes and the book
  celebrates — stated by Poliphilo as the thing Polia's cruelty denies them both. The value the whole work commends.
- **the threat of death.** He tells her plainly: if she persists, "it will behove me to depart this life" — the death
  that (chs. XXVI, XXIX) actually happens. His letter announces his own death-by-cruelty before it occurs. The letter
  concludes on p. 451.
