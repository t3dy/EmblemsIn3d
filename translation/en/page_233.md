# Page 233 — Chapter XVIII

Whence, full and surrounded by marvellous terror, and fearfully agitated, I invoked, silent, whatever divine
subsidy and pity. And, scarce somewhat opening the frightened eyes, I looked to the smoking Altar. Out of
which, purest smoke I saw miraculously issue, germinating, and successively multiplying itself into a
verdant **rose-bush** — which, with multiplied little branches, a great part of the sacred sacello copiously
occupied, to the raised altitude of the cupola, with a numerosity of vermilion and rubricating roses
together, and with many round fruits, of wondrous odour fragrant, of colouring candid-and-vermilioned; which
offered themselves more pleasing to the taste than, by chance, are not those which, to the famished mouth of
Tantalus, present themselves. More beautiful were they not, those desired by Eurystheus (the Hesperides'
apples).

Upon this rosy bush, then, appeared three white little doves, with some little birds gregariously fluttering
in the branches; and, festive, they leapt, colluding, and most-sweetly sang. And, by such a portent, the
numen presented — in that guise occulted — of the most-holy Mother, rightly I suspected.

For which thing, the sacrificing Priestess having risen, with matronal decorum — and Polia too, with special
beauty (more than ever to my eyes she appeared gracious), and in her sweet aspect laughing — both, reassuring
me, invited me into the sacrosanct sacello to enter; and, within, calling me before, venerating, of the divine
Altar, between the Priestess and Polia genuflecting me, the Priestess, with veteran ceremony, three of the
miraculous fruits extirpated: the one for herself reserved; of the two, to me one, and to Polia the other,
offering. Those, with revoked religion, and the utmost integrity of heart, together, all three, we tasted.

## Notes

**Confidence: high.** The **miracle and its communion** — the climax of chapter XVIII: from the smoking altar
a **verdant rose-bush miraculously springs**, filling the sanctuary to the dome with red roses and fragrant
fruits, three white doves and birds in its branches (Venus's epiphany "occulted" in the bush); the priestess
leads Poliphilo into the sanctuary, plucks three fruits, and **the three eat together** — Priestess, Poliphilo,
Polia. This ends chapter XVIII. All secure. *(On the page the closing lines are set as a tapering colophon —
the printer's chapter-end device.)*

- **The rose-bush theophany.** The prayer's Adonis-precedent (p. 230 — his blood turned to a flower) is
  answered in kind: the lovers' sacrifice yields a rose-bush that fills the sanctuary, Venus present within it
  ("occulted"), her doves in its branches. The rose — the flower born of Venus's grief and love — is the
  goddess's own sign that the union is blessed. A spectacular, buildable miracle for the world's rite: a rose-
  bush erupting from the altar to the dome.
- **the communion of the fruit.** Poliphilo, admitted at last into the inner sanctuary (he was kept at the
  threshold, p. 220), is given one of three miraculous fruits; he, Polia and the priestess **eat together** — a
  shared sacrament sealing the love. It answers Poliphilo's Iphigenia-terror (p. 227): Polia is not taken away,
  but joined to him in the goddess's own communion. This is the mystical fulfilment the whole temple sequence
  built toward.
- **Polia "more gracious than ever, laughing."** After the terror, Polia's reassuring laughter and heightened
  beauty mark the resolution: the rite has not consumed her but transfigured her. The emotional counterpart to
  the miracle.
- **The rite's climax reached.** From the incense-rite and the Cupid-epiphany (pp. 225–227), through the great
  prayer to Venus (pp. 229–231) and the earthquake (p. 232), to the rose-bush and the shared fruit here. The
  remaining pages of chapter XVIII (234–241) bring the lovers out of the temple toward the **ruined temple and
  its antique epitaphs**, which occupy chapter XIX (the longest in the range, pp. 242–282).
