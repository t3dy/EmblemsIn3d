## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The miracle completes: Poliphilo **revives fully** in Polia's arms — she kisses him, bares her
breast, and he wakes, meekly saying *"Polia, my sweet Lady, why so wrongfully dost thou make me suffer?"* — and Polia, her
heart bursting with joy, gives him a "must-sweet kiss," the two **entwined like the serpents of the caduceus** (and
Asclepius's staff). All secure; the resolution of Polia's crime and the reunion of the lovers.

- **"Why so wrongfully dost thou make me suffer?" — the revived lover's first word.** Poliphilo wakes not with reproach for
  his death but with the same tender plaint he made alive (ch. XXVI): his love is unchanged by dying. His gentleness meets
  her guilt with grace — she is forgiven by his very reviving. The circle closes: the man she killed for loving her, loves
  her still, revived by her love.
- **the serpent-embrace.** The lovers entwine "like the serpents of the caduceus… and Asclepius's staff" — the emblem of
  *healing* (Asclepius, the divine physician who raised the dead). The image seals the miracle: love is the medicine that
  raised Poliphilo, and the reunion is a healing. It also echoes the Aldine dolphin-and-anchor and the book's serpent-
  imagery (the polyandrion, the caduceus-hieroglyph, p. 244), here redemptive.
- **the two temperatures resolved.** Polia's blood, "constricted by grief and fright," now "loosens the veins" in joy — the
  frozen circulation of her cruelty thawed into warm life, as she thawed his cold body (p. 421). Both lovers are restored
  to life and warmth together. Diana's cold is finally overcome by Venus's fire, in both hearts.
- **the reunion.** This is the meeting Book I showed from the outside (the temple-rite, the union, ch. XVII); Book II now
  gives its true origin — the revival by love. The lovers are joined; what remains (pp. 423+) is Diana's nymphs driving
  them out, and their coming to Venus's temple, where Book I's dream will meet Book II's history.
