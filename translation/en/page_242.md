# Page 242 — Chapter XIX

Above all the excogitated and exquisite torments of love not mediocrely beset seeing myself — and to the pre-
chosen and salubrious and present remedy, and my heart-healer, being near — stupefied I marvelled that,
against every natural order, she more morbidly infected me. And whatever her outstanding act, ornate eloquence,
biting regard, from so opportune a health, finding myself near, abdicated me (drove me off); whence ever more
multiplied an exhortable suasion, of wishing, to the provoking commodity (a grateful little gift to ravishers),
not to present myself ungrateful and pusillanimous. Fremishing like a furious and snarling dog, the pursued
beast having reached, in the alpine leaps: so I, neither more nor less, a furious coveter, the very desired prey
here reached, wholly to satisfy myself. Accustomed now to an assiduous and familiar death of love, I did not,
for this, repute the atrocity a passion, that thence might be ensued. And therefore every inconvenience, however
most-damaging, licit it suaded me. Whence my *eutrapela* (witty) Polia, shrewd of the improbe condition of
blind Love, and aware — to mortify so importunate a burning, and somewhat to syncopate it, and, as my singular
preserver, succouring — thus benignly says to me:

"Poliphilo, of all mine most-beloved, I am never unaware that antique works supremely please thee to see. So,
conveniently thou canst, in this interval, while we await lord Cupid, go licitly to see these deserted temples,
collapsed by devouring and obsolete old age — or by fire assumed, or by many years shaken — to thy solace to
behold, and the noble fragments left, of veneration most-worthy, to speculate. And I, in this place sitting,
content, will await thee, watchful, awaiting our lord to come, who must ferry us to the holy and coveted
maternal realm."

Then I, greatly most-avid — with the other most-commendable works seen — also these, most-accurately and
multividuously to contemplate, rose from the happy session, from under the tempered shades of laurel, and of
myrtle, and among tallish cypresses (then, about the place, the honeysuckle painting its odorous flowers, where
a voluble flowering jasmine, with soft shade, opaquely covered us, disseminating over us copiously its whitest
flowers, at that time most-sweetly odorous). Absorbed, without other cogitating, from beside Polia, by those
devious embankments of fastigiate and vast heap and ruin — in the greater part occupied with ground-ivy, and
*terrambula*, and implicated with thorns — solicitous I came. Where, ponderingly, I conjectured this to have
been a magnificent and marvellous temple, of choice and superb structure, according as the proven and

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The opening of **chapter XIX** (the longest in the range): Poliphilo's desire still
raging, Polia shrewdly sends him to explore the ruined **Polyandrion** while they await Cupid (defusing the
temptation of ch. XVIII); he enters the flower-shaded ruin, conjecturing it a once-magnificent round temple. All
secure.

- **Polia's stratagem.** Knowing "antique works supremely please" Poliphilo, Polia sends him to survey the ruin
  precisely to cool his "importunate burning" — turning his antiquarian passion against his erotic one. It is
  the same self-mastery of p. 241, now aided by Polia's wit ("eutrapela"). The chapter's whole antiquarian
  content is thus framed as a therapy for desire.
- **the chapter's shape.** Chapter XIX is Poliphilo *reading the ruin* — its obelisk, hieroglyphs, tombs and
  epitaphs (the graves of dead lovers), culminating in the Rape-of-Proserpina relief that frightens him back to
  Polia. It is the antiquarian heart of the Polyandrion, and the direct source for the world's polyandrion
  station (obelisk, hieroglyphs, tombs).
- **the flower-shaded ruin** — laurel, myrtle, cypress, honeysuckle, jasmine over the thorny rubble: the same
  life-in-death setting as p. 239. Colonna keeps the tomb beautiful.
- The obelisk of Caesar and its hieroglyphs begin on p. 243.
