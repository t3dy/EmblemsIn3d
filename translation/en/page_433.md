# Page 433 — Chapter XXXI

"— with every solemn and ratified faith and most-firm loyalty, I have disposed myself, with thee, wholly amorously to
live; and to be, by nothing, condemned in the holy college of the crowned lovers, in the sight of the Divine Mother, and
of her flying God, individual son, of obstinate immanity — the ire of whom frightens me, inasmuch as he, of that, has
ominously shown me, threatening (his) part.

"But thou, who so festively and perpetually, to the furious torches, and to this angarious weight of that enormous Cupid,
a perpetual bondman, wishedst to succumb — and so many unjust agitations, and painful wounds, for me intimately hast
tolerated — I esteem it most-equitable that, also, for me, vice-versa, and really, I fulfil thy gracious and meritorious
will, and, satisfying the ardent desire, refrigerate (it); and, of my inviolate and florid person, licitly take delight
copiously. Whence, Poliphilo, my sweet and amorous little soul, my unique presidium, and triumphal Bulla of my breast,
and most-safe Asylum whither securely I take refuge, at present impelled by the insolent and impelling Cupid — my treasure,
above all the jewels of the world most-appreciated — no sooner, here, a circumspectress, saw thee, cupidinously beheld
thee, than, broken and spurned every hardness, and excluded every contradiction, I disposed myself, with my pleasant voice
to respond, and placidly, to thy precious love, with all the soul, with all the heart, with all my power, benignly to
assent. Wherefore, already, in the intrinsic of the praecordia — nay, in the base of my life, and of the soul, burnt and
scorched — deservedly, to one and the other (of us) I wish opportunely to remedy: doubting sanely (rightly) lest the
inexorable cruelty, used against those seen girls (in the vision), monstrously admonishing me, in me, for no cause, more
should be found. Most-limpidly conjecturing that Eurydice the Rhodopeian would not have been by the venomous viper bitten
— nor, then, for that, upon the three-team, by Pluto, to the infernal and tartarine seats and high Barathrum conveyed —
had she, to Aristaeus, pleasing shown herself. Nor Daphne, by the like, daughter of Peneus of Thessaly, would have vainly
repented herself of the verdant fronds (her laurel-transformation), Phoebus not attending, had she, to the newest
entreated, shown herself easy. Nor even Hesperie, likewise, from the tortuous serpent would have proved the mortal biting,
had she, to Aesacus, benign been. And Arethusa the Nymph, bathing in the Alphean waves, would not have mutated the
virginal members into flowing waters, in her subterranean channel, had she, to Alpheus, mild shown herself. And Picus,
for such-like resistance and flight, would not have clad himself of fluttering plumes, had he, to Circe, consentaneous
rendered himself. By such-like fugacities, many have experienced what it is, to the grateful loves, to be a flee-er and
refusing. And beyond —"

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Polia's declaration continues: she vows, "with ratified faith and firm loyalty, wholly amorously
to live" with Poliphilo, giving him her "inviolate florid person"; she calls him her "presidium, triumphal Bulla,
Asylum, treasure above all jewels," and — fearing to become one of the punished cruel (Eurydice, Daphne, Hesperie,
Arethusa, Picus, all destroyed for refusing love) — commits wholly. All secure; her vow of love, sealed with exempla.

- **The vow of loving service.** Polia gives Poliphilo everything — "all the soul, all the heart, all my power" — and
  her "inviolate florid person." The refusal of ch. XXVI is completely reversed: she who would not "extinguish her
  torch" now offers herself wholly. The union the temple exists to make is here consummated in her vow. Her endearments
  ("my little soul, my Asylum, my treasure") are the love-language she once refused him, now lavished.
- **the exempla of the punished refusers.** Her fear-catalogue (Eurydice bitten fleeing Aristaeus; Daphne turned to
  laurel fleeing Apollo; Hesperie snakebit fleeing; Arethusa turned to a spring fleeing Alpheus; Picus turned to a
  woodpecker refusing Circe) is the mirror of the nurse's counsel (ch. XXVIII) and the visions (ch. XXVII) — all the
  transformed-for-refusing-love. Polia now reads them as warnings *to herself*, and chooses love to avoid their fate. The
  transformation-theme, her lifelong terror, is resolved by yielding.
- **love as refuge, not conquest.** She calls Poliphilo her "Asylum, most-safe, whither securely I take refuge" — love as
  sanctuary, the opposite of the "cruel enemy" she once resisted (p. 409). The reversal of her whole relation to Love is
  complete: what she fled is now her refuge.
- The kiss-pledge (the *arra*) and the priestess's response complete the chapter (pp. 434–435).
