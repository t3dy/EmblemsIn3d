# Page 10 — Front matter

*(Poliphilo's dedication of the work to Polia. Italian — and here the book's own voice begins:
the Latinate, clause-heaped style that will run for another four hundred and fifty pages.)*

POLIPHILO SENDS MUCH GREETING TO POLIA.

MANY TIMES, POLIA, CONSIDERING THAT the ancient authors fittingly dedicated their works to
princes and magnanimous men — some for a price, others for favour, some for praise — for none of
such causes, then, but for the middle one, I offer this my *Hypnerotomachia*, finding no worthier
prince to whom to dedicate it than to you, my high Empress: whose excellent condition and
incredible beauty, and venerable and greatest virtues, and most illustrious manners, holding
sovereignty over whatever nymph there is in our centuries, have exceedingly inflamed, burned and
consumed me with your signal Love.

Receive then this little gift — you, suffused with the splendour of beauties, and adorned with
every comeliness, and conspicuous of glorious aspect. Which gift you yourself have industriously
made and fashioned, painted with gilded arrows in my amorous heart and marked with your angelic
likeness; so that you possess it singularly as its Patroness.

Which gift I thereafter commit to your diligent and ingenious judgement, leaving the style I
began in and translating it into this one at your instance. Whence, if a fault appear, and if in
some part you find it barren and meagre, falling short of your elegant worth, then you shall be
the one accused — you, best of workwomen, and sole key-bearer of my mind and of my heart.

Therefore I esteem and desire no prize of greater talent or price, especially, than your gracious
love; and, for this, your kind favour. Farewell.

---

## Notes

**Confidence: high** for the sense. The syntax is the book's own — subject and verb held apart
across four or five subordinate clauses — and the English here keeps the suspension rather than
tidying it, because the difficulty is the style Crasso defends on page 2 and not an artefact of
translation.

- **"For none of such causes, then, but for the middle one."** Poliphilo lists the three motives
  of ancient dedications — money, **favour**, praise — and claims only the second. The book is
  offered to get something. What he wants he states at the end, plainly: *your gracious love*.
- **"My high Empress."** Polia is addressed as the prince to whom the work is dedicated, in exact
  parallel with Crasso's dedication to the Duke of Urbino eight pages earlier. The front matter
  therefore has **two dedications, one real and one fictional**, and the fictional one is the
  better letter.
- **The gift she made herself.** The finest turn on the page: the book he is giving her is a gift
  *she* fashioned, having painted it into his heart with gilded arrows and stamped it with her own
  likeness — so she already owns it, and he is returning her property. Every word of it is her
  doing, therefore every fault in it is hers too: *you shall be the one accused*. It is a love
  letter and a disclaimer at once.
- **"Leaving the style I began in and translating it into this one at your instance."** A claim
  that the work was begun in some other manner — Latin is the usual reading — and turned into
  this hybrid vernacular at Polia's asking. Whether that is biography or fiction, it is the book
  accounting for its own strange language, and it puts the responsibility on a woman who is dead
  before the story starts.
- **"Sole key-bearer of my mind and of my heart."** *Unica clavigera* — the key-bearer, she who
  holds the keys. The image returns at the Temple of Venus, where the Antistita unseals the
  mysterial cistern, and at the three gates, where the choosing is done.
- **Compare Marone on page 8**: *Polia, who live though dead — and better so.* Poliphilo
  dedicates the book to a living Empress; the epigrammatist has already told us she is a dead
  woman kept alive by learned speech. The reader is given the disenchantment before the
  enchantment, and it is the same order the last page of Book I uses.
