Demophoon summoned; and the Greek nut, and the almond, and the Thasian.

Not without utmost pleasure I beheld also a little wood of chestnuts, with the
fruit armed in a pricking bristly husk, such as first the Sardians found — whereby,
by the Greek word, they were called Sardian acorns; to which afterwards the divine
Tiberius gave the name Balanus. I sincerely thought that to these the *parthenia*
rightly yielded, and the Tarentine of easiest shelling, and the more easily
balanite, and rounder. These surpass both the pure Salarian and the praised
Corelian, and the quick-boiling, and the Tarentine and Neapolitan; where too was
the broom, or myrica, or Aspalathus.

There were then here also little woods of most noble quinces, or cydonians, and a
carob-grove such as Cyprus does not produce the like; and of slow palms there
offered itself a dense palm-grove of most useful fronds with knife-like point,
resistant and not to be bent at its summit, laden most thickly with its pulpy
fruit — not squalid and small like the Libyan, nor like the sweet dates the inner
Syria produces, but far more excellent in magnitude too, and in sweetness, than
Arabia and Babylon yield. There was also a most lovely one of most noble
pomegranates of all the kinds — sweet, sharp, mixed, acid, and vinous — to which
are not to be compared the Egyptian, nor the Samian, nor the Cretan, nor the
Cyprian, both the seedless and the red-tufted and the white-tufted, most fecund of
fruits and blossoms.

Afterwards I saw a most gratifying little wood of lotus, or nettle-tree, Syrian
bean, *ciceraso*, or *melli*, or *celtis*, with a far sweeter yield than the
Syrtic and Nasamonian — here in all Africa surpassed, it stands. Nor was there
lacking one of Christ's-thorn with reddening fruit, a rival to wine in sweetness.
Let Cyrenaica yield here, and inner Africa, and that too which is born about the
shrine of Hammon; and one of both the mulberries — the one expresses in its fruit
the mournful love, the other affords nourishment to our delights —

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** Another dense fruit-and-nut catalogue, secure in
substance; the nomenclature is Pliny throughout, with Colonna's usual Greek-Latin
doublets. A botanist could refine several of the varietal names.

- **More of the twenty *bosco* compartments**: larch, walnut, chestnut, quince,
  carob, palm, pomegranate, lotus/nettle-tree, Christ's-thorn, mulberry. The
  catalogue keeps its one-genus-per-compartment order, and — as Segre notes
  (GARDENS.md §5) — runs from familiar northern trees toward "mythical" Greek and
  African species, an antiquarian geography of plants.
- **Phyllis grown to a tree** — turned to an almond (or leafless tree that leafed
  at Demophoon's return); Colonna makes her the etymology of *phylla*, leaves.
  **the two mulberries** — the black mulberry reddened by the "mournful love" of
  Pyramus and Thisbe (whose blood dyed the fruit), the white one merely
  nourishing. The trees carry their myths, as everywhere in this grove.
- **the Sardian acorn / Balanus** — the chestnut, and Tiberius's renaming of it,
  from Pliny. **Hammon** — the oracle of Ammon in the Libyan desert. Named to be
  surpassed, as with all the "let X yield here" figures.
