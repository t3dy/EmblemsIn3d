# Page 369 — Chapter XXIV opens

**By the coming of the armed one just recounted, Poliphilo tells how they went
out of the theatre with all the company, and with the other nymphs came to a
sacred fountain; where the nymphs tell of the sepulchre of Adonis, and how the
goddess yearly assembled there to fulfil the holy ceremonies; and, ceasing from
the dance and song, they urged Polia that she should tell her origin and her
falling in love.**

Adorned with new qualities, with my excellent Polia and with our companions,
departing thence from the sacred fountain, we returned out likewise by that same
gate and entrance by which we had come in, where all the nymphs of the sweet
sounds and singing awaited. Who came festively together with us; whereupon I,
stuffed with fruitful love in my kindled and enlarged breast, with every
accessory sweetness, shut out my past sorrows, every troublesome contrary
extinguished, every doubtful thought curbed, no longer now hesitating over Polia,
but as to the sole Augusta of my soul. Sylvia of my heart, and the Ptolemaïs of
my life. Arsacid of my senses, and Myrrha of my love, and of all of me the
mistress and revered Empress — festive and glad, humbly and most devotedly, I
contentedly succumb, with a more intense, sincere and honourable love than pious
Aeneas bore to his dear, beautiful and divine mistress. Having now at last won
her precious love and willing heart, in this amorous contest an Aristaeus.

It came to pass that the merry little throng of nymphs returned to their former
solaces and pleasures, with the celestial harmonies and angelic concord, with
girlish games and solacing jests, and showing themselves briskly full of laughter
at our obtained and consummated desires, gently giving themselves around us in a
sporting round. And through the holy island, by the paths or streets marked out
among the plants of the fruit-bearing orchards, which were green with perennial
and spring —

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The chapter argument and the narrative are secure. The
difficulty is the cascade of learned epithets Poliphilo heaps on Polia — each a
famous lover or beauty, and each identified below; the syntax is a list and
translates plainly, but the allusions are dense.

- **Chapter XXIV** is short (twelve pages) and takes the company from the theatre
  to a second sacred fountain and the tomb of Adonis, ending as the nymphs ask
  Polia to tell her own story — which is Book II. This is the hinge of the whole
  work.
- **Polia's titles** — *Augusta* and *Empress* (imperial); **Sylvia**, **the
  Ptolemaïs**, **Arsacid** (of the Parthian royal house), **Myrrha**: a chain of
  royal and legendary women, some loving, some (Myrrha) tragic, piled up as
  honorifics. Colonna is not being careful about their stories, only their
  grandeur.
- **"than pious Aeneas bore to his… divine mistress"** — Dido, or Venus his
  mother; the epithet *diva* leans toward Venus, keeping the Aeneas/Venus thread
  of the last pages. Left as the text's ambiguity.
- **"in this amorous contest an Aristaeus"** — Aristaeus pursued Eurydice; naming
  himself so, at the moment of winning Polia, is faintly ominous, and it echoes
  the earlier Actaeon (Aristaeus's son) motif. Colonna's mythology is a web, and
  the translation keeps the thread rather than smoothing it.
