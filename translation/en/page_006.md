# Page 6 — Front matter

*(An Italian poem in terza rima, addressed to Leonardo Crasso and then to the reader. It is the
anonymous Latin elegy of pages 3–4 done over again in the vernacular, for readers who have no
Latin — which is itself an argument about who the book was for. It runs over onto page 7.)*

> Leonardo Crasso, my revered doctor,
>    prelate accomplished in the liberal arts
>    and, as I understand it, in every excellent virtue:
> he merits the highest and immortal praise
>    for the expense and the undertaking he has assumed
>    to furnish us with such a compendium.
> Polished reader, then — listen, listen
>    to the dreams of Poliphilo as he tells them,
>    sent down from heaven with much sweetness.
> You will not waste your time extravagantly;
>    rather you will rejoice at having heard
>    the work, exuberant with various things.
> If, grim, you scorn the new erotic
>    invitation, then do not scorn the well-disposed
>    sequence, with its fine and exquisite style.
> If the grave discourse and well-composed learning
>    should not please you, then look at the figures,
>    ancient in geometry — which costs little.
> The many signs, with their measures,
>    kept beside the Nile by the Egyptians;
>    the ancient pyramids, sepulchres,
> with the obelisks set upright on top of them;
>    the therms and baths and statues of colossi,
>    such that whoever looks on them is left undone.
> Here are diverse bases and thick arches,
>    various columns proportioned to them,
>    with capitals and beams that can bear them,
> and the cornices with their quadrants,
>    the symmetries, the zophori, epistyles,
>    which have shown us proud roofs.
> There you will see palaces cultivated and fair
>    of kings and lords, and the nymphal fountains,
>    and the banquets befitting them.
> There you will see diverse chessmen joined
>    in a game of robbers, and human acts
>    conjoined in labyrinth and darkness.
> Here you will read of threefold and not idle
>    deeds, and the majesty of the great Thunderer
>    in the three gates and his sound counsels.
> Who Polia was, fair and triumphant,
>    you will see thereafter; and of Jove the celestial —

*(the poem continues on page 7)*

## Notes

**Confidence: high.** Rendered line for line without rhyme. Terza rima in English costs either
accuracy or dignity, and this edition will not pay either.

- **Why this page exists at all.** It is the anonymous Latin elegy of pages 3–4, translated into
  Italian verse and printed immediately after it. The front matter therefore addresses two
  audiences in succession — the Latinate and the vernacular — and says the same thing to both.
  Set that beside Crasso's claim on page 2 that the book needs "no less Greek and Latin than
  Tuscan": the publisher hedged, and printed a version for people who had only one of them.
- **"Which costs little."** *Che poco costa* — of looking at the pictures instead of reading the
  text. The Latin elegy merely offered the geometry as a fallback; the Italian adds that it is
  cheap. This is the earliest and bluntest statement of the book's own fallback position:
  **if you cannot read it, look at it.**
- **"Whoever looks on them is left undone."** *Che chi le mira ne riman sconfitti* — literally
  "is left defeated". The colossi overcome the person looking. Poliphilo faints, weeps or is
  struck motionless a dozen times in the book; the poem is promising the reader the same
  treatment.
- **The architectural vocabulary is the summary's** (page 5) and the Latin elegy's: *zophori*
  (friezes), *epistyli* (architraves), *coronice cum gli sui quadrati* (cornices with their
  quadrants). Three of the four front-matter pieces name the same mouldings in the same order,
  which suggests one hand behind them, or one reading.
- **"A game of robbers."** *Gioco de latroni*, matching the Latin *chorea latronum* on page 4 —
  the human chess match of chapter XI. See `CHESSBOARD.md`.
