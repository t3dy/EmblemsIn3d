"— (I suspect the dark, funereal, and deadly pre-announcer was the night-wandering, ill-omened, immense and improbe
Ascalaphus (screech-owl)) — the unhappy Hymen; and pompously celebrated (as is bounteously wont) the tight commerce of
matrimony. And, come the desired night, that the yearning woman so long had concupiscently expected — firmly judging (it) to
be the hour of extinguishing the fiery and venereal appetites, not considering what the husband might be: for she, at that
point, blindly excited of libido, of unbridled desire enveloped, only pressed to the fruit of the delectable conjugio; and,
beyond measure of itching appetiting, and to the libido most-abandoned — the frigid and impotent old man adhering, and set
in the debile arms — inflamed beyond the fit by incontinent and biting concupiscence to sub-enter (be roused), Cupid
irritant, sedulous in amplifying the fire (more than the bellows to the little fire exciting) — she consequuted no other (by
her ill misfortune) save that, by the foaming old man, her comely face and purple mouth, from the salivous lips, was spat
upon and slobbered, as if a snail, running over it, had lined it. Nor with blandishments, nor with venereal and petulant
gesticulation, and caresses, nor endearments, could she ever heat, commove, nor irritate the impotent and sterile, queasy
old man to be roused — the breath of whom seemed an aura of a putrid cloaca, and of a fetulent quagmire. His gaping mouth
held the pallid lips, gapped and crisping, and with a discordant voice; and almost toothless — in the upper palate had
remained but two rotten big-teeth, and cavernous, like Pumice stone; and below, four others, two to a side, quaking, and, in
their site, unstable. The beard hard, like the hairs of a long-eared ass, like pricking briars, abundant and whitening. The
vermilion eyes moist and tearful. The nose *silo* (flat), in its hollows bushy, and gaping, and mucous, and snaily, and ever
snoring — so that all that night it seemed he bellowed with a most-windy wine-skin (bellows). The face foul, and the head
whitening of *morphea* (leprosy); and the cheeks warty; and, over the eyes, the brows turgid. The throat, with hispid skin
like a marsh-tortoise's, deformed and goitred. And the trembling hands, without any vigour. The rest of the body putrid,
morbid, and invalid, and, of its tardigrade, decrepit. And, in moving, the garments exhaled a putor of fetid urine.

"For which thing (my daughter, attend, and lend thy memory to this audition), the most-lascivious woman, of her voluptuous
appetitions totally frustrated, could never (all the scortatory efforts, and of illustrious harlotry, discharged) excite the
prostrated members of the enormous and unvigorated senectitude. Now it befalls that, for a long time, she, by the malevolent
and tedious old man — idle, inert, desidious and craven, more jealous than the barbarian decurion — not being able to
receive, nor consequute, other save beatings (converted into infinite Jealousy), and railing, and garrulous cries, and cold
and languid sloth, and fastidious tedium —"

## Notes

**Confidence: medium.** The wedding-night disaster: the lust-maddened girl, married to the **repulsive, impotent old man**
(described in extended grotesque detail — toothless, fetid, leprous, urine-smelling), gets only spittle, impotence, beatings
and jealousy from him; her "voluptuous appetites totally frustrated." All secure; the exemplum's punishment complete.

- **The perfect punishment.** The girl who refused a worthy young lover in her prime is condemned to *unquenchable lust*
  chained to an *impotent, disgusting old man* — desire without any possible satisfaction. It is the exact *contrapasso* for
  refusing love in due season: she gets love's fire and none of its fulfilment, forever. The nurse's warning to Polia is
  total — refuse now, and Cupid will make you burn, too late, for what you cannot have.
- **the grotesque as moral.** The old husband's catalogued repulsiveness (like the executioners, p. 406) is Book II's
  signature: ugliness is the wage of misusing beauty. The girl who scorned a beautiful lover ends in a bed of putrescence.
  Colonna makes the moral physical and unforgettable.
- **"attend, and lend thy memory."** The nurse breaks the frame to drive the lesson home to Polia directly: *this is your
  future*. The exemplum is not a digression but a mirror held to Polia's exact case (beauty, refusal, a worthy suitor
  scorned). It is the argument that, with the visions, breaks her.
- **the woman's fate** — the argument (p. 409) said she "killed herself for intemperate love": the tale's end (her suicide)
  is the ultimate warning, completing on p. 415. Both of Book II's love-deaths (Poliphilo's, from cold refusal; this woman's,
  from forced excess) frame Polia between the two errors the whole book warns against.
